16.1 Tabular foundation models ⭐⭐

“GBDTs beat deep learning on tabular data” was a safe answer for years. As of 2026 it is genuinely contested, and knowing that — with the caveats — is the clearest currency signal in this subject.

The answer to give, near-verbatim

“Historically GBDTs, decisively. As of 2026 that's genuinely contested for small-to-medium data by tabular foundation models like TabPFN — I'd benchmark both on my own data, and I'd still default to a GBDT where I need interpretability, monotonic constraints, cheap retraining and a well-understood ops story.” That sentence shows currency, judgement and appropriate scepticism in one breath. Also be able to name the deep tabular architectures — TabNet, FT-Transformer, SAINT, TabR — and AutoGluon as the strong AutoML baseline worth beating.

Likely interview questions
Modern⭐ GBDT or deep learning for tabular data? Has that changed?

“Historically GBDTs, decisively — faster to train, less tuning, native missing-value and categorical handling. As of 2026 it's genuinely contested for small-to-medium data by tabular foundation models: TabPFN-2.5 reported a 100% win rate against default XGBoost on ≤10k rows / ≤500 features, and TabPFN-3 (May 2026) scales to 1M training rows, reporting that a single forward pass beats 8-hour-tuned GBDT baselines up to 1M rows / 200 features on TabArena and outperforms AutoGluon 1.5 'extreme' while roughly 10× faster. The caveats matter: pretrained exclusively on synthetic data, benchmarks have selection effects, and the deployment, licensing and interpretability stories are less mature. So I'd benchmark both on my own data and still default to a GBDT where I need monotonic constraints, interpretability, cheap retraining and a known ops story.”

Deep diveHow does TabPFN actually work? It doesn't train on your data?

Correct — it does in-context learning. A transformer was pretrained once, offline, on millions of synthetic tabular datasets generated from structural-causal-model priors, learning to approximate Bayesian inference over that prior. At use time your dataset is fed in as context alongside the rows you want predictions for, and the answer comes out of a single forward pass — no fitting loop, no hyperparameter search, no per-dataset optimisation. That's why it can compete with a long tuning run: the "training" already happened. The consequences to name: its inductive bias is whatever the synthetic prior encoded, and its limits (row count, feature count) are architectural rather than statistical.

DesignWould you put TabPFN in production?

Cautiously, and I'd benchmark it first because the reported results are strong enough to be worth the test. Reasons for hesitation: the model is pretrained on synthetic data, so its inductive bias is inherited rather than learned from your domain; the interpretability story is thinner than a GBM with SHAP or monotonic constraints; latency is different in kind (a forward pass carrying context, not a tree traversal); and licensing and operational maturity lag. Where I'd reach for it readily: small-data problems where a GBM is starved and tuning is unstable, and as a strong baseline to check whether my heavily-tuned model is actually earning its complexity.

16.2 Aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty

Two kinds of “I don't know”, and they call for opposite responses.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualAleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty.

Aleatoric is irreducible noise in the process — two identical applicants can have different outcomes. More data will not reduce it; you model it, with quantile regression or a predictive distribution. Epistemic is model ignorance where data is sparse — it does shrink with more data, which is exactly what active learning targets (§10.1), and you estimate it with ensemble variance or bootstrap spread. The practical payoff: epistemic uncertainty tells you where to collect data; aleatoric tells you where to widen the interval and stop trying. Confusing them wastes either data budget or modelling effort.

16.3 Conformal prediction ⭐⭐

The cheapest way to attach a real, distribution-free guarantee to any model — and it fits on a slide.

interactive Split conformal — set α, get coverage
—target coverage
—empirical coverage
—interval width
—conformal quantile q̂

This is the complete algorithm, implemented: fit any model on a training split, compute absolute residuals on a disjoint calibration split, take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of those residuals as q̂, and emit [ŷ − q̂, ŷ + q̂]. Coverage lands on target with no distributional assumption. Then push the drift slider and watch it break: exchangeability is the one assumption it needs.

PropertyWhat it means
Distribution-freeNo assumption about the noise distribution or the model's correctness
Model-agnosticWraps any predictor — a GBM, a neural net, a lookup table
Finite-sampleThe guarantee holds at your actual n, not asymptotically
Marginal, not conditionalCoverage holds on average over the test distribution — not for every subgroup. Easy cases get intervals that are too wide, hard cases too narrow, unless you use a normalised score
Assumes exchangeabilityCalibration and test data must be exchangeable — which breaks under drift and for time series, hence adaptive and weighted variants
Conformalised quantile regression is the practical best of both

Plain split conformal gives a constant-width band, which is wrong wherever the noise varies. Fit a quantile regression at α/2 and 1−α/2 first (adaptive width, §5.9), then conformalise its residuals — you get an interval that is both adaptive and carries the finite-sample guarantee. That combination is the answer when someone asks how you'd produce prediction intervals you can actually commit to.

Likely interview questions
Modern⭐ What is conformal prediction and what guarantee does it give?

A distribution-free, model-agnostic wrapper turning point predictions into sets or intervals with finite-sample coverage. Split conformal: fit on a training split, compute a nonconformity score (e.g. absolute residual) on a disjoint calibration split, take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile as q̂, emit ŷ ± q̂. The guarantee is marginal coverage ≥ 1−α under exchangeability of calibration and test data — which breaks under drift and for time series, hence adaptive and weighted variants. Two caveats worth volunteering: coverage is marginal, not conditional (too wide for easy cases, too narrow for hard ones unless you normalise the score), and the interval is constant-width unless you conformalise a quantile model.

DesignYou need prediction intervals. Quantile regression, bootstrap, or conformal?

Depends on the guarantee you need. Quantile regression is cheap and gives adaptive, heteroscedasticity-aware intervals, but coverage relies on the model being right. Bootstrap captures parameter uncertainty but not irreducible noise, so it typically under-covers for a new observation unless you add the residual distribution back. Conformal gives a finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantee and wraps any model — my default when the interval carries a real commitment. The strongest practical answer is conformalised quantile regression: adaptive width from the quantile model, guaranteed coverage from the conformal wrapper.

GotchaDoes conformal prediction work for time series?

Not out of the box, because it assumes exchangeability — that calibration and test points are drawn from the same distribution in a way that's invariant to ordering. Time series violates that by construction: there's a temporal trend, autocorrelation, and drift, so residuals from last month don't calibrate next month. The fixes are named variants: adaptive conformal inference (ACI), which updates α online based on realised coverage, and weighted conformal, which down-weights older calibration points. Knowing that exchangeability is the assumption — and that it's the only one — is what the question is testing.

16.4 Causal inference ⭐⭐

Most ML answers “what will happen?”. Most business questions are “what should we do?”. Those are different, and a correlation-trained model answers the wrong one.

ConceptWhat it isWhy it bites
Potential outcomesEach unit has Y(1) and Y(0); you observe only oneThe fundamental problem of causal inference — there is no per-row label to be right about (§16.5)
ATE / ATT / CATEAverage effect / effect on the treated / effect conditional on xCATE is what targeting needs; ATE is what an experiment reports
ConfounderCauses both treatment and outcomeMust be controlled for, or the estimate is biased
ColliderCaused by both treatment and outcomeControlling for it creates bias that wasn't there — the opposite of a confounder, and the reason "control for everything" is wrong
MediatorOn the causal path between treatment and outcomeControlling for it removes part of the effect you're trying to measure
Selection biasWho ends up in your data depends on the outcomeThe reject-inference and feedback-loop problems (§15.7)
Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Your model predicts churn accurately. The business asks who to give a discount to. What's wrong?

The churn model answers "who will leave?", but the decision needs "who will stay because we acted?" — different quantities. The highest churn scores are dominated by lost causes who leave regardless; budget also leaks to sure things who'd have stayed; and some customers are sleeping dogs for whom the offer is a reminder to cancel, so the treatment effect is negative. You need uplift / CATE, ideally from a randomised holdout, evaluated with Qini and uplift curves rather than AUC. The general principle: a predictive model is invalid under intervention.

Deep diveWhat's a collider, and why does controlling for it hurt?

A collider is a variable caused by both the treatment and the outcome (or by two otherwise-independent causes). Confounders must be controlled for; colliders must not be — conditioning on one creates a spurious association between its causes that didn't exist in the population. The classic illustration: among hospitalised patients, two unrelated diseases appear negatively correlated, because being admitted (the collider) means you probably have at least one. This is why "control for everything you have" is wrong: adding a covariate can introduce bias as easily as remove it, and only a causal graph tells you which is which.

DesignYou can't run an experiment. What do you do?

Pick a method and state its assumption explicitly, because that's what makes the estimate reviewable. Propensity matching/IPW assumes no unmeasured confounders — strong and usually unverifiable. Difference-in-differences assumes parallel trends, which you can partially check on pre-period data. Regression discontinuity assumes continuity at the cutoff and is very credible where a real threshold exists. Instrumental variables needs relevance and the exclusion restriction. DML lets you use ML for the nuisance functions while keeping valid inference. Then run refutation tests — placebo treatments, random common causes, subset stability — because an estimate nobody tried to break isn't evidence.

16.5 Uplift modelling ⭐⭐

The concrete form of the causal argument — and the widget makes the cost of getting it wrong visible in pounds.

interactive Churn score vs uplift — who actually gets the discount?
—customers actually retained
—discounts to sure-things
—sleeping dogs woken
—net effect per 100 contacts

2,000 simulated customers, each with a true churn probability and a true treatment effect that are only weakly related — the realistic case. Switch rules at a fixed budget and read the retained count. Targeting the highest churn scores spends most of the budget on lost causes and sure things; targeting uplift spends it on persuadables. Same data, same budget, very different outcome.

Likely interview questions
Modern⭐ Explain uplift modelling. Why is AUC the wrong metric for it?

Uplift estimates the conditional average treatment effect E[y|x,treat] − E[y|x,control] — the change caused by the action, not the outcome level. Estimators: T-learner (separate models per arm), S-learner (one model with treatment as a feature), X-learner (better when arms are imbalanced), causal forests. AUC is wrong because there's no per-row label to be right about — you never observe both potential outcomes for the same person. Evaluation must be aggregate: rank by predicted uplift and compare treated vs control outcomes within score deciles, which is what the Qini curve plots.

DesignHow do you get the data to train an uplift model?

Ideally from a randomised experiment: treat a random subset, hold out a random control, and you have unbiased treated and untreated outcomes across the whole feature space. That's why mature retention programmes keep a permanent randomised holdout — it costs a little margin and it's the only thing that makes uplift estimable and measurable over time. Without randomisation you're doing observational causal inference (§16.4) with propensity weighting or DML, and you must state the no-unmeasured-confounders assumption — which is usually violated when past campaigns targeted people because they looked like churners. Recommending the holdout before the modelling is the senior move.

ConceptualWhat are sleeping dogs and why do they matter so much?

Customers whose treatment effect is negative — the retention offer reminds them the subscription exists, or the marketing email prompts an unsubscribe. They matter disproportionately because they're the one group where the campaign is worse than doing nothing, so every contact is a double loss: the cost of the offer plus a customer you'd otherwise have kept. A churn-score-ranked campaign can't distinguish them from persuadables (both may score high on risk), which is the sharpest single argument for uplift over propensity — and it's the kind of effect that only shows up if you kept a randomised control to measure against.

16.6 Double machine learning ⭐

How to use a GBM for the messy parts of a causal estimate without destroying your inference.

You want the effect of a treatment D on an outcome Y, with high-dimensional confounders X. DML fits two nuisance models with any ML method — E[Y|X] and E[D|X] — takes the residuals of each, and regresses one residual on the other. That is the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, made robust.

IngredientWhat it prevents
Neyman orthogonalityThe effect estimate is insensitive to first-order errors in the nuisance models — so a slightly wrong GBM doesn't bias the effect
Cross-fittingA row's nuisance predictions come from a model that never saw it — without this, regularization bias and overfitting contaminate the residuals
ResultA √n-consistent effect estimate with valid confidence intervals, while using flexible ML for the nuisances
Cross-fitting is the same idea as out-of-fold everything else

Notice the pattern: out-of-fold target encoding (§3.5), ordered boosting (§8.6), stacking's OOF meta-features (§8.8), and now cross-fitting in DML — all four are the same insight, that a row's derived value must come from a model that never saw that row. Spotting that as one principle rather than four tricks is exactly the kind of connection that makes an interview go well.

Likely interview questions
Deep diveWhat is double machine learning for?

Estimating a causal effect with high-dimensional confounders while still using flexible ML. Fit two nuisance models with any learner — outcome given confounders, and treatment given confounders — take the residuals of each, and regress one on the other (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell). Two ingredients make it valid: Neyman orthogonality, so small nuisance errors don't bias the effect to first order, and cross-fitting, so a row's nuisance predictions come from a model that never saw it — otherwise regularization bias contaminates the estimate. The payoff is a √n-consistent effect with valid confidence intervals while using gradient boosting for the messy parts. EconML implements it.

ConceptualWhy can't you just put the treatment in a regression with all the confounders?

You can, and with a correctly-specified linear model it's fine. The problem is when you use a flexible model to handle high-dimensional or non-linear confounding: the regularization that makes the ML model work also biases the treatment coefficient (it shrinks it along with everything else), and the model's own overfitting to the confounders contaminates the estimate. Standard inference then gives confidence intervals that are simply wrong. DML fixes both — orthogonalisation removes the first-order sensitivity to nuisance error, cross-fitting removes the overfitting contamination — which is why it exists as a separate procedure rather than "just add the controls".

16.7 Data-centric ML ⭐

One sentence carries this section: most of the remaining accuracy is in the data, not the model.

Likely interview questions
DesignYour model is stuck at 84% and the business wants 90%. What do you do?

Before touching the model: audit 200 random errors. That single hour usually tells you whether the remaining gap is label noise, a missing feature, a broken slice, or genuine difficulty — and those have completely different fixes. Then check the ceiling: what's inter-annotator agreement? If humans agree 88% of the time, 90% is not achievable and the honest answer is to say so. Then slice analysis (§11.6) to see whether the deficit is concentrated. Only after those would I look at features, and only after that at the model — because the ordering of that list is the answer.

GotchaWhen does synthetic data help and when does it hurt?

Helps for genuinely rare classes where you need more coverage of a known pattern, and for privacy-constrained sharing where the alternative is no data at all. Hurts when it fabricates structure — the generator imposes its own assumptions, the model learns them as real, and performance on genuine data doesn't improve or degrades. That's the SMOTE critique (§12.9) generalised. The non-negotiable check: train on synthetic (or augmented) data and evaluate on real held-out data. If that number doesn't improve, the synthetic data isn't helping regardless of how good it looks.

16.8 Shift, robustness & the big question ⭐

The closing section, and the one that gives you a position rather than a fact.

ShiftWhat movesResponse
Covariate shiftP(x), with P(y|x) stableImportance weighting; domain adaptation; often just retrain on recent data
Label / prior shiftP(y)Re-threshold; prior correction on the probabilities (§2.3)
Concept shiftP(y|x)Retrain — nothing else helps
Strategic / adversarialThe subjects adapt to the modelFraudsters and credit-score gamers respond to your rules — strategic classification. Prefer features that are costly to fake, and expect to retrain on a cycle
Shortcut learningNothing — you learned the wrong thingSpurious correlations that hold in training and not in deployment; stress-test with counterfactual slices
⭐ “Is classical ML dead?”

No — and the reason is worth saying carefully. Most of the world's high-value prediction problems are still tabular, latency-bound, regulated and small-data: credit, insurance, fraud, demand, pricing, clinical risk. What changed is that the baseline moved. Strong AutoML and tabular foundation models mean that getting a competent model is no longer where the difficulty is — so the job has shifted toward framing the problem, owning the data, designing the evaluation and running the deployment. That is a better answer than either “no, GBDTs still win” or “yes, LLMs do everything now”, because it is the one that is actually true.

Privacy, at a glance

Differential privacy — a formal guarantee bounded by ε, with a real accuracy cost; k-anonymity and its successors, which are weaker and defeatable by linkage; federated learning, which keeps raw data on-device but does not by itself provide privacy (gradients leak; combine it with DP). Enough to have an opinion, not enough to claim expertise — which is the right calibration for a classical ML interview.

Likely interview questions
Modern⭐ Is classical ML dead?

No. Most of the world's high-value prediction problems are still tabular, latency-bound, regulated and small-data — credit, insurance, fraud, demand, pricing, clinical risk — and none of that is going to an LLM. What changed is that the baseline moved: strong AutoML and tabular foundation models mean getting a competent model is no longer the hard part. So the job has shifted toward framing the problem, owning the data, designing the evaluation, and running the deployment — which is exactly where this whole subject spends its time. That's a more honest answer than either "GBDTs still win" or "LLMs do everything now".

Deep diveWhat is strategic classification, and where have you seen it?

When the subjects of the model adapt to it, the data distribution changes because you deployed. Credit-score gaming (opening accounts to manipulate utilisation), fraudsters probing which transaction sizes clear, SEO against a ranking model, applicants tailoring CVs to a screener. It's distinct from ordinary drift because the shift is adversarial and responsive — fixing the model provokes the next adaptation. Practical responses: prefer features that are costly to fake or causally related to the outcome rather than merely correlated; keep the exact rules non-public; expect a retraining cycle rather than a stable model; and monitor for sudden distribution changes concentrated in gameable features.
