Level 7 is the most examined level in classical ML, for the simple reason that it is what wins. If you can explain, unprompted, that bagging attacks variance and boosting attacks bias, that each new boosting tree is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss, and what leaf-wise growth, GOSS and ordered boosting actually change, you have covered a large fraction of the technical surface of a tabular ML interview. The unsupervised half is asked more narrowly but predictably: k-means' assumptions, PCA's scaling requirement, and the t-SNE trap — cluster distances and sizes in a t-SNE plot mean nothing.

3.1 Why ensembles work

Combining models helps only under one condition, and naming that condition is what separates a real answer from “more models is better”.

The one-liner that gets asked constantly

Bagging attacks variance; boosting attacks bias. Bagging trains high-variance, low-bias learners in parallel on different bootstrap samples and averages, which shrinks variance while leaving bias roughly unchanged. Boosting trains weak, high-bias learners sequentially, each correcting the previous ensemble's residual error, which drives bias down — and can drive variance up, which is why boosting overfits with too many rounds and bagging does not.

3.2 Bagging & Random Forest ⭐

Random Forest is bagging plus one extra idea, and that extra idea is the whole reason it works better.

interactive Watch variance divide as trees are added
—avg single-tree MSE
—ensemble MSE
—avg pairwise corr ρ
—rows out of bag

Real regression stumps-of-depth-3 fitted to real bootstrap samples. Drag the tree count and watch the ensemble MSE fall while the average individual tree MSE stays put — that gap is variance reduction, made visible. Then toggle feature subsampling off to see ρ rise and the benefit shrink: that is exactly the difference between plain bagging and a Random Forest.

HyperparameterEffectGuidance
n_estimatorsMore trees ⇒ lower varianceMore is never worse, only slower — this is not a regularization knob
max_featuresThe decorrelation dial√p classification, p/3 regression; lower ⇒ more decorrelation, more bias
max_depthIndividual tree capacityUsually leave unbounded; the averaging handles the variance
min_samples_leafSmooths leaf estimatesThe knob to reach for on noisy data
Extra TreesRandom split thresholds tooMore variance reduction, slightly more bias, noticeably faster
“Can you overfit a Random Forest?”

The precise answer is two clauses. Adding trees does not overfit — the ensemble converges as M grows, it doesn't degrade. But a forest can absolutely overfit: fully-grown trees on noisy data will happily memorise label noise, and the averaging reduces variance without removing that memorisation. So the fix is min_samples_leaf and max_features, not fewer trees. Contrast with a GBM, which overfits readily with too many rounds — hence early stopping.

3.3 Boosting fundamentals ⭐

“Explain gradient boosting” is a top-five question. The answer that lands is one sentence: each new tree is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions.

interactive Boosting, one tree at a time
—train MSE
—test MSE
—best round (early stop)
—mean |residual|

Genuine gradient boosting on squared loss with depth-2 regression stumps, fitted live. Two experiments worth doing. ν = 1.0: the fit locks on fast and test error starts climbing early — overfitting. ν = 0.05: progress is slow but the test curve is much flatter and bottoms out lower. That is the learning-rate × n_estimators trade-off in one picture, and it is why “lower the learning rate and add trees” is the standard tuning move.

3.4 XGBoost vs LightGBM vs CatBoost ⭐⭐

The most reliably-asked comparison in tabular ML. Answer with mechanisms, then a selection rule, then the honest caveat that once tuned they are usually within noise of each other.

XGBoost 3.4LightGBM 4.7CatBoost 1.2
OptimisationSecond-order (Newton): uses gradients and HessiansGradient-based, histogram binningGradient-based, ordered boosting
Tree growthLevel-wise (leaf-wise available)Leaf-wise (best-first)Symmetric / oblivious — same split across a whole level
Signature tricksExplicit λ, α, γ in the objective; sparsity-aware default direction for NaNGOSS (keep large-gradient rows, subsample small ones) + EFB (bundle mutually exclusive sparse features)Ordered target statistics for categoricals; permutation-based to avoid target leakage
CategoricalsSupported; historically one-hot/encode yourselfNative categorical splitsBest-in-class, zero manual preprocessing
Inference speedFastFastVery fast — oblivious trees vectorise beautifully
Reach for it whenMaximum control, best-understood tuning recipe, widest ecosystemVery large data, tight training budgetMany high-cardinality categoricals, limited tuning time
A tuning order that actually works

(1) Fix a modest learning rate (0.05–0.1) and let early stopping choose n_estimators. (2) Tune tree complexity — max_depth / num_leaves, min_child_weight / min_data_in_leaf. (3) Tune sampling — subsample, colsample_bytree. (4) Tune regularization — lambda, alpha, gamma. (5) Lower the learning rate and refit with more trees. Giving an order rather than a list of parameters is what makes this answer sound like experience. And mention monotonic and interaction constraints — the mechanism for injecting a business rule (“risk must not decrease as debt rises”) into a boosted model, which is close to mandatory in credit and insurance.

3.6 Sample weighting & cost-sensitive learning

The cheapest way to encode business costs into a model — and, in modern scikit-learn, no longer awkward to route through a pipeline.

MechanismWhereEffect
sample_weightfit()Per-row importance in the loss — the general tool (recency weighting, confidence weighting, cost weighting)
class_weight='balanced'Estimator paramInverse-frequency weights; equivalent to oversampling without synthetic rows

A currency check that will date you

If you say “scikit-learn can't pass sample_weight through a Pipeline, so I write a manual loop over folds”, you are describing a library from several versions ago. Metadata routing has been broadly available since 1.4 and was expanded through 1.7 — including routing through Pipeline and through bagging ensembles' predict* methods. Also worth knowing: weighting the positive class up decalibrates the output probabilities, so if you need real probabilities downstream, prefer tuning the threshold.

3.7 Clustering ⭐

k-means is asked about far more than it is used. Know its algorithm, its three assumptions, and which algorithm to reach for when they fail.

interactive Run Lloyd's algorithm step by step
0iterations
—inertia
—silhouette
—points reassigned

Real Lloyd iterations with a real k-means++ seeding routine, and a genuinely computed silhouette score. Try the bad seed initialisation a few times: it converges to a visibly worse local optimum with higher inertia. That is exactly why n_init exists and why k-means++ is the default — the objective is non-convex, so the starting point changes the answer.

How do you choose k?

Technically: the elbow on inertia (subjective and often absent), the silhouette score (b−a)/max(a,b), the gap statistic, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz. Honestly: k is usually a business decision — how many segments can the marketing team actually run campaigns for? Saying both halves is the complete answer. And for evaluation, distinguish internal indices (no labels — silhouette, DB) from external ones (ARI, NMI, homogeneity/completeness) when you happen to have ground truth.

3.8 Dimensionality reduction ⭐

PCA has one requirement everyone forgets and t-SNE has one caveat everyone should state unprompted.

interactive PCA — and what happens if you forget to scale
—PC1 explained var.
—PC2 explained var.
—PC1 angle

The covariance matrix and its eigenvectors are computed exactly (closed form for 2×2). Set the mode to raw and stretch x₁: PC1 swings to align with it and its explained-variance share races toward 100% — not because x₁ is more informative, but because it is measured in smaller units. Switch to standardised and the axes stop moving. That is the whole reason PCA requires scaling.

3.9 Anomaly detection

The hard part is never the algorithm. It is that you usually have no labels, so you cannot evaluate.

MethodIdeaStrength / weakness
z-score / MahalanobisDistance from the centreFast; assumes a (multivariate) Gaussian
Isolation ForestAnomalies need fewer random splits to isolateScales well, few assumptions — the practical default
LOFLocal density relative to neighboursFinds local anomalies; needs a neighbourhood size; poor in high dimensions
One-Class SVMBoundary around the normal regionSensitive to ν and γ; expensive
AutoencoderReconstruction errorGood for high-dimensional structured signals; needs enough clean data
Three distinctions that make this answer senior

(1) Novelty vs outlier detection: novelty detection trains on clean data and flags anything new; outlier detection assumes the training data is already contaminated. Scikit-learn's API distinguishes them, and choosing the wrong one silently changes what “normal” means. (2) The contamination parameter is a prior you are asserting, not something the data tells you — and it directly sets your alert volume. (3) If you have labels, use a classifier. Anomaly detection is what you do when you cannot frame it as highly-imbalanced classification; framing it that way when you can is throwing away supervision.

3.14 Time series with ML ⭐

Framing a forecast as supervised learning is easy. Validating it honestly is where people fall over.

MetricWhat it doesWatch out
MAE / RMSEAbsolute error in target unitsNot comparable across series of different scale
MAPEPercentage errorExplodes near zero and penalises over- and under-prediction asymmetrically
sMAPESymmetric variantBetter, still unstable near zero
MASEMAE ÷ MAE of seasonal-naiveScale-free and comparable across series — the one to reach for with many SKUs; < 1 means you beat the naive baseline
Pinball lossQuantile forecastsThe right metric when you forecast intervals, not points
“ML or ARIMA?” — the honest answer

Many related series with shared structure and exogenous features (10,000 SKUs, promotions, prices, holidays) ⇒ a global gradient boosting model on lag features usually wins, and it wins mostly because it can learn across series. A single short series ⇒ classical methods are hard to beat, and are far easier to explain. Add the constraint that decides many real cases: trees cannot extrapolate (§2.11), so a trending series needs detrending, differencing, or a hybrid with a linear trend component.

3.15 Interview focus for Part 3

Twelve questions on the material that decides most tabular ML interviews.

Part 3 · ensembles, unsupervised and beyond
1. ⭐ Bagging vs boosting — what does each reduce, and why?

Bagging trains high-variance low-bias learners in parallel on bootstrap samples and averages them. Averaging M decorrelated estimators cuts variance toward ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/M, leaving bias roughly unchanged — so it reduces variance. Boosting trains weak high-bias learners sequentially, each fitted to the current ensemble's errors, so the ensemble's systematic error shrinks — it reduces bias, and can increase variance, which is why it overfits with too many rounds and needs early stopping.

2. How does Random Forest differ from plain bagging? What is OOB error?

Random Forest adds random feature subsetting at each split (max_features). Without it, a dominant feature is chosen at the root of every tree and the trees end up highly correlated — and correlation is the ceiling on how much averaging can help. OOB error: each bootstrap omits about (1−1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 36.8% of rows, so each row can be predicted by the trees that never saw it, giving a validation estimate for free without a holdout.

3. ⭐ Explain gradient boosting. What exactly is each new tree fitted to?

Start from a constant prediction. At each round, compute the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions — the pseudo-residuals — fit a shallow tree to those, and add it scaled by the learning rate: Fₘ = Fₘ₋₁ + ν·hₘ. For squared loss the negative gradient is the ordinary residual, which is why the textbook version says “fit to the residuals”; saying negative gradient is what generalises it to log loss, Poisson, quantile and everything else.

4. Can you overfit a Random Forest by adding trees? Can you overfit a GBM?

RF: adding trees does not overfit — the ensemble converges rather than degrading. But a forest of deep unpruned trees on noisy data absolutely can overfit by memorising label noise; control it with min_samples_leaf and max_features, not with fewer trees. GBM: yes, readily — each round fits the remaining error, including noise. Use a validation set with early stopping, plus shrinkage and subsampling.

5. XGBoost vs LightGBM vs CatBoost — the real differences, and how do you choose?

XGBoost: second-order (Newton) approximation using gradients and Hessians, explicit λ/α/γ regularization, sparsity-aware learned default direction for missing values, level-wise growth by default. LightGBM: leaf-wise growth, plus GOSS (keep large-gradient rows, subsample the rest) and EFB (bundle mutually exclusive sparse features) — fastest on large data. CatBoost: ordered boosting and ordered target statistics to eliminate the target leakage in naive encoding, symmetric oblivious trees for very fast inference, strong defaults. Choice: many high-cardinality categoricals + little tuning time ⇒ CatBoost; very large data + tight budget ⇒ LightGBM; maximum control and ecosystem ⇒ XGBoost. Then say that once tuned they are usually within noise of each other.

6. Leaf-wise vs level-wise growth — what's the risk of leaf-wise?

Level-wise expands every node at a depth before going deeper, giving balanced trees and predictable cost. Leaf-wise always expands the leaf with the largest loss reduction, so it reaches a lower loss with fewer leaves — faster and often more accurate. The risk is that it grows very deep down one branch and overfits small or noisy datasets. Control it with num_leaves and min_data_in_leaf — and note that setting max_depth alone while leaving num_leaves at its default is a common misconfiguration.

7. What problem does CatBoost's ordered boosting solve?

The prediction shift caused by target leakage in naive gradient boosting with target statistics: if a row's encoding and residual are computed using its own label, training residuals are systematically biased and the model is over-optimistic on data it has seen. Ordered boosting computes each row's statistics and residuals from only the rows preceding it in a random permutation (several permutations, to avoid starving early rows) — the algorithmic version of out-of-fold target encoding.

8. How does stacking work, and what's the leakage trap in it?

Base models produce predictions that become features for a meta-learner. The trap: those meta-features must be out-of-fold — each row's base prediction must come from a model that never saw that row. Train the base models on all the data and predict the same data and you have handed the meta-learner in-sample fit, so it learns to trust whichever base model overfits hardest. Keep the meta-learner simple (regularized linear), value diversity over individual strength, and be honest that stacking rarely survives a production review on latency and maintenance grounds.

9. ⭐ How does k-means work, how do you choose k, and what are its assumptions?

Lloyd's algorithm: initialise centroids (k-means++, which seeds them far apart), assign each point to its nearest centroid, recompute centroids as cluster means, repeat until assignments stop changing. It minimises inertia (within-cluster sum of squares), which is non-convex, so the result depends on the seed — hence n_init. Assumptions: spherical clusters, roughly equal variance, and a meaningful Euclidean distance, so it is scale-sensitive and outlier-sensitive. Choosing k: elbow, silhouette, gap statistic — and honestly, k is usually a business constraint.

10. ⭐ Explain PCA. Why must you scale first? How many components?

PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance — the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, equivalently the right singular vectors of the centred data matrix. Scaling: variance depends on units, so an unscaled feature measured in small units dominates the first component purely by measurement convention. Number of components: cumulative explained-variance ratio (a threshold like 95%), the scree-plot elbow, or — best — cross-validated downstream performance. Caveat to add: components are linear mixtures of all features, so interpretability is limited.

11. PCA vs t-SNE vs UMAP — and what's the trap with t-SNE plots?

PCA is linear, deterministic, reversible and safe as a preprocessing step. t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear, stochastic visualisation tools. The trap: in a t-SNE plot, distances between clusters and the sizes of clusters are not meaningful — the method preserves local neighbourhoods only, and the layout is heavily influenced by perplexity. UMAP preserves more global structure and is faster, but is still stochastic and still primarily for visualisation. Never conclude “these two clusters are similar because they're close on the plot”.

12. ML or ARIMA for forecasting? And how do you validate it?

Many related series with shared structure and exogenous drivers ⇒ a global gradient boosting model over lag, rolling, calendar and Fourier features usually wins. One short series ⇒ classical methods are hard to beat and easier to explain. Validate with walk-forward / rolling-origin splits — never a random split — with a gap (purge and embargo) when features arrive with latency. Report MASE when comparing across series, avoid MAPE near zeros, and always show the seasonal-naive baseline. Finally: trees can't extrapolate, so detrend or use a hybrid for trending series.
