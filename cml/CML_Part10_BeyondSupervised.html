When the target isn't a plain label Active learning, weak supervision and pseudo-labelling; multi-label and ordinal targets and why treating them as multi-class throws information away; survival analysis and censoring; learning to rank and nDCG; framing forecasting as supervised learning; and walk-forward validation with purging and embargo.

Recognising that a target is not plain binary classification is very often the whole insight a case question is testing. Four trigger phrases are worth memorising. “When will this happen?” rather than “will it happen?” ⇒ survival analysis with censoring. “Rank these for a user” ⇒ learning to rank and nDCG, not classification with a sort. “Poor / fair / good” ⇒ ordinal, not multi-class. “Forecast the next N periods” ⇒ lag features, a global model, and above all walk-forward validation with a gap — the place where more forecasting projects go wrong than anywhere else.

10.1 Learning with few labels ⭐

“You have 500 labels and need 10,000” is a standard senior question. The good answer is a ladder, not a technique — and it starts by questioning the premise.

Question the premise first

Before proposing techniques, ask: does the model actually need 10,000 labels? A strong regularized baseline on 500 labels with proper cross-validation might already clear the bar. And ask what the label quality is — 500 carefully-adjudicated labels beat 10,000 noisy ones, because label noise caps achievable performance (§4.6) and inter-annotator agreement is your accuracy ceiling (§16.7). Candidates who go straight to semi-supervised methods have skipped the two cheapest options.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ What would you do with 500 labels and a need for 10,000?

First question the premise — a strong regularized baseline on 500 labels with honest CV might clear the bar, and 500 well-adjudicated labels beat 10,000 noisy ones. Then a ladder: active learning to spend the next labels where uncertainty is highest, with a diversity/core-set term so you don't label 500 near-duplicates; weak supervision (labelling functions plus a label model) if domain rules exist; pseudo-labelling with a clean held-out set to catch confirmation bias; transfer from a related task; a cheaper proxy label; and — often correct — buy the labels, with clear guidelines and an inter-annotator agreement check, because that agreement rate is your ceiling. Also worth naming: TabPFN-style models are strongest exactly in this small-data regime (§16.1).

GotchaWhen does pseudo-labelling make things worse?

When the model's confident predictions are systematically wrong — it then trains on its own mistakes and reinforces them, which is confirmation bias. It's most dangerous when the unlabelled pool has a different distribution from the labelled one (so the model is confidently extrapolating), or when classes are imbalanced (the majority class gets pseudo-labelled disproportionately, worsening the imbalance). Guards: keep a clean human-labelled validation set that never receives pseudo-labels, add them gradually with a high confidence threshold, and check per-class rates rather than overall accuracy.

DesignWhy does uncertainty sampling alone fail?

Because the most uncertain points are often near-duplicates of each other — one ambiguous region generates hundreds of similarly-uncertain examples, and labelling 500 of them teaches the model almost nothing beyond what the first 20 did. It also over-selects outliers and mislabelled points, which are uncertain for the wrong reason. The fix is to combine uncertainty with a diversity or core-set criterion so the selected batch covers the input space, and to select in batches with an explicit redundancy penalty rather than greedily one at a time.

10.2 Multi-label and ordinal targets

Two target shapes that are routinely mis-framed as multi-class, throwing away information in both cases.

Target shapeWrong framingRight framingMetric
Multi-label — several labels per rowMulti-class softmax (forces exactly one)Per-label sigmoid (binary relevance); classifier chains if labels are dependentPer-label F1, macro/micro-F1, Hamming loss, subset accuracy
Ordinal — ordered categoriesMulti-class (throws away the order)Ordinal models (cumulative logit), or a chain of binary "≥ k" classifiers; or regression + thresholdsQuadratic weighted kappa, MAE on the ordinal scale
Multi-output regressionIndependent models per outputShared representation, or a model with native multi-output supportPer-output plus an aggregate
Hierarchical labelsFlat multi-classLevel-by-level classifiers, or a hierarchical lossHierarchical precision/recall
Why treating ordinal as multi-class is a real loss

With categories poor / fair / good / excellent, multi-class treats predicting “poor” when the truth is “excellent” as exactly as wrong as predicting “good” — it has no notion that some errors are worse than others. That both wastes information during training and makes your metric meaningless. Ordinal models exploit the ordering; at minimum, use quadratic weighted kappa or MAE on the ordinal scale so the metric knows that near-misses are better than far ones.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualMulti-label vs multi-class — how do they differ in practice?

Multi-class assigns exactly one label from k options, so a softmax is appropriate and the probabilities sum to 1. Multi-label allows any subset, so you use per-label sigmoids (binary relevance) and the probabilities don't sum to anything. Consequences: metrics differ (per-label F1, Hamming loss, subset accuracy rather than plain accuracy), thresholds are per-label, and if labels are dependent — "cat" makes "animal" certain — binary relevance ignores that and classifier chains or a hierarchical structure captures it. Using softmax on a multi-label problem silently forces mutual exclusivity that doesn't exist.

DesignYour target is a 1–5 satisfaction rating. Classification or regression?

Neither, strictly — it's ordinal. Multi-class classification throws away the ordering (predicting 1 when the truth is 5 counts the same as predicting 4). Plain regression assumes the gaps are equal and the scale is interval, which is rarely true for satisfaction ratings — the step from 4 to 5 isn't the same as 2 to 3. Best options: an ordinal model (cumulative logit), or a chain of binary "is it ≥ k?" classifiers whose outputs you combine. If I do use regression, I'd threshold the output and report quadratic weighted kappa, which penalises distant errors more — that's the standard metric for exactly this situation.

10.3 Survival analysis ⭐

The trigger phrase is “when will this happen?” rather than “will it happen?” — and the giveaway in the data is that some rows simply haven't had the event yet.

Why labelling censored rows as 0 biases everything

A customer who joined last month and hasn't churned is not the same as one who has been around three years and hasn't churned — but binary labelling calls both “0”. The model then learns that recent tenure predicts staying, which is an artefact of your snapshot date rather than a property of customers. It also makes the label depend on when you extracted the data, so refreshing the dataset changes labels for the same rows. That is the concrete reason survival framing exists.

Likely interview questions
DesignWhat is censoring and why does it matter?

Right-censoring means you know only that the event hadn't occurred by the end of observation — the subject may fail tomorrow or never. Treating those rows as negatives is wrong twice: it conflates "survived three years" with "joined last month", so the model learns that short tenure predicts staying, which is an artefact of your snapshot; and it makes the labels depend on when you pulled the data, so refreshing changes labels retroactively. Survival methods handle it correctly — Kaplan-Meier for the marginal curve, Cox proportional hazards for covariate effects, or XGBoost's survival objectives if you want a GBM.

ConceptualWhat does the Cox model assume, and how would you check it?

Proportional hazards: the ratio of hazards between any two subjects is constant over time, so covariates shift the hazard multiplicatively but don't change its shape. It's semi-parametric — the baseline hazard is left unspecified, which is why it's so widely applicable. Check it with Schoenfeld residuals (they should show no trend against time), or by plotting log(−log(S(t))) curves per group and confirming they're roughly parallel. If it fails, options are: stratify on the offending variable, add a time-varying coefficient, or switch to an AFT model or a survival forest, neither of which needs proportional hazards.

10.4 Ranking & recommendation ⭐

Ranking is not classification with a sort. The loss knows about order, and the metrics know about position.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualPointwise, pairwise, listwise — what's the difference?

Pointwise predicts a relevance score per item independently, so it's just regression or classification and ignores that ranking is relative. Pairwise learns which of two items should rank higher (RankNet), which captures relativity but treats all pairs equally regardless of position. Listwise optimises a whole-list objective directly — LambdaMART is the classical workhorse, weighting each pair's gradient by how much swapping them would change nDCG, so it focuses effort on the top of the list. In practice LambdaMART on a GBM is still extremely strong for tabular ranking.

ConceptualWhy nDCG rather than precision@k?

Because precision@k is blind to order within the top k — putting the relevant items at ranks 1–3 or at ranks 8–10 gives the identical score, yet the user experience is completely different. nDCG discounts each item's gain by 1/log₂(rank+1) and normalises by the ideal ordering, so rank 1 is worth roughly 3× rank 8, and it also supports graded relevance rather than just binary. Use MRR instead when there's essentially one right answer and all you care about is where it lands.

DesignWhy is recommendation split into candidate generation and ranking?

Latency and cost. You cannot score ten million items per request with an expensive model, so a cheap, high-recall retrieval step narrows the catalogue to ~1,000 candidates (embedding ANN search, co-occurrence, popularity, business rules), and only then does an expensive high-precision ranker score those. A third re-ranking stage applies diversity, freshness and business constraints on the final ~10. The design consequence worth stating: the ranker can never recover an item the retriever missed, so retrieval recall is the ceiling on the whole system — and it's the stage people under-invest in.

10.5 Time series as supervised learning ⭐

Framing a forecast as a supervised problem is straightforward. The two things that trip people up are the feature set and the fact that trees cannot extrapolate.

Feature familyExamplesNote
Lagsy[t−1], y[t−7], y[t−28]The backbone. Choose lags from the ACF/PACF and domain seasonality
Rolling statisticsMean/std/min/max over 7, 28, 91 daysMust be computed with a shift — a window ending at t includes y[t] and leaks
CalendarDay-of-week, month, holiday, days-to-holiday, paydayCyclically encoded where appropriate (§13.3)
Fourier termssin/cos(2πkt/P) for k = 1…KSmooth seasonality with far fewer parameters than dummies; handles multiple periods
ExogenousPrice, promotion, weather, competitor activityThe main reason ML beats classical methods — but they must be known in advance for the forecast horizon
Series identitySKU, store, category embeddings or target-encoded IDsWhat makes a global model work across many series
Trees cannot extrapolate — the single biggest gotcha in ML forecasting

A GBM's prediction is a leaf constant, so it is bounded by the training target range (§7.3). On a series with a genuine trend, a model trained on 2024 data will predict 2024 levels forever, and the error grows without bound as you forecast further out. Three fixes: detrend or difference the target and model the residual; use a hybrid (linear trend + GBM on residuals); or include time as a feature only if you're confident the trend continues — which is usually a bad bet. This is the most common reason a "simple" classical model beats a GBM on a forecasting task.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ ML or ARIMA for forecasting?

Many related series with shared structure and exogenous drivers (10,000 SKUs, promotions, prices, holidays) ⇒ a global gradient boosting model on lag, rolling, calendar and Fourier features usually wins, mostly because it can learn across series and use covariates that classical univariate methods can't. A single short series ⇒ classical methods (ETS, ARIMA, seasonal-naive) are hard to beat and far easier to explain. And the constraint that decides many real cases: trees can't extrapolate, so a trending series needs detrending, differencing, or a hybrid with a linear component.

DebuggingYour rolling-mean feature gives 0.99 R² in backtest and fails live. Why?

Almost certainly the rolling window includes the current value. A 7-day mean computed as rolling(7).mean() at time t contains y[t] itself, so the feature is a smoothed copy of the target — spectacular in backtest, unavailable at forecast time. The fix is a shift: rolling(7).mean().shift(1), and more generally shift by the full forecast horizon, since at t you're predicting t+h and may not even have y[t] yet given reporting lag. This is temporal leakage (§3.7) in its most common concrete form.

ConceptualDirect vs recursive multi-step forecasting?

Direct: train a separate model for each horizon h, each predicting y[t+h] from features at t. No error accumulation, but h models to train and maintain, and each sees less structure. Recursive: one model predicting one step ahead, fed its own prediction as the next lag. One model, any horizon — but errors compound step by step, and the model is being applied to inputs (its own predictions) that don't look like its training inputs. Direct is the safer default for short fixed horizons; recursive is fine for a few steps. There's also a hybrid ("DirRec") that retrains at each step.

10.6 Forecast validation & metrics ⭐⭐

More forecasting projects go wrong here than anywhere else — a random split on time-series data produces a number that means nothing.

MetricWhat it doesWatch out
MAE / RMSEAbsolute error in target unitsNot comparable across series of different scale
MAPEPercentage errorUndefined/explodes near zero; asymmetric — over-prediction caps at 100%, under-prediction doesn't, so optimising it biases forecasts low
sMAPESymmetric variantBetter, still unstable near zero
MASEMAE ÷ MAE of seasonal-naiveScale-free and comparable across series — the one to use with many SKUs; < 1 means you beat the naive baseline
Pinball lossQuantile forecastsThe right metric when you forecast intervals rather than points; report coverage too
WAPE / weighted MAEError weighted by volumeOften the closest to the business quantity in retail
Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ How do you cross-validate time series data? What are purging and embargo?

Walk-forward / rolling-origin: train on everything up to time t, test on the window after it, then roll the origin forward — with an expanding or sliding window. Never a random split. Purging removes training samples whose label horizon overlaps the test window: if a label is observed 30 days after its features, a training row from just before the boundary has a label that partly occurs during the test period. Embargo additionally drops a buffer immediately after the test window before training resumes, so serial correlation and feature reporting latency can't leak backwards. Without both, you get an optimistic score and a production surprise.

Very commonWhen does MAPE break? What do you use instead?

Two failures. It's undefined and explodes as the actual approaches zero — fatal for intermittent demand, where many periods are genuinely zero. And it's asymmetric: over-prediction error is bounded at 100% while under-prediction is unbounded, so optimising MAPE systematically biases forecasts low. Use sMAPE (better, still unstable near zero) or — the right answer for many series at different scales — MASE, which divides by the seasonal-naive error, is scale-free and comparable across series, and has the useful property that < 1 means you beat the naive baseline. For volume-weighted business impact, WAPE is often closest to what people actually care about.

DesignYou're forecasting 10,000 SKUs. One model or 10,000?

One global model, almost certainly. It learns patterns shared across series (weekly seasonality, promotion response, holiday effects) so short-history and sparse SKUs borrow strength from the rest, and it's one artefact to train, monitor and deploy rather than 10,000. Give it series identity features (SKU, category, store) so it can specialise. Per-series models make sense only for a small number of high-value, long-history, idiosyncratic series — and a common good answer is a hybrid: global model for the long tail, bespoke treatment for the top 50 SKUs by revenue. Evaluate with MASE so results are comparable across the whole catalogue.
