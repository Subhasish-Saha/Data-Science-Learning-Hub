Part 6 of 7 · Production & Frontier

Shipping it, and the questions that are still open Training/serving architecture, latency budgets, drift detection when labels arrive late, retraining triggers and A/B testing — then the frontier: tabular foundation models, conformal prediction, causal inference and data-centric ML.

The production half is where senior interviews spend most of their time, and the highest-value idea in it is negative: not every drift alert warrants a retrain. Confirm the distribution change actually degraded the metric first — alert fatigue kills monitoring programmes faster than missing drift does. The frontier half exists to give you defensible positions on the four questions that separate a 2026 candidate from a 2019 one: whether GBDTs still beat deep learning on tabular data (genuinely contested now — TabPFN-3), how you attach a guarantee to a prediction (conformal), why a churn model is the wrong model for deciding who gets a discount (uplift/CATE), and why most of the remaining accuracy is in the data rather than the model.

6.1 The architecture

One rule dominates this whole level: the same feature code must serve training and inference. Almost every production ML incident traces back to a violation of it.

6.2 Serving

Latency budgets are decomposed, not asserted — and you report p95/p99, not p50.

6.3 Monitoring & drift ⭐

The highest-value idea in production ML is a negative one: not every drift alert warrants a retrain.

interactive PSI, KS and the question they can't answer
—PSI
—KS statistic
—PSI band
—action

PSI is computed exactly as Σ (aᵢ − eᵢ)·ln(aᵢ/eᵢ) over 10 quantile bins of the reference distribution, and KS as the maximum gap between the empirical CDFs. The third slider is the one that isn't in the formula: a large PSI on an unimportant feature is not an incident, and a small PSI on the feature carrying half your model's signal might be. Drift detection tells you something changed; only a metric tells you something broke.

6.4 Retraining & deployment

Shadow, canary, A/B, rollout — plus the feedback-loop problem, which is the part that makes fraud and credit systems genuinely hard.

6.5 A/B testing an ML model

The statistics are standard. What makes it an ML question is everything that can invalidate the test.

Design decisionGet it rightWhat goes wrong
Randomisation unitRandomise the unit the effect acts on — usually the user, not the requestPer-request randomisation lets one user see both variants, diluting the effect and breaking independence
Sample sizeCompute it up front from the MDE, baseline rate and powerUnderpowered tests read as “no effect” when the effect exists
Primary vs guardrailOne primary metric; guardrails on latency, revenue, complaints, fairnessPicking the winning metric after the fact is p-hacking
PeekingFix the duration, or use a proper sequential testChecking daily and stopping at significance inflates the false-positive rate badly
Novelty / primacyRun at least one full business cycleEarly lift that decays; or early loss from users adapting
Network effectsCluster/geo randomisationTreatment leaks to control through the marketplace, biasing everything toward zero
Variance reductionCUPED using pre-period dataNot using it just means a longer, more expensive test
The offline-to-online gap, stated before someone asks

Your offline metric is a proxy. A model with better AUC can lose an A/B test because it improved ranking in a region users never see, because it shifted the score distribution and the fixed threshold now behaves differently, or because the business metric depends on something the model doesn't optimise (diversity, latency, fairness of exposure). Say explicitly: “I'd confirm that the offline metric actually moves the online objective — ideally by checking historical launches — before trusting it as a launch criterion.”

6.6 Engineering hygiene

The unglamorous section that separates a system from a notebook. Interviewers ask it as “how do you make an ML pipeline reproducible?”

PracticeConcretely
Data-schema testsTypes, ranges, allowed categories, null rates, row-count bounds — run on every batch, fail loudly
Pipeline invariantsDoes fit then transform equal fit_transform? Are outputs deterministic given a seed? Does the pipeline reject unseen categories gracefully?
Golden-prediction testsA fixed set of inputs with expected outputs, checked in CI — the single cheapest way to catch a silent regression
No-network unit testsTests must not depend on a live warehouse; fixture the data
ReproducibilityPinned dependencies (a lockfile), seeded randomness, data snapshots or versioned datasets (DVC / lakeFS style), captured environment, and lineage from a prediction back to a model, a dataset and a commit
Config over codeHyperparameters, feature lists and thresholds in versioned config, not scattered literals
Experiment trackingEvery run's params, metrics, artefacts and data version recorded automatically
DocumentationModel card, stated assumptions, known limitations, a retraining runbook and an on-call playbook — “the model is returning nulls, now what?”
Reproducibility is more than random_state

“Set the seed” is the 2018 answer. The 2026 answer is: pinned dependencies, a versioned data snapshot, captured environment, config-as-code, recorded lineage, and golden-prediction tests — because the thing that actually breaks reproducibility is a library minor-version bump changing a default, or the upstream table having been backfilled since you trained. Seeds only control the randomness you own.

6.7 Tabular foundation models — the question that changed ⭐⭐

“GBDTs beat deep learning on tabular data” was a safe answer for years. As of 2026 it is genuinely contested, and knowing that is a currency signal.

6.8 Uncertainty & conformal prediction ⭐

A point prediction is not enough when a human has to decide whether to trust it. Conformal prediction is the cheapest way to attach a real guarantee.

interactive Split conformal — set α, get coverage
—target coverage
—empirical coverage
—interval width
—conformal quantile q̂

This is the complete algorithm, implemented: fit any model on a training split, compute absolute residuals on a disjoint calibration split, take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of those residuals as q̂, and emit [ŷ − q̂, ŷ + q̂]. Empirical coverage lands on the target — with no distributional assumption about the model or the data. Then push the drift slider and watch coverage break: exchangeability is the one assumption it does need.

6.9 Causal inference ⭐

Most ML answers “what will happen?”. Most business questions are “what should we do?”. Those are different questions, and a correlation-trained model answers the wrong one.

interactive Churn score vs uplift — who actually gets the discount?
—customers actually retained
—discounts to sure-things
—sleeping dogs woken
—net effect per 100 contacts

2,000 simulated customers, each with a true churn probability and a true treatment effect that are only weakly related — which is the realistic case. Switch between the two rules at a fixed budget and read the retained count. Targeting the highest churn scores spends most of the budget on lost causes (they leave anyway) and sure things; targeting uplift spends it on persuadables. Same model family, same budget, very different outcome.

6.11 Distribution shift, robustness & the big question

The closing section, and the one that gives you a position rather than a fact.

ShiftWhat movesResponse
Covariate shiftP(x), with P(y|x) stableImportance weighting; domain adaptation; often just retrain on recent data
Label / prior shiftP(y)Re-threshold; prior correction on the probabilities
Concept shiftP(y|x)Retrain — nothing else helps
Strategic / adversarialThe subjects adapt to the modelFraudsters and credit-score gamers respond to your rules — strategic classification. Prefer features that are costly to fake, and expect to retrain on a cycle
Shortcut learningNothing — you learned the wrong thingSpurious correlations that hold in training and not in deployment; stress-test with counterfactual slices
⭐ “Is classical ML dead?”

No — and the reason is worth saying carefully. Most of the world's high-value prediction problems are still tabular, latency-bound, regulated and small-data: credit, insurance, fraud, demand, pricing, clinical risk. What changed is that the baseline moved. Strong AutoML and tabular foundation models mean that getting a competent model is no longer where the difficulty is — so the job has shifted toward framing the problem, owning the data, designing the evaluation and running the deployment. That is a better answer than either “no, GBDTs still win” or “yes, LLMs do everything now”, because it is the one that is actually true.

Privacy, at a glance

Differential privacy — a formal guarantee bounded by ε, with a real accuracy cost; k-anonymity and its successors, which are weaker and easier to defeat by linkage; federated learning, which keeps raw data on-device but does not by itself provide privacy (gradients leak; combine with DP). Enough to have an opinion, not enough to claim expertise — which is the right calibration for a classical ML interview.

6.12 Interview focus for Part 6

Twelve questions covering production and the frontier — where senior interviews spend most of their time.

Part 6 · production, uncertainty, causality
1. Walk me through deploying a model end to end.

Frame the decision and the metric → build the offline pipeline with point-in-time-correct features → validate with a split that mimics production → pick a threshold from costs or capacity → package the whole pipeline (not just the estimator) and register it with a version and lineage → deploy in shadow to check latency and stability → canary at a few percent with guardrails → A/B test against the incumbent → full rollout with a rollback path → monitor data quality, drift, prediction distribution and (when labels arrive) performance → define retraining triggers and write the runbook. The two things that separate a good answer: the same feature code serves training and inference, and there is a rollback path and an on-call playbook.

2. Batch vs real-time serving — how do you decide?

One question: do the features exist before the request arrives? If yes, precompute on a schedule and write to a key-value store — prediction becomes an O(1) lookup, which is cheaper, more stable and easier to monitor. If a feature only exists at request time (this transaction's amount, this session's behaviour), you need real-time. Secondary considerations: how stale can a prediction be, how expensive is the model, and what is the p99 budget. Most real systems are hybrid — precomputed aggregates joined with a few live features.

3. ⭐ How do you detect drift when labels arrive 60 days late?

Use proxies, layered. Input side: per-feature PSI (<0.1 stable, 0.1–0.2 moderate, >0.2 significant), KS or chi-square, Jensen-Shannon and Wasserstein as smoother alternatives to KL on noisy production data — weighted by feature importance. Output side: the prediction distribution and mean predicted probability, which move immediately. Behavioural: override rate, abstention rate, analyst agreement, downstream conversion. Partial labels: any subset that resolves quickly. Data quality: schema, nulls, ranges, cardinality — which in practice catches more incidents than drift statistics do. And the discipline: confirm the change actually degrades a metric before retraining — alert fatigue kills monitoring programmes.

4. Data drift vs concept drift — how do the responses differ?

Data/covariate drift is P(x) moving while P(y|x) holds — a new customer segment, a new channel. The model may still be valid; you might reweight, retrain on recent data, or do nothing. Concept drift is P(y|x) itself changing — fraudsters adapt, a recession redefines risk. Nothing but retraining helps, and you may need to reconsider features and even the target definition. The practical difference: covariate drift is detectable without labels; concept drift generally is not, which is why the proxy stack above exists.

5. When do you retrain? What triggers it?

Three triggers, in preference order: performance-triggered (best, when labels arrive in time), drift-triggered (the proxy, with a confirmation step so you don't retrain on noise), and scheduled (the boring default that mostly works). The trade-off is staleness vs churn: retraining constantly makes predictions change under users for no visible reason and makes incidents unreproducible. Always run champion/challenger and gate the retrain behind a golden-set regression suite so it cannot silently regress a known case.

6. What is training/serving skew and how do you prevent it?

The training pipeline and the serving path compute the same feature differently — a different window, a different null handling, a different timezone, a different rounding — so the model sees a distribution it was never trained on, and no alert fires because both systems are individually working. Prevent it architecturally: one shared code path (a feature store, a shared library, or a batch job that writes what the API reads), plus a skew check that logs serving features and compares their distribution to training. Related and worse: point-in-time incorrectness, where training features use values that did not exist at prediction time.

7. Your model was great offline and users are complaining. Debug it.

Work down a fixed list, cheapest first. (1) Leakage — was the offline number ever real? Re-validate with a time split. (2) Split mismatch — did validation mimic production (groups, time, purge)? (3) Slices — is the aggregate fine but a segment broken (§4.4)? (4) Skew — compare the live feature distribution against training. (5) Drift — has the input or the concept moved since training? (6) Threshold — is 0.5 still deployed while the base rate has changed? (7) Label quality — were the offline labels defined the same way as the outcome users care about? (8) The metric itself — does the offline metric actually correlate with the user experience? Naming this as an ordered checklist is worth more than any single answer.

8. What is the feedback-loop problem in a fraud or credit model?

The model's decisions determine the data it will be trained on next. A fraud model never observes whether the transactions it blocked were really fraud; a credit model never sees how rejected applicants would have performed (the reject inference problem). Training data becomes systematically censored, the model becomes confident about a shrinking slice of the world, and performance degrades in a way that offline evaluation cannot see. Mitigations: hold out a small exploration/control slice that bypasses the model, use propensity weighting on the observed data, and treat the blocked population explicitly rather than dropping it.

9. ⭐ GBDT or deep learning for tabular data? Has that changed?

“Historically GBDTs, decisively — faster to train, less tuning, native missing-value and categorical handling, better under about a million rows. As of 2026 that is genuinely contested for small-to-medium data by tabular foundation models: TabPFN-2.5 reported a 100% win rate against default XGBoost on ≤10k rows, and TabPFN-3 scales to a million training rows and reports that a single forward pass beats 8-hour-tuned GBDT baselines on TabArena, outperforming AutoGluon 1.5 while roughly 10× faster. The caveats matter: these are pretrained purely on synthetic data, benchmarks have selection effects, and the deployment, licensing and interpretability stories are less mature. So I'd benchmark both on my own data, and still default to a GBDT where I need monotonic constraints, interpretability, cheap retraining and a well-understood ops story.”

10. What is conformal prediction and what guarantee does it give?

A distribution-free, model-agnostic wrapper that turns point predictions into prediction sets or intervals with a finite-sample coverage guarantee. Split conformal is a few lines: fit on a training split, compute a nonconformity score (e.g. absolute residual) on a disjoint calibration split, take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile as q̂, and emit ŷ ± q̂. The guarantee is marginal coverage ≥ 1−α under exchangeability of calibration and test data — which is the assumption that breaks under drift and for time series, hence adaptive and weighted variants. Uses: selective prediction, deferral to a human, safe deployment. And note it says nothing about conditional coverage — the interval may be too wide for easy cases and too narrow for hard ones unless you use a normalised score.

11. ⭐ Your churn model is accurate. The business asks who to give a discount to. What's wrong with using it?

The churn model answers “who will leave?”, but the decision needs “who will stay because we acted?” — those are different quantities. The highest churn scores are dominated by lost causes who leave regardless, and the discount budget also leaks to sure things who would have stayed. Worse, some customers are sleeping dogs for whom the offer is a reminder to cancel, so the treatment has negative effect. You need uplift / CATE estimation — ideally from a randomised holdout, otherwise T/S/X-learners or causal forests with a DML-style approach — evaluated with Qini and uplift curves rather than AUC, because there is no per-row counterfactual label to be right about. The general principle: a predictive model is invalid under intervention.

12. What is double machine learning for?

Estimating a causal effect when you want to use flexible ML for the messy parts without destroying your inference. You have a treatment, an outcome and high-dimensional confounders. DML fits two nuisance models with any ML method — outcome given confounders, and treatment given confounders — takes the residuals of each, and regresses one residual on the other (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell). Two ingredients make it work: Neyman orthogonality, so small errors in the nuisance estimates don't bias the effect to first order, and cross-fitting, so a row's nuisance predictions come from a model that never saw it — otherwise regularization bias and overfitting contaminate the estimate. The payoff is a √n-consistent effect estimate with valid confidence intervals while still using gradient boosting for the nuisances. EconML implements it.
