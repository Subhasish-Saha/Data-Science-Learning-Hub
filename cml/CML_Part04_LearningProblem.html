Part 4 of 17 · The Learning Problem

What “best” means, and why models fail The loss function as a definition of best, empirical risk minimization and where the i.i.d. assumption breaks, the bias-variance decomposition with its diagnostic, L1 versus L2 in all three of its answers, structural and implicit regularization, and the learning-curve reading that tells you whether more data will help.

Two questions from this part appear in almost every classical ML interview: “explain the bias-variance trade-off” and “L1 versus L2”. Both have a complete answer and a partial one, and the difference is visible immediately. The complete bias-variance answer writes the decomposition, gives the two-number diagnostic, and notes that it is exact only for squared loss. The complete L1/L2 answer has a geometric half (the diamond's corners lie on the axes), a probabilistic half (MAP with a Laplace prior), and a practical half (Lasso is unstable under correlation; Elastic Net fixes it). Both widgets in this part let you watch those claims happen.

4.1 Loss functions ⭐

The loss defines what “best” means. Choosing MSE when the business cares about the median is a framing error, not a tuning error — and no amount of hyperparameter search will fix it.

§2.4 established that every common loss is a maximum-likelihood statement. This section is the practical consequence: each loss recovers a different summary of the conditional distribution, and you should pick the one the business acts on.

LossRecoversUse whenFailure mode
MSE / squaredThe conditional meanLarge errors are disproportionately bad; noise is roughly GaussianOne outlier drags the whole fit
MAE / absoluteThe conditional medianOutliers you can't clean; "typical error" is the business quantityNot differentiable at 0 (rarely a real issue); predictions can be less smooth
HuberMean, robustlyBoth — quadratic near zero, linear in the tails; δ sets the elbowOne more hyperparameter to choose
Pinball / quantileThe τ-th quantilePrediction intervals; asymmetric costs (stock-out vs overstock)Quantiles fitted separately can cross — check monotonicity
Log lossCalibrated probabilitiesAny time the probability itself is used downstreamPunishes confident mistakes very hard — which is usually the point
HingeA marginSVMsNo probability comes out of it
FocalEmphasis on hard examplesExtreme imbalance where easy negatives swamp the gradientAdds two hyperparameters; often threshold tuning is enough (§12.9)
Why we optimise log loss instead of accuracy

Accuracy is a step function of the parameters — flat almost everywhere, with a jump where a prediction flips. It has no useful gradient, so there is nothing for an optimiser to follow. Log loss is differentiable, convex in the linear model's parameters, and a proper scoring rule: it is minimised by the true probability, which is why it is the correct surrogate when you need probabilities rather than just a ranking. Brier score is the other proper rule you should be able to name.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhat does the loss function actually decide?

What "best" means. MSE recovers the conditional mean, MAE the median, pinball loss at τ the τ-th quantile, log loss calibrated probabilities. So "which loss?" is really "which summary of the conditional distribution does the business act on?" On a right-skewed target those summaries differ materially — optimising MSE when someone will act on a typical value is a framing error no amount of tuning will fix.

Very commonWhy do we optimise log loss rather than accuracy directly?

Accuracy is a step function of the parameters — flat everywhere with jumps where predictions flip — so it has no useful gradient and can't be optimised directly by gradient methods. Log loss is differentiable, convex in the linear model's parameters, and a proper scoring rule (minimised by the true probability), which is why it gives you well-behaved probabilities rather than just a ranking. It's also the Bernoulli likelihood, so it has a principled derivation rather than being a convenient surrogate.

DesignYour forecast is used to set inventory. Stock-outs cost 4× more than overstock. Which loss?

Pinball loss at τ = 0.8, because the optimal service level under those costs is c_under/(c_under + c_over) = 4/5. That fits the 80th percentile rather than the mean, so you deliberately over-forecast — which is exactly right when under-forecasting is four times worse. Using MSE here would give you the conditional mean and systematically under-stock. I'd also report coverage (what fraction of actuals fall below the forecast) to confirm the quantile is honest, and check that quantiles fitted at several τ don't cross.

GotchaWhen is Huber loss the right choice over MAE?

When you want robustness to outliers without giving up the efficiency of squared error on the bulk of the data. MAE treats a residual of 0.1 and 0.2 as equally informative per unit, so it can converge more slowly and produce less stable fits; Huber is quadratic within δ (efficient, like MSE) and linear outside it (bounded influence, like MAE). Practically: use Huber when most residuals are small and well-behaved but a handful are extreme. The cost is choosing δ, which is often set from a robust scale estimate like the MAD.

4.2 Empirical risk minimization & generalization

You minimise error on the sample you have and hope it transfers. Everything about validation exists because that hope needs checking.

The quantity you want to minimise is the true risk — expected loss over the real data distribution. You cannot compute it, so you minimise the empirical risk — average loss on your sample — and rely on the two converging. That convergence needs the sample to be representative (the i.i.d. assumption) and the hypothesis class not to be so flexible that it can fit any sample perfectly.

Where the i.i.d. assumption actually breaks

ViolationReal exampleConsequence
Not independentMany rows per customer; sequential observationsEffective sample size is far smaller than n; CV is optimistic (§3.6)
Not identically distributed over timePrices, fraud patterns, user mix all driftYesterday's risk estimate doesn't bound tomorrow's (§15.5)
Sampling biasOnly approved applicants have outcomesYou estimate risk on a selected population — the reject-inference problem (§15.7)
FeedbackThe model's decisions change who you observeThe distribution is no longer exogenous at all
Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhat is empirical risk minimization, and what does it assume?

You want to minimise true risk — expected loss over the real distribution — but you can't compute it, so you minimise empirical risk on your sample and rely on the two converging. That relies on the sample being i.i.d. from the deployment distribution and the hypothesis class being constrained enough that fitting the sample means something. Both assumptions break routinely in practice: rows nested in customers aren't independent, distributions drift over time, and selection bias means you only observe outcomes for a filtered population. Each break is why a specific validation technique exists.

ConceptualWhat is the "no free lunch" theorem and what does it imply practically?

Averaged over all possible problems, no algorithm outperforms any other — superiority comes entirely from assumptions matching the problem structure. Practically: there is no universally best model, which is exactly why benchmarking is a step in the process rather than a formality, and why "always use XGBoost" is a well-calibrated heuristic about the distribution of real tabular problems rather than a theorem. It also justifies spending effort on the inductive bias — feature engineering, monotonic constraints, the right loss — rather than only on the algorithm.

ModernWhat is double descent, and does it matter for tabular ML?

Beyond the classical U-curve, test error can rise to a peak near the interpolation threshold (roughly where parameters ≈ samples) and then fall again as capacity grows further — well documented in over-parameterised models. It matters for tabular ML mostly as a fact you should know rather than one you'll exploit: with regularized GBMs on structured data you're firmly in the classical regime, and early stopping keeps you there. Mentioning it shows currency; claiming the U-curve is obsolete would show the opposite.

4.3 Bias, variance and the decomposition ⭐⭐

Be able to write E[(y − f̂)²] = Bias² + Variance + σ², and — more importantly — to diagnose which term you are suffering from using two numbers you already have.

interactive Fit a polynomial and watch the decomposition move
—train MSE
—validation MSE
—gap (val − train)
—diagnosis

Real least-squares fits, solved by Gaussian elimination on the normal equations with inputs mapped to [−1,1] for conditioning. Try: degree 1 (both errors high and close — bias). Degree 12 with 20 points (train ≈ 0, validation explodes — variance). Then raise the training points to 80 and watch the variance problem shrink while the bias problem at degree 1 does not move at all.

The decomposition, term by term

Bias² Error from wrong assumptions
Means
The model class cannot represent the true function, no matter how much data you give it. A straight line fitted to a curve.
Symptom
Training error is high, and validation error is close to it.
Fix
More capacity (deeper trees, higher degree, more boosting rounds), better features, weaker regularization. More data will not help.
Variance Sensitivity to the particular sample
Means
Refit on a different sample from the same distribution and you get a noticeably different function. The model is fitting noise.
Symptom
Training error is low and validation error is far above it.
Fix
More data, more regularization, less capacity, bagging (which attacks variance directly), feature selection.
σ² Irreducible noise
Means
Two identical rows can have different outcomes. Includes genuine randomness and label noise.
Symptom
A floor your validation error never goes below, no matter what you do.
If you beat it
You are memorising noise, or you have leakage. A model that beats the label-noise rate is not learning (§16.7).
The subtlety worth adding

The decomposition is exact for squared loss and only heuristic for others — there is no equally clean bias-variance split for log loss or 0-1 loss (several competing definitions exist, and they disagree). Saying that takes four seconds and shows you know it as a theorem rather than a slogan.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Explain the bias-variance trade-off. Write the decomposition.

E[(y − f̂(x))²] = Bias[f̂]² + Var[f̂] + σ². Bias is error from wrong assumptions — the model class can't represent the truth. Variance is sensitivity to the particular training sample. σ² is irreducible noise. Diagnosis from two numbers: both errors high and close ⇒ bias; train low and validation far above ⇒ variance. Levers: complexity, regularization strength, feature count, tree depth, k, ensemble size. The one-liner they're listening for: bagging reduces variance, boosting reduces bias. And the credit-earning caveat: the decomposition is exact only for squared loss.

DebugTrain MSE 0.02, validation MSE 0.31. What's wrong and what do you do?

High variance — a gap that large means the model is fitting sample-specific noise. In order of what I'd try: more regularization (raise alpha, lower max_depth, raise min_samples_leaf), fewer features if many are noisy, bagging or a larger ensemble, and more data if it's obtainable — a learning curve tells me whether that will help. What I would not do is add capacity. And before any of it, I'd check for leakage in reverse: a gap this large sometimes means the validation set is genuinely different (a time or group boundary), not that the model is overfitting.

ConceptualWhich knobs move bias and which move variance?

Toward less bias, more variance: deeper trees, more leaves, higher polynomial degree, smaller k in kNN, larger C in SVM, more boosting rounds, more features. Toward less variance, more bias: stronger L1/L2, shallower trees, larger min_samples_leaf, larger k, smaller C, early stopping, feature selection. Two special cases worth naming: bagging reduces variance with almost no bias cost (which is why n_estimators in a Random Forest isn't a regularization knob), and more data reduces variance without touching bias at all.

GotchaDoes the bias-variance decomposition apply to classification?

Not cleanly. The exact additive decomposition Bias² + Var + σ² holds for squared loss. For 0-1 loss and log loss there are several proposed decompositions (Kong-Dietterich, Domingos, James) and they don't agree — variance can even reduce error on the wrong side of a boundary. The intuition transfers perfectly and is what interviewers want; the mathematics doesn't. Saying that explicitly is a small, cheap signal that you learned it properly.

4.4 L1 vs L2 regularization ⭐⭐

One of the three most-asked questions in classical ML. The complete answer has a geometric half, a probabilistic half and a practical half — give all three and the question is finished.

interactive Ridge vs Lasso on correlated features — sweep α
—Ridge · exact zeros
—Lasso · exact zeros
—Ridge · val MSE
—Lasso · val MSE

Genuinely fitted: Ridge by solving (XᵀX + αI)w = Xᵀy directly, Lasso by cyclic coordinate descent with the soft-threshold operator. Eight standardised features — x₀…x₃ carry signal (x₂ and x₃ are 0.97-correlated with each other), x₄…x₇ are pure noise. Push α up and watch Ridge shrink everything smoothly while Lasso sets coefficients to exactly zero, killing noise first — and then, revealingly, dropping one of the correlated pair rather than splitting the credit.

The geometric half

The probabilistic half

From §2.4: Ridge is MAP with a Gaussian prior on the coefficients, Lasso is MAP with a Laplace prior. The Laplace density has a sharp spike at zero, so the posterior mode genuinely lands there — the sparsity is not an artefact of the optimiser but a consequence of the prior. This also gives α a meaning: it is σ²/τ², the ratio of noise variance to prior variance, i.e. how strongly you believe coefficients are near zero relative to how noisy the data is.

The practical half

L2 / RidgeL1 / LassoElastic Net
Effect on coefficientsShrinks smoothly, never exactly zeroDrives some to exactly zeroBoth
Feature selectionNoYes, automaticallyYes, in groups
Correlated featuresShrinks them together; handles collinearity wellPicks one arbitrarily, zeroes the rest — unstable across refitsKeeps the group together — this is why it exists
When p > nFineSelects at most n featuresNo such limit
Closed formYes — (XᵀX + αI)⁻¹XᵀyNo — coordinate descentNo
Reach for it whenMany small effects; collinearity; you want stabilityYou believe few features matter and want a sparse modelCorrelated groups, or you don't know which of the above
Standardise before you regularize

The penalty α‖w‖ treats every coefficient on the same footing. If income is in pounds and age in years, their coefficients live on wildly different scales and the penalty falls almost entirely on one of them — you are regularizing by choice of measurement unit. Always standardise inside the pipeline, and note that the intercept is conventionally left unpenalised (there's no reason to shrink the baseline toward zero).

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ L1 vs L2 — what does each do, and why does L1 produce exact zeros?

L2/Ridge shrinks coefficients smoothly, handles multicollinearity by re-conditioning XᵀX + αI, and never reaches exactly zero. L1/Lasso drives coefficients to exactly zero, so it does feature selection. Three reasons for the zeros: Geometric — the L1 constraint region is a diamond whose corners lie on the axes, and elliptical loss contours generically first touch it at a corner. Probabilistic — Lasso is MAP with a Laplace prior, whose density spikes at zero, so the posterior mode lands there. Algorithmic — coordinate descent's soft-thresholding update literally contains a max(·, 0). Then the practical caveat: Lasso is unstable with correlated features, and Elastic Net fixes that.

Very commonWhy must you standardise features before regularizing?

The penalty is on coefficient magnitude, and magnitude depends on the feature's units. Income in pounds and age in years produce coefficients orders of magnitude apart, so the penalty lands almost entirely on one feature — you'd be regularizing by choice of measurement unit rather than by any belief about importance. Standardise inside the pipeline so it's refitted per fold, and leave the intercept unpenalised. Note this is exactly why unregularized OLS doesn't need scaling: the coefficient absorbs the units and nothing compares across features.

ConceptualWhen would you use Elastic Net over Lasso?

With groups of correlated features. Lasso picks one essentially arbitrarily and zeroes the rest, and picks a different one on a slightly different sample — fine for prediction, bad for stability and interpretation, and confusing for anyone who has to explain the model. Elastic Net's L2 component makes correlated coefficients shrink together so the group survives or dies as a unit. Also when p > n: Lasso can select at most n features by construction; Elastic Net has no such cap. The cost is a second hyperparameter (l1_ratio).

GotchaDoes regularization change the bias-variance balance? In which direction?

It adds bias to remove variance. The penalty pulls coefficients away from their unbiased least-squares values — so the estimator is deliberately biased — but it shrinks the variance more than the bias grows, over a useful range of α. That's the whole trade, and it's why there's an optimal α rather than "more is better": push α too far and bias dominates, which is visible in the widget above as validation MSE turning back upward. Worth adding: Ridge can beat OLS in MSE even when OLS is unbiased, which is a nice concrete demonstration that unbiasedness is not the goal.

4.5 Regularization beyond penalties

“Name three regularization techniques that aren't a penalty term” is a common follow-up, and it separates people who learned the concept from people who learned the formula.

KindExamplesMechanism
PenaltyL1, L2, Elastic Net, group lassoAdds a cost for coefficient magnitude
Structuralmax_depth, min_samples_leaf, num_leaves, max_features, polynomial degreeLimits the hypothesis space directly — the model literally cannot express complex functions
StochasticRow subsampling, column subsampling, bagging, dropoutDecorrelates and injects noise, so no single pattern can be relied on
Implicit / early stoppingStop boosting when validation stops improvingHalts before the model reaches full capacity. In boosting, early stopping ≈ shrinkage — they trade off against each other
DataMore data, augmentation, noise injection, label smoothingRaises the bar for memorisation — the same pattern must hold across more cases
ConstraintMonotonic constraints, interaction constraints, non-negativityEncodes domain knowledge as a hard restriction; strong regularizer and a compliance tool (§8.6)
EnsemblingAveraging several modelsVariance reduction without a bias cost (§8.1)
Monotonic constraints are underrated as regularization

Most people know them as a compliance feature — “risk must not decrease as debt rises”. They are also one of the strongest regularizers available on tabular data, because they eliminate an enormous fraction of the hypothesis space using genuine domain knowledge rather than a generic penalty. On small or noisy datasets they frequently improve held-out performance, not just interpretability, and they make the model far more robust to drift. Mentioning that dual role is a strong signal.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualName three regularization techniques that aren't a penalty term.

Structural: tree depth, min_samples_leaf, num_leaves, max_features — these limit the hypothesis space directly. Stochastic: row and column subsampling, bagging, dropout — inject noise so no single pattern can be relied on. Implicit: early stopping, which halts before full capacity is reached and in boosting is approximately equivalent to shrinkage. Two more if they want them: constraints (monotonic and interaction constraints, which are strong regularizers as well as compliance tools) and more data, which raises the bar for memorisation.

ConceptualWhy is early stopping considered regularization?

Because it limits the effective capacity the model reaches. In boosting, each round adds capacity and moves the fit further from the initial constant prediction; stopping early leaves the model closer to that simple starting point, which is functionally the same as shrinking coefficients toward zero. There's a formal correspondence for gradient descent on a linear model: the number of steps and the L2 penalty trade off almost exactly. Practically it means learning rate and number of rounds are not independent knobs — halve the learning rate and you'll need roughly twice the rounds for the same effective complexity.

4.6 Overfitting & underfitting in practice ⭐

The question is never “what is overfitting”. It is “how do you know which one you have, and will more data help?” — and learning curves answer both.

Reading a learning curve, precisely

ShapeReadingAction
Both curves converged, above targetHigh biasMore capacity, better features, less regularization. Not more data
Large gap, still narrowingHigh variance, data-limitedMore data will help; also more regularization or bagging
Large gap, flatHigh variance, capacity-limitedMore data won't close it — reduce capacity or regularize
Both converged at targetYou're doneShip it; spend effort on serving and monitoring
Validation below trainingUsually a bugCheck for leakage in reverse, a mis-stratified split, or a regularizer active only at train time
Validation erratic across sizesValidation set too smallUse CV rather than a single hold-out; report the spread
Label noise sets a ceiling

If 8% of your labels are wrong, no model can be more than ~92% right on that measurement — and a model that beats the noise rate is memorising the noise, not learning. Before blaming the algorithm, audit 200 random labels. This is the practical face of the data-centric argument in §16.7, and it is why “we'll fix it with a better model” is a trap answer (§17.16).

Likely interview questions
Very commonOverfitting vs underfitting — how do you diagnose which one you have?

Two numbers. Training error high and validation error close to it ⇒ high bias / underfitting: add capacity, add features, reduce regularization. Training error low and validation error far above ⇒ high variance / overfitting: more data, more regularization, less capacity, bagging. Then confirm with a learning curve, which also answers the follow-up about whether more data will help. One caution: a huge gap can also mean the validation set is genuinely different — a time or group boundary — rather than the model overfitting, so check the split before concluding.

Very commonWill more data help? How do you know?

Plot a learning curve — training and validation error against training-set size. If both have converged and sit above your target, you have a bias problem and more data will do nothing: change the model, the features, or the regularization. If a substantial gap is still narrowing as size grows, more data will help, and you can extrapolate roughly how much you'd need. The compact statement: more data fixes variance, not bias. And it's worth costing: if 2× the data buys 0.005 AUC, that's a business decision, not a modelling one.

DebuggingValidation error is lower than training error. What's going on?

Almost always a bug or an artefact, not good news. Common causes: regularization active only at training time (dropout, or noise injection) so training loss is measured on a handicapped model; a validation split that's easier — mis-stratified, or a time period with lower base rate; leakage in the other direction, where validation rows appeared in training; or simply a validation set too small to be stable. I'd check the split composition first, then whether training loss is being measured with the regularizer applied.

GotchaYour model hits 99% accuracy on a task humans do at 92%. Good news?

No — that's a red flag. If human agreement is 92%, the labels themselves contain roughly 8% noise, which sets a ceiling: a model that beats it isn't learning the concept, it's memorising the specific noise pattern in your labels. The likely explanations are leakage (§3.7) or test-set contamination. I'd check the label-generation process, measure inter-annotator agreement properly, and audit 200 random cases where the model and the label disagree — that audit usually explains it within an hour.

4.7 Discriminative vs generative

A clean conceptual axis that interviewers use to test whether you understand models rather than remember them.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualDiscriminative vs generative — an example and a trade-off.

Discriminative models P(y|x) directly: logistic regression, SVM, trees, GBMs. Generative models the joint P(x,y) and derives the posterior by Bayes' rule: Naive Bayes, LDA/QDA, GMM, HMM. Generative imposes more structure so it needs less data, handles missing features naturally, and can sample; discriminative typically wins on accuracy with enough data because all its capacity goes into the boundary rather than into modelling the inputs. Same axis as HMM vs CRF in sequence labelling.

Deep diveIs there a case where a generative model beats a discriminative one on the same data?

Yes, in the small-sample regime. The classic result (Ng & Jordan) compares Naive Bayes with logistic regression on the same features: Naive Bayes has higher asymptotic error because its independence assumption is wrong, but it converges to that error much faster — so with few training examples it wins, and the discriminative model overtakes it only once there's enough data. That's the practical version of "more structure means less data needed". It also explains why generative methods persist in domains where labelled data is genuinely scarce.
