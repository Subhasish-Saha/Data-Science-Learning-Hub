What ML is, the maths, data before models, leakage, bias-variance, regularization

1.1 The framing question ⭐

Machine learning replaces explicit rules with examples plus a loss function. Everything else in this subject is a consequence of that one trade.

When you write a rule, you own the logic and you can audit it. When you fit a model, you own the data, the loss and the evaluation — and the logic is inferred. That is a good trade when the rule is genuinely unknown or too complex to write, and a terrible trade when it isn't. Interviewers probe this constantly, usually disguised as a design question.

ParadigmWhat you give itWhat it learnsA real problem
SupervisedFeatures + labelsf: x → yCredit default, claim severity, churn
UnsupervisedFeatures onlyStructure — clusters, components, densityCustomer segmentation, anomaly detection
Semi-supervisedFew labels + much unlabelled dataf: x → y, propagatedFraud where only confirmed cases are labelled
Self-supervisedUnlabelled data + a pretext taskRepresentationsPretraining — the whole of the NLP guide
ReinforcementAn environment + a rewardA policy π: s → aBidding, inventory control, routing

A distinction most candidates blur, and which is worth a lot of credit: prediction, inference and decision are three different projects. Predicting who will churn is a supervised model. Understanding why people churn is inference, and needs identifiability, not accuracy. Deciding who to call is a decision problem and — as §6.9 will show — a churn model is the wrong tool for it.

Two more axes worth naming

Online vs batch: does the model update continuously from a stream, or is it refitted on a schedule? Almost all production classical ML is batch-trained and served online — say so, because “online learning” is usually the wrong answer to “how do you keep it fresh”. Transductive vs inductive: do you need predictions only for a known fixed set of rows (transductive — label propagation, some graph methods), or a function that generalises to rows you have never seen (inductive — everything else)?

1.2 The lifecycle

The loop is not linear, and the modelling box — the one everyone practises — is the smallest box in it.

Define success before you model

Before a single fit(), three things should be written down: the target metric, the baseline it must beat, and the decision it feeds. If you cannot state the decision, you cannot choose the metric — and if you cannot choose the metric, you are optimising something arbitrary. Interviewers who ask “how would you know the project succeeded?” are checking whether you do this.

1.3 Framing a problem correctly

Choosing the target variable is the highest-leverage decision in the whole project, and the one most often made in thirty seconds.

Take “predict churn”. It is not a specification. Churn could be a cancelled subscription, ninety days of inactivity, a downgrade, or a support ticket saying “how do I close my account”. Each definition produces a different label, a different base rate and a different model. And the same business problem can be framed four ways:

FramingTargetWhat it buysWhat it costs
ClassificationWill this customer churn in 30 days?Simple, calibratable, thresholdableLoses timing information
Regression / survivalWhen will they churn?Timing for the intervention calendar; handles censoringHarder to evaluate and explain
RankingWho are the top 500 at risk?Matches a fixed outreach capacity exactlyScores stop being probabilities
Uplift / CATEWho will churn only if we do nothing?The correct target for a retention offerNeeds experimental data; harder to validate
Cost asymmetry, in the units the business uses

“What does a false positive cost versus a false negative?” is the question that turns a metric argument into a business conversation. A wrongly-blocked legitimate payment costs a lost sale plus a support contact; a missed fraud costs the transaction value plus a chargeback fee. Once those are numbers, the threshold (§4.9) stops being a matter of taste.

1.4 Baselines you must always report

A model without a baseline is an unfalsifiable claim. Reporting baselines is cheap, and forgetting them is one of the most visible junior tells.

BaselineWhat it isWhat it tells you
Majority / meanAlways predict the majority class or the training meanThe floor. On a 99:1 problem it scores 99% accuracy — which is the whole argument of §4.6
Previous valueNaive or seasonal-naive forecastThe one that embarrasses time-series models most often (§3.14)
The existing ruleWhatever the business does todayThe real bar. If you can't beat it, the project's value is negative
Random-but-calibratedPredict the base rate for everyoneFixes intuition about what a log loss or Brier score of X means
Strong simple modelLogistic regression or a small GBM on obvious featuresThe one that usually gets 90–95% of the final performance in 2% of the effort
The finding is allowed to be “don't ship it”

If the complex model doesn't beat those baselines by a margin that justifies its serving cost, its retraining cost and its risk, that is the result — report it. Candidates who volunteer this are describing a real project; candidates who can't imagine it are describing a tutorial.

1.7 Probability

Two results carry almost all of the interview weight: the base-rate fallacy, and the fact that your favourite losses are maximum likelihood in disguise.

EstimatorAssumptionEquivalent loss
MLEGaussian noiseLeast squares / MSE
MLEBernoulli outcomeLog loss / cross-entropy
MLEPoisson countsPoisson deviance (§2.4)
MAPGaussian prior on wRidge / L2
MAPLaplace prior on wLasso / L1
A connection worth volunteering

“Ridge is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior and Lasso with a Laplace prior” is one of the highest-value sentences in this guide. It reframes regularization from “a trick that stops overfitting” to “a stated belief about the coefficients” — and it immediately explains why L1 zeroes things out: the Laplace density has a sharp peak at zero, so the posterior mode lands exactly there.

1.8 Statistics

The statistics you need are the statistics of comparing two models honestly and of not being fooled by a subgroup.

What a p-value is not

It is not the probability the hypothesis is true, not the probability the result was chance, and not a measure of effect size. It is the probability of data at least this extreme under the null. If you evaluated forty model variants, some of them will clear p < 0.05 by construction — report effect sizes with confidence intervals, and correct for multiple comparisons. The two resampling tools you will actually use are the bootstrap (a CI for a metric or a difference of metrics) and the paired permutation test (is this difference bigger than reshuffling would produce?).

1.11 Missing data

The taxonomy is examinable, the indicator column is the trick nobody mentions, and the pipeline rule is non-negotiable.

StrategyWhat it doesCost
Listwise deletionDrop rows with any NaNUnbiased only under MCAR; can destroy most of your data
Mean / median / modeConstant fillShrinks variance, distorts correlations, and pretends to certainty
kNN / MICEPredict the missing value from other columnsBetter under MAR; expensive; must be fitted on the training fold only
Indicator columnadd_indicator=TrueAlmost free, frequently the biggest win
Native handlingXGBoost/LightGBM learn a default direction per split; sklearn's HistGradientBoosting* takes NaN directlyUsually the best answer on tabular data — no imputation at all
Imputation is model-fitting

A median is a statistic learned from data. Computing it over the whole dataset before splitting leaks test information into training — the same class of error as scaling before splitting. Imputers belong inside the Pipeline, and the pipeline is fitted inside the CV loop.

1.12 Outliers & robustness

Detection is easy and mostly irrelevant. The judgement call is what the point is.

Before you touch an outlier, answer one question: is this a data error (a negative age, a duplicated decimal point), a rare but real event (a genuine £400,000 transaction), or exactly the thing you are trying to predict (fraud, equipment failure, a claim)? Removing outliers from a fraud dataset is removing the positive class.

DetectionAssumptionHandlingWhen
z-score > 3Roughly Gaussian, univariateRemoveConfirmed data errors only
IQR fence (1.5×)Distribution-free, univariateClip / winsorizeHeavy tails you want bounded
MahalanobisMultivariate GaussianTransform (log, Yeo-Johnson)Skew rather than true outliers
Isolation ForestAnomalies are easy to isolateUse a robust modelThe default answer — trees, Huber, quantile regression
LOFLocal density variesRobust scaling (median/MAD)Clusters of differing density
Robust by construction beats robust by cleaning

Trees split on order, not magnitude, so a value of 10⁹ is just “the largest one” — they barely notice outliers. Huber loss is quadratic near zero and linear in the tails; quantile regression ignores magnitude entirely. Reaching for a robust loss is usually a better answer than deleting rows, because deletion is irreversible and unauditable.

1.13 Scaling & transformation

“Which models need scaling?” is asked in almost every interview, and the reason matters more than the list.

Log-transforming the target changes the loss

Fitting on log(y) and minimising MSE means you are optimising relative error, and exp(ŷ) gives you back the conditional median, not the mean. That is often what you wanted — but say it out loud, and remember to invert before reporting an error in the target's own units.

1.15 Splitting — where careers are made or lost ⭐

One rule covers every case: your split must simulate how the model will actually be used. Random splits are the default and they are wrong more often than people think.

The three-case answer

When asked “when is a random split wrong?”, give three concrete cases rather than a definition: (1) rows grouped by an entity — many transactions per customer; (2) temporal data, where random splitting trains on the future; (3) near-duplicate rows, which put copies of the same record on both sides. And touch the test set exactly once — every extra look is a selection you have not accounted for.

1.16 Data leakage — the most examinable topic in ML ⭐⭐

If you learn one thing from this part, learn the four kinds of leakage, their symptoms, and the one structural fix that prevents most of them.

interactive Leakage simulator — the CV score you'd have reported

This is a real simulation, not a mock-up: 800 rows are generated with a genuinely weak signal, each enabled leak adds a component to the validation-time score that was computed using the validation labels, and both AUCs are computed exactly by the Mann-Whitney rank formula. The production score never sees those channels — which is precisely what happens when the pipeline is refitted on training data alone.

How to audit someone else's notebook in five minutes

(1) Find the split — is it the first data operation? (2) Search for fit_transform outside a pipeline. (3) Sort feature importances and ask what the top feature is and when it was recorded. (4) Re-run with a time-based split and see how far the score falls. (5) Check for duplicate IDs across train and test. Those five checks catch the overwhelming majority of real leakage.

1.17 Loss functions

The loss defines what “best” means. Choosing MSE when the business cares about the median is a framing error, not a tuning error.

LossOptimisesUse when
MSE / squaredThe meanLarge errors are disproportionately bad; no heavy outliers
MAE / absoluteThe medianOutliers you can't clean; “typical error” is the business quantity
HuberMean, robustlyBoth — quadratic near zero, linear in the tails (δ sets the elbow)
Pinball / quantileA quantilePrediction intervals; asymmetric costs (stock-outs vs overstock)
Log lossCalibrated probabilitiesAny time the probability itself is used downstream
HingeA marginSVMs — no probability comes out of it
FocalHard examplesExtreme imbalance where easy negatives swamp the gradient
Why we optimise log loss instead of accuracy

Accuracy is a step function of the parameters — flat almost everywhere, with a jump where a prediction flips. It has no useful gradient. Log loss is differentiable, convex in the linear model's parameters, and a proper scoring rule: it is minimised by the true probability, which is exactly why it is the right surrogate when you need probabilities rather than just a ranking. Brier score is the other proper rule you should be able to name.

1.18 Empirical risk minimization & generalization

You minimise error on the sample you have and hope it transfers. Everything about validation exists because that hope needs checking.

1.19 Bias, variance, and the decomposition ⭐

Be able to write E[(y − f̂)²] = Bias² + Variance + σ², and — more importantly — to diagnose which term you are suffering from using two numbers you already have.

—train MSE
—validation MSE
—gap (val − train)
—diagnosis

Real least-squares fits, solved by Gaussian elimination on the normal equations with the inputs mapped to [−1,1] for conditioning. Try: degree 1 (both errors high and close — bias). Degree 12 with 20 points (train ≈ 0, validation explodes — variance). Then raise the training points to 80 and watch the variance problem shrink while the bias problem at degree 1 does not move at all.

The subtlety worth adding

The decomposition is exact for squared loss and only heuristic for others — there is no equally clean bias-variance split for log loss or 0-1 loss. Saying that shows you know it as a theorem rather than a slogan, and it takes four seconds.

1.20 Regularization ⭐

L1 versus L2 is one of the three most-asked questions in classical ML. The answer has a geometric half, a probabilistic half, and a practical half — give all three.

interactive Ridge vs Lasso on correlated features — sweep α
—Ridge · exact zeros
—Lasso · exact zeros
—Ridge · val MSE
—Lasso · val MSE

Genuinely fitted: Ridge by solving (XᵀX + αI)w = Xᵀy directly, Lasso by cyclic coordinate descent with the soft-threshold operator. Eight standardised features — x₀…x₃ carry signal (x₂ and x₃ are 0.97-correlated with each other), x₄…x₇ are pure noise. Push α up and watch Ridge shrink everything smoothly toward zero while Lasso sets coefficients to exactly zero, killing the noise features first — and then, revealingly, dropping one of the correlated pair rather than splitting the credit.

Standardise before you regularize

The penalty α‖w‖ treats every coefficient on the same footing. If income is in pounds and age in years, their coefficients live on wildly different scales and the penalty falls almost entirely on one of them — you are regularizing by measurement unit. Always standardise inside the pipeline, and note that the intercept is conventionally left unpenalised.

PenaltyL1, L2, Elastic NetAdds a cost for coefficient magnitude

1.21 Overfitting & underfitting in practice

The question is never “what is overfitting”. It is “how do you know which one you have, and will more data help?” — and learning curves answer both.

Label noise sets a ceiling

If 8% of your labels are wrong, no model can be more than ~92% right on that measurement — and a model that beats the noise rate is memorising the noise, not learning. Before blaming the algorithm, audit 200 random labels. This is the practical face of the data-centric argument in §6.10, and it is also why “we'll fix it with a better model” is a trap answer (§7.16).

1.22 Discriminative vs generative

A clean conceptual axis that interviewers use to test whether you understand models rather than remember them.

1.23 Interview focus for Part 1

Twelve questions that between them cover most of what this part exists to teach. Answer out loud first.

Part 1 · foundations, data and the learning problem
1. When would you not use machine learning?

Four branches. (1) The rule is stable and writable — ship the rule, it is auditable and free. (2) You have no labels and no path to getting them — reframe as unsupervised, or buy labels. (3) An unexplainable error is unacceptable — use a deterministic system, or ML with a human in the loop. (4) The data is too small for the signal you want — a GLM, a statistical estimate or a lookup table will beat an under-trained model. Volunteering this unprompted is a strong senior signal.

2. ⭐ What is data leakage? Name four distinct kinds.

Target leakage: a feature that is a consequence of the target or recorded after it (account_closed_date). Train-test contamination: scaling, imputing, encoding, selecting or SMOTE-ing before the split. Temporal leakage: features computed over the whole period, or from the future. Group leakage: the same entity or a near-duplicate on both sides. Symptom in all four: an implausibly good validation score that collapses in production. Structural fix: everything learned from data goes inside a Pipeline, and the pipeline is fitted inside the CV loop.

3. ⭐ Explain the bias-variance trade-off and write the decomposition.

E[(y − f̂(x))²] = Bias[f̂]² + Var[f̂] + σ². Bias is error from wrong assumptions — the model can't represent the truth. Variance is sensitivity to the particular training sample. σ² is irreducible noise. Diagnosis: both errors high and close ⇒ bias; train low, validation far above ⇒ variance. Levers: complexity, regularization strength, feature count, depth, k, ensemble size. And the one-liner: bagging reduces variance, boosting reduces bias. Worth adding that the decomposition is exact only for squared loss.

4. ⭐ L1 vs L2 — what does each do, and why does L1 give exact zeros?

L2/Ridge shrinks coefficients smoothly, handles multicollinearity, never reaches exactly zero; it is MAP with a Gaussian prior. L1/Lasso drives coefficients to exactly zero, so it performs feature selection; it is MAP with a Laplace prior. The geometric reason: the L1 constraint region is a diamond whose corners lie on the axes, and the elliptical loss contours typically touch it at a corner — where one or more coefficients are exactly zero. The L2 region is a circle with no corners, so contact happens at a generic point. Elastic Net mixes them and keeps correlated groups together where Lasso would pick one arbitrarily.

5. Why must you standardise features before regularizing?

The penalty is applied to coefficient magnitudes, and coefficient magnitude depends on the feature's units. Income in pounds and age in years produce coefficients orders of magnitude apart, so the penalty lands almost entirely on one feature — you would be regularizing by choice of measurement unit. Standardise inside the pipeline; leave the intercept unpenalised.

6. When is a random split wrong? Give three cases.

(1) Grouped rows — many transactions per customer; the model learns to recognise the customer, so use GroupKFold. (2) Temporal data — a random split trains on the future to predict the past and hides drift; use TimeSeriesSplit with a gap. (3) Near-duplicates — deduplicate before splitting, or copies straddle the boundary. The governing rule: your split must simulate how the model will be used.

7. MCAR / MAR / MNAR — and which is dangerous?

MCAR: missingness is independent of everything; deletion is unbiased, imputation is fine. MAR: missingness depends on observed variables — model-based imputation (MICE, kNN) is principled. MNAR: missingness depends on the unobserved value itself (high earners decline to state income) — no imputation can recover it from the data alone; you must model the mechanism or flag the limitation. MNAR is the dangerous one. In all cases, add a missingness indicator, and prefer the GBM's native NaN handling on tabular data.

8. Which models need feature scaling and which don't — and why?

Need it: anything that measures distance (kNN, k-means, DBSCAN, RBF-SVM), maximises variance (PCA), or penalises coefficient magnitude (Ridge, Lasso, regularized logistic), plus neural nets for gradient conditioning. Don't: trees and every tree ensemble, because a split is a threshold on rank order and a monotone transform can't change which side of it a value falls on. Unregularized OLS also doesn't need it — the coefficient just absorbs the units — but the moment a penalty appears, it does.

9. What is target encoding and how do you do it without leaking?

Replace a category with a statistic of the target within that category. Done naively it computes each row's encoding using that row's own label, which puts the target into the feature — CV soars, production collapses, and rare categories become literally their own labels. Do it out-of-fold/cross-fitted (each fold's encoding from the other folds), smoothed toward the global mean by category count, with an explicit unknown bucket for serve-time categories — all inside the pipeline. CatBoost's ordered target statistics solve it structurally.

10. What does the loss function actually decide?

What “best” means. MSE recovers the conditional mean, MAE the median, pinball loss at τ the τ-th quantile, log loss calibrated probabilities. So the question “which loss?” is really “which summary of the conditional distribution does the business act on?” And we optimise log loss rather than accuracy because accuracy is a step function with no useful gradient, while log loss is differentiable, convex in the linear parameters, and a proper scoring rule.

11. Will more data help? How would you know?

Plot a learning curve — error against training-set size for both train and validation. If the two curves have converged and sit above your target, you have a bias problem and more rows will do nothing: add capacity, add features, or reduce regularization. If a large gap is still closing, you have a variance problem and more data will help — as will more regularization or bagging. More data fixes variance, not bias.

12. Discriminative vs generative — an example and a trade-off.

Discriminative models P(y|x) directly: logistic regression, SVM, trees, GBMs. Generative models the joint P(x,y) and derives the posterior by Bayes' rule: Naive Bayes, LDA/QDA, GMM, HMM. Generative models impose more structure so they need less data, handle missing features naturally and can sample; discriminative models typically win on accuracy with enough data because all their capacity goes into the boundary. Same axis as HMM vs CRF in sequence labelling.

2.1 Linear regression

The model everyone can write down and almost nobody can criticise properly. The criticism is the interview.

The closed form is w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, obtained by setting ∂(Xw−y)ᵀ(Xw−y)/∂w = 2Xᵀ(Xw−y) to zero. 2.2 Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net

You met the geometry in §1.20. Here is what it looks like as α sweeps, and how you choose it.

When Elastic Net beats Lasso

With a group of correlated features, Lasso picks one essentially arbitrarily and zeroes the rest — refit on a slightly different sample and it picks a different one. That is fine for prediction and terrible for interpretation or stability. Elastic Net's L2 component makes correlated coefficients shrink together, so the group survives or dies as a group. Also relevant when p > n: Lasso can select at most n features; Elastic Net has no such limit.

2.8 Support Vector Machines ⭐

Mostly displaced on tabular data, and still a favourite interview topic — because margin, the dual and the kernel trick test whether you understand optimisation or only APIs.

interactive The kernel trick, watched happening

In one dimension the two classes are interleaved and no threshold separates them. Add a second coordinate x² and a straight line does. That is the whole idea — and the “trick” is that the SVM's dual objective only ever needs inner products ⟨φ(x), φ(x′)⟩, which a kernel K(x,x′) computes directly. You get the high-dimensional geometry without ever constructing the high-dimensional vector — and with the RBF kernel that space is infinite-dimensional.

The honest 2026 positioning

“When would you still choose an SVM?” — small-to-medium data with a clear margin, high-dimensional sparse features where a linear SVM is excellent and extremely fast (LinearSVC on text), and cases where you want a maximum-margin decision rule rather than a probability. Otherwise gradient boosting wins on tabular data, trains faster, needs no scaling and handles mixed types. Also: SVMs produce no probabilities natively — probability=True runs Platt scaling with an internal 5-fold CV, which is slow and often poorly calibrated anyway.

2.11 Properties, good and bad — and the importance trap ⭐

Trees are the most-loved and most-misused model in tabular ML. The misuse is almost always feature_importances_.

⭐ The feature-importance trap

The default feature_importances_ is mean decrease in impurity, and it has two documented biases: it favours high-cardinality and continuous features (they offer more candidate split points, so more chances to reduce impurity by luck), and it is computed on training data, so it rewards features the model overfitted to. 2.12 Interview focus for Part 2

Eleven questions covering the whole model zoo. If you can answer these without hedging, the “which model would you use” conversation goes well.

Part 2 · linear, distance and tree models
1. State the assumptions of linear regression and what breaks when each fails.

Linearity (in the parameters) — fails ⇒ biased predictions, visible as curvature in the residual plot; fix with splines, polynomials or a different model. Independent errors — fails with time or clustered data ⇒ standard errors are wrong. Homoscedasticity — fails ⇒ inefficient estimates and wrong CIs; fix with a log/GLM or robust standard errors. Normal residuals — needed only for inference, not prediction. No perfect multicollinearity — fails ⇒ XᵀX singular, no unique solution; near-failure inflates coefficient variance (VIF > 5–10).

2. Why can't you use MSE as the loss for logistic regression?

Two reasons. Composed with a sigmoid, squared error is non-convex in the parameters, so gradient descent has no guarantee of finding the global optimum. And log loss is the negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model — it is the MLE, and a proper scoring rule, so it yields well-behaved probabilities. MSE also barely penalises a confidently wrong prediction, which is exactly what you want punished.

3. Interpret a logistic regression coefficient. What is an odds ratio?

A one-unit increase in x increases the log-odds by β, equivalently multiplies the odds by exp(β) — the odds ratio. β = 0.4 ⇒ odds ×1.49. The effect on the probability is not constant: it is largest near p = 0.5 and vanishes in the tails. Standardise features first if you want to compare coefficient magnitudes, and remember C in sklearn is the inverse penalty strength.

4. What is multicollinearity, how do you detect it, and does it hurt?

Two or more features carrying near-identical information, so XᵀX becomes ill-conditioned and the loss surface has a flat valley. Detect with a correlation matrix, condition number, or VIF = 1/(1−R²ⱼ) above ~5–10. It inflates coefficient variance — signs flip and magnitudes swing between refits — so it damages interpretation and stability, but usually not predictive accuracy. Fixes: drop one, combine them, use PCA, or use Ridge, which literally re-conditions the problem by adding αI.

5. Why does R² always increase when you add a feature?

Adding a column can only enlarge the space the fit is chosen from, so the residual sum of squares cannot rise — even for random noise, since the coefficient can always be set to zero and least squares will do slightly better than that by chance. Use adjusted R², which penalises p, or a held-out score. Test-set R² is a different statistic and can be negative.

6. When would you reach for a GLM — Poisson or Tweedie — instead of linear regression?

When the target's distribution is obviously not Gaussian and you know its shape. Poisson (log link) for counts; negative binomial when counts are over-dispersed; Gamma for positive right-skewed amounts like claim severity; Tweedie for zero-inflated positive targets like insurance pure premium, where most policies claim nothing and a few claim a lot. You get sensible non-negative predictions, a correct variance function, and coefficients a regulator will accept — and all three objectives exist in XGBoost and LightGBM if you want trees.

7. How does kNN work, and why is scaling mandatory?

Store the training set; to predict, find the k nearest rows by some distance and take their majority vote or mean. Because the prediction is a distance computation, a feature measured in a larger unit dominates the distance entirely — income in pounds swamps age in years, so you are effectively doing 1-D nearest neighbour on income. k is a pure bias-variance dial: k = 1 has zero training error and maximum variance; k = n is the majority-class baseline. It also suffers most from the curse of dimensionality.

8. Why does Naive Bayes work despite a false independence assumption?

Because classification needs the correct argmax, not correct probabilities. Correlated features are counted as independent evidence, which multiplies their effect and drives the posterior toward 0 or 1 — but the ordering of the classes usually survives. Consequences: it is badly calibrated and overconfident, so never use its predict_proba as a probability without calibration. Add Laplace smoothing so an unseen pair doesn't zero the product, and compute in log space.

9. Explain the SVM margin, the role of C, and the kernel trick.

The SVM finds the hyperplane with the largest margin — distance 2/‖w‖ to the nearest points, the support vectors, which are the only points that define the solution. The soft margin allows violations with slack variables; C trades margin width against violations (large C ⇒ narrow margin, low bias, high variance). The kernel trick: the dual objective depends on the data only through inner products, so replacing ⟨xᵢ,xⱼ⟩ with K(xᵢ,xⱼ) gives you the geometry of a high-dimensional feature space — infinite-dimensional for the RBF kernel — without ever computing φ(x). Cost: O(n²)–O(n³) training, mandatory scaling, and no native probabilities.

10. LDA vs PCA — what's the difference in objective?

PCA is unsupervised and maximises total variance; it never looks at the labels, so it can discard exactly the direction that separates classes if that direction happens to be low-variance. LDA is supervised and maximises the ratio of between-class scatter to within-class scatter, giving at most k−1 components for k classes. Both are linear projections; only one of them is trying to help your classifier.

11. ⭐ Why is impurity-based feature importance misleading? What do you use instead?

Mean decrease in impurity is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features — more candidate split points means more chances to reduce impurity by luck — and it is computed on training data, so it rewards overfitting. A random continuous ID-like column will show up as important. Use permutation importance on held-out data, which measures the actual drop in a metric when a feature is shuffled. Then state the remaining caveat: with correlated features, permuting one leaves its information available through the other, so importance is understated — and SHAP shares that problem in a different form (§5.7–5.8). Three methods, three questions; pick the one matching the question you're actually asking.

3.1 Why ensembles work

Combining models helps only under one condition, and naming that condition is what separates a real answer from “more models is better”.

The one-liner that gets asked constantly

Bagging attacks variance; boosting attacks bias. Bagging trains high-variance, low-bias learners in parallel on different bootstrap samples and averages, which shrinks variance while leaving bias roughly unchanged. Boosting trains weak, high-bias learners sequentially, each correcting the previous ensemble's residual error, which drives bias down — and can drive variance up, which is why boosting overfits with too many rounds and bagging does not.

3.15 Interview focus for Part 3

Twelve questions on the material that decides most tabular ML interviews.

Part 3 · ensembles, unsupervised and beyond
1. ⭐ Bagging vs boosting — what does each reduce, and why?

Bagging trains high-variance low-bias learners in parallel on bootstrap samples and averages them. Averaging M decorrelated estimators cuts variance toward ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/M, leaving bias roughly unchanged — so it reduces variance. Boosting trains weak high-bias learners sequentially, each fitted to the current ensemble's errors, so the ensemble's systematic error shrinks — it reduces bias, and can increase variance, which is why it overfits with too many rounds and needs early stopping.

2. How does Random Forest differ from plain bagging? What is OOB error?

Random Forest adds random feature subsetting at each split (max_features). Without it, a dominant feature is chosen at the root of every tree and the trees end up highly correlated — and correlation is the ceiling on how much averaging can help. OOB error: each bootstrap omits about (1−1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 36.8% of rows, so each row can be predicted by the trees that never saw it, giving a validation estimate for free without a holdout.

3. ⭐ Explain gradient boosting. What exactly is each new tree fitted to?

Start from a constant prediction. At each round, compute the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions — the pseudo-residuals — fit a shallow tree to those, and add it scaled by the learning rate: Fₘ = Fₘ₋₁ + ν·hₘ. For squared loss the negative gradient is the ordinary residual, which is why the textbook version says “fit to the residuals”; saying negative gradient is what generalises it to log loss, Poisson, quantile and everything else.

4. Can you overfit a Random Forest by adding trees? Can you overfit a GBM?

RF: adding trees does not overfit — the ensemble converges rather than degrading. But a forest of deep unpruned trees on noisy data absolutely can overfit by memorising label noise; control it with min_samples_leaf and max_features, not with fewer trees. GBM: yes, readily — each round fits the remaining error, including noise. Use a validation set with early stopping, plus shrinkage and subsampling.

5. XGBoost vs LightGBM vs CatBoost — the real differences, and how do you choose?

XGBoost: second-order (Newton) approximation using gradients and Hessians, explicit λ/α/γ regularization, sparsity-aware learned default direction for missing values, level-wise growth by default. LightGBM: leaf-wise growth, plus GOSS (keep large-gradient rows, subsample the rest) and EFB (bundle mutually exclusive sparse features) — fastest on large data. CatBoost: ordered boosting and ordered target statistics to eliminate the target leakage in naive encoding, symmetric oblivious trees for very fast inference, strong defaults. Choice: many high-cardinality categoricals + little tuning time ⇒ CatBoost; very large data + tight budget ⇒ LightGBM; maximum control and ecosystem ⇒ XGBoost. Then say that once tuned they are usually within noise of each other.

6. Leaf-wise vs level-wise growth — what's the risk of leaf-wise?

Level-wise expands every node at a depth before going deeper, giving balanced trees and predictable cost. Leaf-wise always expands the leaf with the largest loss reduction, so it reaches a lower loss with fewer leaves — faster and often more accurate. The risk is that it grows very deep down one branch and overfits small or noisy datasets. Control it with num_leaves and min_data_in_leaf — and note that setting max_depth alone while leaving num_leaves at its default is a common misconfiguration.

7. What problem does CatBoost's ordered boosting solve?

The prediction shift caused by target leakage in naive gradient boosting with target statistics: if a row's encoding and residual are computed using its own label, training residuals are systematically biased and the model is over-optimistic on data it has seen. Ordered boosting computes each row's statistics and residuals from only the rows preceding it in a random permutation (several permutations, to avoid starving early rows) — the algorithmic version of out-of-fold target encoding.

8. How does stacking work, and what's the leakage trap in it?

Base models produce predictions that become features for a meta-learner. The trap: those meta-features must be out-of-fold — each row's base prediction must come from a model that never saw that row. Train the base models on all the data and predict the same data and you have handed the meta-learner in-sample fit, so it learns to trust whichever base model overfits hardest. Keep the meta-learner simple (regularized linear), value diversity over individual strength, and be honest that stacking rarely survives a production review on latency and maintenance grounds.

9. ⭐ How does k-means work, how do you choose k, and what are its assumptions?

Lloyd's algorithm: initialise centroids (k-means++, which seeds them far apart), assign each point to its nearest centroid, recompute centroids as cluster means, repeat until assignments stop changing. It minimises inertia (within-cluster sum of squares), which is non-convex, so the result depends on the seed — hence n_init. Assumptions: spherical clusters, roughly equal variance, and a meaningful Euclidean distance, so it is scale-sensitive and outlier-sensitive. Choosing k: elbow, silhouette, gap statistic — and honestly, k is usually a business constraint.

10. ⭐ Explain PCA. Why must you scale first? How many components?

PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance — the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, equivalently the right singular vectors of the centred data matrix. Scaling: variance depends on units, so an unscaled feature measured in small units dominates the first component purely by measurement convention. Number of components: cumulative explained-variance ratio (a threshold like 95%), the scree-plot elbow, or — best — cross-validated downstream performance. Caveat to add: components are linear mixtures of all features, so interpretability is limited.

11. PCA vs t-SNE vs UMAP — and what's the trap with t-SNE plots?

PCA is linear, deterministic, reversible and safe as a preprocessing step. t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear, stochastic visualisation tools. The trap: in a t-SNE plot, distances between clusters and the sizes of clusters are not meaningful — the method preserves local neighbourhoods only, and the layout is heavily influenced by perplexity. UMAP preserves more global structure and is faster, but is still stochastic and still primarily for visualisation. Never conclude “these two clusters are similar because they're close on the plot”.

12. ML or ARIMA for forecasting? And how do you validate it?

Many related series with shared structure and exogenous drivers ⇒ a global gradient boosting model over lag, rolling, calendar and Fourier features usually wins. One short series ⇒ classical methods are hard to beat and easier to explain. Validate with walk-forward / rolling-origin splits — never a random split — with a gap (purge and embargo) when features arrive with latency. Report MASE when comparing across series, avoid MAPE near zeros, and always show the seasonal-naive baseline. Finally: trees can't extrapolate, so detrend or use a hybrid for trending series.

4.12 Interview focus for Part 4

Twelve questions from the part where most classical ML interviews are actually decided.

Part 4 · validation, metrics, thresholds and imbalance
1. ⭐ Accuracy is 95% and the model is useless. Explain.

Ask for the base rate. If 95% of rows are negative, predicting “negative” for everything scores 95% while catching nothing — accuracy has become a majority-class detector. Report instead: the confusion matrix at the deployed threshold, per-class recall, and PR-AUC / average precision. And note the second failure: accuracy is also blind to the cost difference between a false positive and a false negative.

2. ⭐ ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC — when do you prefer which?

ROC-AUC plots TPR against FPR and is a good summary of pure ranking quality when classes are roughly balanced. Its weakness under imbalance is structural: FPR divides by a huge negative count, so thousands of false positives barely move the x-axis — it flatters rare-positive problems. PR-AUC / average precision plots precision against recall, so it responds directly to false positives among your alerts, and its baseline is the base rate rather than 0.5. Prefer PR-AUC when positives are rare and you care about the alert queue. Also state that neither says anything about calibration.

3. What does ROC-AUC actually mean, probabilistically?

It is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative: P(score(x⁺) > score(x⁻)), with ties counted as a half. It equals the normalised Mann-Whitney U statistic. That is why it is threshold-free and invariant to any monotone transform of the scores — including Platt scaling, which is why calibration leaves it unchanged.

4. Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report?

Micro pools TP/FP/FN across all classes, so it is dominated by frequent classes — and in single-label multi-class it is exactly equal to accuracy. Macro averages per-class F1 with equal weight, so a rare class counts as much as a common one; report it when the rare classes matter. Weighted averages by support and mostly reproduces micro. The real answer is: report the one that matches the decision, and say which one you are reporting.

5. ⭐ What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?

A model is calibrated if, among cases predicted at p, a fraction p are actually positive. Measure with a reliability diagram, ECE, and the Brier score. Fix with Platt scaling (fit a sigmoid on held-out scores — parametric, works with little data), isotonic regression (non-parametric, more flexible, needs more data and can overfit), or Venn-Abers. Always calibrate on held-out data, and — the 2026 nuance — verify it helped, because a recent benchmark found Platt and isotonic can degrade proper-scoring performance for strong tabular models. It matters whenever the probability itself is used: expected value, pricing, ranking by risk, cost-based thresholds.

6. Which models are typically miscalibrated, and in which direction?

Well-regularized logistic regression is usually close to calibrated (log loss is a proper scoring rule and it optimises exactly that). Random Forests are under-confident at the extremes — averaging many trees pulls predictions away from 0 and 1. Boosted trees are typically over-confident. Naive Bayes is wildly overconfident because it double-counts correlated evidence. SVMs produce no probabilities at all natively. Rule of thumb: check, never assume.

7. ⭐ How do you choose a decision threshold?

Not 0.5. Three legitimate sources: (1) a cost matrix — pick the t minimising expected cost, which for costs c_FP and c_FN is around c_FP/(c_FP+c_FN) for a calibrated model; (2) an operating constraint — “analysts can review 200 cases a day” sets the alert volume and therefore t; (3) a guarantee — the lowest t meeting a contractual precision floor. Tune it on a validation set (TunedThresholdClassifierCV), never on test, and consider abstention for low-confidence cases.

8. ⭐ How do you handle class imbalance? Give me your order of operations.

(0) Characterise it — 1:10 or 1:10,000? separated or overlapping? (1) Tune the decision threshold on a strong baseline: cheapest experiment, no new failure modes, often sufficient. (2) Class weights / scale_pos_weight: no synthetic rows, no leakage risk — but it decalibrates, so recalibrate if you use the probabilities. (3) Only then resampling — SMOTE, under-sampling, Tomek links, BalancedRandomForest/EasyEnsemble — always inside the pipeline. Throughout: PR-AUC, per-class recall and cost-aligned F-β, never accuracy.

9. What's wrong with applying SMOTE before the train-test split? And when is SMOTE actively harmful?

Before the split it leaks: synthetic minority points are interpolated from real rows, and some of those real rows end up in validation — so validation rows are partly reconstructions of training data. You get an excellent CV score and a production collapse. Actively harmful when classes overlap or labels are noisy: interpolating between minority points that sit in the majority region manufactures ambiguous synthetic examples right on the boundary, which inflates false positives. On clean tabular data, inverse-frequency weighting gives near-identical results and is simpler and leak-proof.

10. ⭐ Explain k-fold CV and when stratification is necessary.

Partition into k folds; for each, train on k−1 and evaluate on the held-out one; average the k scores and report the standard deviation too. Every row is tested exactly once, which gives a lower-variance estimate than a single hold-out. Stratify whenever the target is imbalanced or you have few rows — otherwise a fold can contain very few (or zero) positives, which makes both training and the metric unstable. Add: stratification is orthogonal to grouping and to time, so it does not protect you from those (§1.15).

11. What is nested cross-validation and why does it exist?

Tuning hyperparameters on the same CV you report gives an optimistically biased estimate — the reported score is the maximum over many noisy estimates, so it is inflated even if all settings were equally good. Nested CV puts hyperparameter selection in an inner loop run separately inside each outer training fold, so no outer test fold ever influences the selection. Cost: k_outer × k_inner fits. It estimates the performance of the whole procedure including tuning, not of one model. Pragmatic alternative: a single held-out test set you touch exactly once.

12. Your model is 0.4% better. Is it better?

Three checks. Statistical: compare across the same folds (paired), give a bootstrap CI on the difference, and use a paired permutation test or the corrected resampled t-test — naive t-tests over folds are anti-conservative because training sets overlap. Selection: if this is the best of 40 variants, some of that 0.4% is luck. Practical: what does it cost in latency, complexity, retraining and explainability? A 0.4% AUC gain that adds a service dependency is usually not worth shipping — and saying so is the senior answer.

Part 5 · features, interpretability and fairness
1. Walk me through feature engineering for a transaction fraud model.

Organise it as entity × window × statistic. Transaction-level: amount, hour (cyclically encoded), channel, merchant category, is-new-country. Card/customer aggregates over 1h/24h/7d/30d: count, sum, max, std, distinct merchants, distinct countries, velocity ratios. Deviation features: amount ÷ 30-day median, geo-distance ÷ elapsed time since the last transaction. Merchant-side: cross-fitted chargeback rate, relationship age. Device/graph: cards seen on this device. Then the constraints that make it a real answer: every feature must be computable inside the ~150 ms budget and must be point-in-time correct, and aggregates must never be computed over the full history at training time.

2. How do you handle high-cardinality categoricals?

In rough order: native handling (CatBoost's ordered target statistics, LightGBM categorical splits, HistGradientBoosting's categorical_features) — least to go wrong. Cross-fitted target encoding with smoothing toward the global mean and an explicit unknown bucket. Frequency/count encoding as a cheap partial signal. Hashing when the vocabulary is unbounded or streaming. Grouping by a business hierarchy (merchant → merchant category) which is often better than any statistical trick. One-hot is the thing you avoid here — it creates thousands of sparse, weak columns.

3. How do you encode a datetime? Why cyclical encoding?

Extract components (hour, day-of-week, day-of-month, month, week-of-year), add domain flags (holiday, business day, days-to-next-holiday), and add time-since-last-event recency features. Use cyclical encoding — sin(2πh/24) and cos(2πh/24) — because on a raw integer scale 23 and 0 are the furthest apart values, when in reality they are one hour apart; the sin/cos pair puts them adjacent on a circle. Two traps: be explicit about timezone/DST, and don't feed the raw absolute timestamp as a feature, or the model will memorise a period rather than a pattern.

4. Filter vs wrapper vs embedded feature selection.

Filter scores each feature independently of any model (correlation, chi², ANOVA F, mutual information) — fast and scalable, but blind to interactions. Wrapper repeatedly refits a model on subsets (forward/backward, RFE/RFECV) — accounts for the actual model and interactions, but is expensive and can overfit the selection itself. Embedded gets selection as a by-product of fitting (L1, tree importances, boosting gain) — the practical default, but it inherits the model's biases. Whichever you use: it must be fitted inside the CV loop.

5. Why would you drop features that improve accuracy?

Because accuracy is not the only cost. Each feature carries serving latency (a fetch, a join), acquisition cost (a paid third-party call), drift and monitoring surface, leakage risk, maintenance burden, and sometimes regulatory exposure (a proxy for a protected attribute). A feature that adds 0.002 AUC and £0.30 per prediction is a bad trade. Being able to make that argument explicitly is a senior signal.

6. ⭐ What is a feature store and what problem does point-in-time correctness solve?

A feature store is a shared, versioned system for computing, storing and serving features so that training and inference use the same definitions. Point-in-time correctness is the hard part it exists for: when you build a training row for March, the feature values must be the values that existed in March, not today's. Joining today's lifetime_spend onto a March label leaks six months of the future into the row. The mechanism is an as-of join against a time-versioned feature history. The sibling problem it also solves is training/serving skew — one code path instead of two implementations that drift apart.

7. ⭐ Explain SHAP. What are its assumptions and failure modes?

SHAP assigns each feature the Shapley value of its contribution to a single prediction — averaged over all orderings in which features could be added — giving additive, locally faithful, consistent attributions that sum to the prediction minus a base value, and which aggregate into a global view. Failure modes: (1) KernelSHAP assumes feature independence when marginalising, so it evaluates the model on unrealistic combinations off the data manifold. (2) Path-dependent TreeSHAP avoids that by using the tree's own paths, but this changes the value function such that a feature with no effect on the prediction can receive non-zero attribution — breaking the dummy axiom. (3) With correlated features, credit is split in unintuitive ways. (4) A SHAP value is not a causal effect. Use it with correlation analysis and other diagnostics, not as an oracle.

8. PDP vs ICE vs ALE — what does each show?

PDP shows the average marginal effect of a feature, holding others at their observed values — but it averages over instances, so it hides heterogeneity, and it evaluates unrealistic combinations when features are correlated. ICE plots one curve per instance, revealing exactly the heterogeneity the PDP averages away — if half your customers respond positively and half negatively, the PDP is flat and the ICE plot shows two fans. ALE computes effects within small local windows of the feature and accumulates them, which avoids the unrealistic-combination problem, making it the correlation-robust alternative. Rule: look at ICE before trusting a PDP.

9. A regulator asks why an application was declined. What do you give them?

Not a SHAP beeswarm. An adverse-action notice: a small number of specific, plain-language, actionable reasons, derived consistently and reproducibly, tied to a recorded model version and input snapshot. In practice that means: a locally faithful attribution method to rank reasons, translated into standardised reason codes, ideally paired with a counterfactual (“had utilisation been under 40%, this would have been approved”), plus the audit trail — model version, feature values at decision time, the policy thresholds applied. Mention that this requirement is often why the model is a logistic regression or a monotonically-constrained GBM in the first place.

10. Define two fairness metrics and explain why you can't satisfy all of them.

Demographic parity: equal positive-prediction rates across groups. Equal opportunity: equal true-positive rates among those who actually qualify. Equalised odds: equal TPR and FPR. Predictive parity: equal precision — i.e. a score means the same thing in every group. The impossibility result: when base rates genuinely differ between groups, calibration within groups and equalised odds cannot both hold except in degenerate cases. So “make it fair” is under-specified — someone must choose which criterion, and that is a policy decision. Add: removing the protected attribute doesn't help because proxies encode it, so you measure per subgroup on the deployed decision and document it in a model card.

⭐ “Is classical ML dead?”

No — and the reason is worth saying carefully. Most of the world's high-value prediction problems are still tabular, latency-bound, regulated and small-data: credit, insurance, fraud, demand, pricing, clinical risk. What changed is that the baseline moved. Strong AutoML and tabular foundation models mean that getting a competent model is no longer where the difficulty is — so the job has shifted toward framing the problem, owning the data, designing the evaluation and running the deployment. 6.12 Interview focus for Part 6

Twelve questions covering production and the frontier — where senior interviews spend most of their time.

Part 6 · production, uncertainty, causality
1. Walk me through deploying a model end to end.

Frame the decision and the metric → build the offline pipeline with point-in-time-correct features → validate with a split that mimics production → pick a threshold from costs or capacity → package the whole pipeline (not just the estimator) and register it with a version and lineage → deploy in shadow to check latency and stability → canary at a few percent with guardrails → A/B test against the incumbent → full rollout with a rollback path → monitor data quality, drift, prediction distribution and (when labels arrive) performance → define retraining triggers and write the runbook. The two things that separate a good answer: the same feature code serves training and inference, and there is a rollback path and an on-call playbook.

2. Batch vs real-time serving — how do you decide?

One question: do the features exist before the request arrives? If yes, precompute on a schedule and write to a key-value store — prediction becomes an O(1) lookup, which is cheaper, more stable and easier to monitor. If a feature only exists at request time (this transaction's amount, this session's behaviour), you need real-time. Secondary considerations: how stale can a prediction be, how expensive is the model, and what is the p99 budget. Most real systems are hybrid — precomputed aggregates joined with a few live features.

3. ⭐ How do you detect drift when labels arrive 60 days late?

Use proxies, layered. Input side: per-feature PSI (<0.1 stable, 0.1–0.2 moderate, >0.2 significant), KS or chi-square, Jensen-Shannon and Wasserstein as smoother alternatives to KL on noisy production data — weighted by feature importance. Output side: the prediction distribution and mean predicted probability, which move immediately. Behavioural: override rate, abstention rate, analyst agreement, downstream conversion. Partial labels: any subset that resolves quickly. Data quality: schema, nulls, ranges, cardinality — which in practice catches more incidents than drift statistics do. And the discipline: confirm the change actually degrades a metric before retraining — alert fatigue kills monitoring programmes.

4. Data drift vs concept drift — how do the responses differ?

Data/covariate drift is P(x) moving while P(y|x) holds — a new customer segment, a new channel. The model may still be valid; you might reweight, retrain on recent data, or do nothing. Concept drift is P(y|x) itself changing — fraudsters adapt, a recession redefines risk. Nothing but retraining helps, and you may need to reconsider features and even the target definition. The practical difference: covariate drift is detectable without labels; concept drift generally is not, which is why the proxy stack above exists.

5. When do you retrain? What triggers it?

Three triggers, in preference order: performance-triggered (best, when labels arrive in time), drift-triggered (the proxy, with a confirmation step so you don't retrain on noise), and scheduled (the boring default that mostly works). The trade-off is staleness vs churn: retraining constantly makes predictions change under users for no visible reason and makes incidents unreproducible. Always run champion/challenger and gate the retrain behind a golden-set regression suite so it cannot silently regress a known case.

6. What is training/serving skew and how do you prevent it?

The training pipeline and the serving path compute the same feature differently — a different window, a different null handling, a different timezone, a different rounding — so the model sees a distribution it was never trained on, and no alert fires because both systems are individually working. Prevent it architecturally: one shared code path (a feature store, a shared library, or a batch job that writes what the API reads), plus a skew check that logs serving features and compares their distribution to training. Related and worse: point-in-time incorrectness, where training features use values that did not exist at prediction time.

7. Your model was great offline and users are complaining. Debug it.

Work down a fixed list, cheapest first. (1) Leakage — was the offline number ever real? Re-validate with a time split. (2) Split mismatch — did validation mimic production (groups, time, purge)? (3) Slices — is the aggregate fine but a segment broken (§4.4)? (4) Skew — compare the live feature distribution against training. (5) Drift — has the input or the concept moved since training? (6) Threshold — is 0.5 still deployed while the base rate has changed? (7) Label quality — were the offline labels defined the same way as the outcome users care about? (8) The metric itself — does the offline metric actually correlate with the user experience? Naming this as an ordered checklist is worth more than any single answer.

8. What is the feedback-loop problem in a fraud or credit model?

The model's decisions determine the data it will be trained on next. A fraud model never observes whether the transactions it blocked were really fraud; a credit model never sees how rejected applicants would have performed (the reject inference problem). Training data becomes systematically censored, the model becomes confident about a shrinking slice of the world, and performance degrades in a way that offline evaluation cannot see. Mitigations: hold out a small exploration/control slice that bypasses the model, use propensity weighting on the observed data, and treat the blocked population explicitly rather than dropping it.

9. ⭐ GBDT or deep learning for tabular data? Has that changed?

“Historically GBDTs, decisively — faster to train, less tuning, native missing-value and categorical handling, better under about a million rows. As of 2026 that is genuinely contested for small-to-medium data by tabular foundation models: TabPFN-2.5 reported a 100% win rate against default XGBoost on ≤10k rows, and TabPFN-3 scales to a million training rows and reports that a single forward pass beats 8-hour-tuned GBDT baselines on TabArena, outperforming AutoGluon 1.5 while roughly 10× faster. The caveats matter: these are pretrained purely on synthetic data, benchmarks have selection effects, and the deployment, licensing and interpretability stories are less mature. So I'd benchmark both on my own data, and still default to a GBDT where I need monotonic constraints, interpretability, cheap retraining and a well-understood ops story.”

10. What is conformal prediction and what guarantee does it give?

A distribution-free, model-agnostic wrapper that turns point predictions into prediction sets or intervals with a finite-sample coverage guarantee. Split conformal is a few lines: fit on a training split, compute a nonconformity score (e.g. absolute residual) on a disjoint calibration split, take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile as q̂, and emit ŷ ± q̂. The guarantee is marginal coverage ≥ 1−α under exchangeability of calibration and test data — which is the assumption that breaks under drift and for time series, hence adaptive and weighted variants. Uses: selective prediction, deferral to a human, safe deployment. And note it says nothing about conditional coverage — the interval may be too wide for easy cases and too narrow for hard ones unless you use a normalised score.

11. ⭐ Your churn model is accurate. The business asks who to give a discount to. What's wrong with using it?

The churn model answers “who will leave?”, but the decision needs “who will stay because we acted?” — those are different quantities. The highest churn scores are dominated by lost causes who leave regardless, and the discount budget also leaks to sure things who would have stayed. Worse, some customers are sleeping dogs for whom the offer is a reminder to cancel, so the treatment has negative effect. You need uplift / CATE estimation — ideally from a randomised holdout, otherwise T/S/X-learners or causal forests with a DML-style approach — evaluated with Qini and uplift curves rather than AUC, because there is no per-row counterfactual label to be right about. The general principle: a predictive model is invalid under intervention.

12. What is double machine learning for?

Estimating a causal effect when you want to use flexible ML for the messy parts without destroying your inference. You have a treatment, an outcome and high-dimensional confounders. DML fits two nuisance models with any ML method — outcome given confounders, and treatment given confounders — takes the residuals of each, and regresses one residual on the other (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell). Two ingredients make it work: Neyman orthogonality, so small errors in the nuisance estimates don't bias the effect to first order, and cross-fitting, so a row's nuisance predictions come from a model that never saw it — otherwise regularization bias and overfitting contaminate the estimate. The payoff is a √n-consistent effect estimate with valid confidence intervals while still using gradient boosting for the nuisances. EconML implements it.

7.1 Foundations & framing

Seven questions. These decide whether the rest of the interview is a conversation between peers.

Q1–7 · foundations
1. What is machine learning, and when would you not use it?

Learning a function from examples plus a loss, instead of specifying rules. Don't use it when: the rule is stable and writable (ship the rule — auditable and free); you have no labels and no path to them; an unexplainable error is unacceptable; or the data is too small for the signal you want. Volunteering the four branches unprompted is a strong senior signal.

2. Supervised vs unsupervised vs reinforcement learning — with a real example of each.

Supervised: features + labels → a mapping. Credit default from application data. Unsupervised: features only → structure. Customer segmentation, anomaly detection. Reinforcement: an environment and a reward → a policy. Bid optimisation, inventory control. Worth adding self-supervised (a pretext task creates the labels — the basis of all pretraining) and semi-supervised (few labels, much unlabelled data — the realistic fraud case).

3. ⭐ How do you choose the target variable and prediction horizon for a churn model?

Work backwards from the decision. Ask what action the model triggers and how long that action needs — if retention needs 30 days to run a campaign, the target is “churns within 30 days”, not “ever churns”. Then define churn concretely (cancellation? 90 days inactive? downgrade?) and check the base rate under each definition. Pin the unit of prediction (customer? customer-month?) because it determines the split. Add a data-latency gap so every feature is knowable at prediction time. Finish with the framing caveat: if the decision is who gets a retention offer, churn is the wrong target and you want uplift (§6.9).

4. Parametric vs non-parametric models — and why does it matter?

Parametric models have a fixed number of parameters regardless of data size (linear/logistic regression, Naive Bayes, GLMs): strong assumptions, fast, data-efficient, and they can't grow to fit more structure. Non-parametric models grow with the data (kNN, trees, kernel SVM, RF): fewer assumptions, more flexible, more data-hungry, and usually higher variance. It matters because it tells you which failure to expect — a parametric model underfits, a non-parametric one overfits.

5. Discriminative vs generative models — an example and a trade-off.

Discriminative models P(y|x) directly (logistic regression, SVM, trees, GBMs); generative models the joint P(x,y) and derives the posterior via Bayes (Naive Bayes, LDA/QDA, GMM, HMM). Generative imposes more structure so it needs less data, handles missing features naturally and can sample; discriminative usually wins on accuracy with enough data because all its capacity goes into the boundary. Same axis as HMM vs CRF.

6. What baselines would you report before any model?

Majority class or mean; previous value / seasonal-naive for time series; the existing business rule (the real bar); a random-but-calibrated predictor to anchor log loss; and a strong simple model — logistic regression or a small GBM on obvious features, which usually reaches 90–95% of the final performance. And the sentence that matters: if the complex model doesn't beat these by enough to justify its cost and risk, that is the finding.

7. What is the offline/online metric gap and how do you manage it?

Your offline metric is a proxy for a business outcome, and the mapping is an assumption. A better AUC can lose an A/B test — because the improvement was in a region users never see, because the score distribution shifted under a fixed threshold, or because the business metric depends on something you didn't optimise. Manage it by: stating the assumed link explicitly, checking it against historical launches, shipping through shadow → canary → A/B, and keeping guardrail metrics.

7.2 Bias, variance & generalization

Six questions. The decomposition is the single most-asked piece of theory in the subject.

Q8–13 · generalization
8. ⭐ Explain the bias-variance trade-off. Write the decomposition.

E[(y − f̂(x))²] = Bias[f̂]² + Var[f̂] + σ². Bias is error from wrong assumptions — the model can't represent the truth. Variance is sensitivity to the particular training sample. σ² is irreducible noise. What moves each: model complexity, regularization strength, feature count, tree depth, k in kNN, ensemble size. The one-liner: bagging reduces variance, boosting reduces bias. The subtlety that earns credit: the decomposition is exact only for squared loss and merely heuristic for log loss or 0-1 loss.

9. Overfitting vs underfitting — how do you diagnose which one you have?

Two numbers. Train error high and validation error close to it ⇒ high bias / underfitting: add capacity, add features, reduce regularization. Train error low and validation error far above ⇒ high variance / overfitting: more data, more regularization, less capacity, bagging. Then confirm with a learning curve, which also tells you whether more data will help.

10. Will more data help? How do you know?

Plot a learning curve — training and validation error against training-set size. If both have converged above your target, you have a bias problem and more data will not help; change the model or the features. If a substantial gap is still closing, more data will help. The compact statement: more data fixes variance, not bias.

11. What is the curse of dimensionality and which models suffer most?

As dimensions grow, volume grows exponentially while your sample doesn't, so points spread to the shell of the space and pairwise distances concentrate — nearest and farthest neighbours become nearly equidistant, and “local” stops meaning anything. Worst affected: kNN, k-means, DBSCAN, RBF-SVM and anything else built on a distance. Barely affected: trees and tree ensembles, which split one feature at a time. Secondary effect: more features = more capacity to memorise = more variance.

12. What is the “no free lunch” theorem and what does it imply practically?

Averaged over all possible problems, no algorithm outperforms any other — superiority comes from assumptions matching the problem. Practically: there is no universally best model, which is why benchmarking is a step rather than a formality, and why “always use XGBoost” is a heuristic about the distribution of real tabular problems rather than a theorem.

13. What is double descent?

Beyond the classical U-curve, test error can rise to a peak at the interpolation threshold (roughly where parameters ≈ samples) and then fall again as capacity grows further. Well documented in over-parameterised models. Know it exists so the U-curve isn't the only story you can tell — while noting that on tabular data with regularized GBMs you are still firmly in the classical regime.

7.3 Regularization

Five questions. L1 vs L2 has a geometric half, a probabilistic half and a practical half — give all three.

Q14–18 · regularization
14. ⭐ L1 vs L2 — what does each do, and why does L1 produce exact zeros?

L2/Ridge shrinks coefficients smoothly, handles multicollinearity by re-conditioning XᵀX + αI, and never reaches exactly zero. L1/Lasso drives coefficients to exactly zero, so it performs feature selection. Geometric: the L1 constraint region is a diamond whose corners lie on the axes, and elliptical loss contours typically first touch it at a corner — where a coordinate is exactly zero; the L2 ball is smooth, so contact is at a generic point. Probabilistic: Ridge is MAP with a Gaussian prior, Lasso with a Laplace prior whose density spikes at zero. Practical: Lasso is unstable with correlated features (picks one arbitrarily); Elastic Net fixes that.

15. Why must you standardise features before regularizing?

The penalty is on coefficient magnitude, and magnitude depends on the feature's units. Income in pounds and age in years produce coefficients orders of magnitude apart, so the penalty falls almost entirely on one feature — you would be regularizing by choice of measurement unit. Standardise inside the pipeline, and leave the intercept unpenalised.

16. When would you use Elastic Net over Lasso?

With groups of correlated features: Lasso picks one essentially arbitrarily and zeroes the rest, and picks a different one on a slightly different sample — fine for prediction, terrible for stability and interpretation. Elastic Net's L2 component makes correlated coefficients shrink together so the group survives or dies together. Also when p > n: Lasso can select at most n features; Elastic Net has no such cap.

17. Show that Ridge is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior.

With Gaussian noise, the likelihood is p(y|X,w) ∝ exp(−‖y−Xw‖²/2σ²). Put a Gaussian prior p(w) ∝ exp(−‖w‖²/2τ²). The posterior is proportional to their product; taking −log gives ‖y−Xw‖²/2σ² + ‖w‖²/2τ², and maximising the posterior means minimising that — which is exactly Ridge with α = σ²/τ². Swap the Gaussian prior for a Laplace prior exp(−‖w‖₁/b) and the penalty becomes ‖w‖₁: Lasso. A tighter prior (small τ) means stronger regularization.

18. Name three regularization techniques that aren't a penalty term.

Structural: tree depth, min_samples_leaf, num_leaves, max features — limiting the hypothesis space directly. Stochastic: row and column subsampling, bagging, dropout — injecting noise and decorrelating. Implicit: early stopping, which in boosting is approximately equivalent to shrinkage; and more data or augmentation, which raises the bar for memorisation.

7.4 Linear models

Six questions. Logistic regression's coefficient interpretation is the one people fumble.

Q19–24 · linear & GLM
19. State the assumptions of linear regression and what breaks when each fails.

Linearity in the parameters — fails ⇒ biased predictions, curvature in the residual plot; fix with splines or a different model. Independent errors — fails with time or clustered data ⇒ standard errors wrong. Homoscedasticity — fails ⇒ inefficient estimates, wrong CIs; log the target, use a GLM, or robust SEs. Normal residuals — needed only for inference, not prediction. No perfect multicollinearity — fails ⇒ no unique solution; near-failure inflates coefficient variance.

20. Why can't you use MSE as the loss for logistic regression?

Composed with a sigmoid, squared error is non-convex in the parameters, so gradient descent has no global guarantee. And log loss is the negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model — the MLE and a proper scoring rule — so it has a principled justification MSE lacks, and it punishes confidently-wrong predictions far more sharply.

21. Interpret a logistic regression coefficient. What is an odds ratio?

A one-unit increase in x raises the log-odds by β, i.e. multiplies the odds by exp(β) — the odds ratio. β = 0.4 ⇒ odds ×1.49. The effect on the probability is not constant: maximal near p = 0.5 (dp/dz = p(1−p)) and vanishing in the tails. Standardise if you want to compare magnitudes, and remember sklearn's C is the inverse penalty strength.

22. What is multicollinearity, how do you detect it, and does it hurt predictions or interpretation?

Features carrying near-identical information, making XᵀX ill-conditioned and the loss surface a flat valley. Detect with a correlation matrix, condition number, or VIF = 1/(1−R²ⱼ) above ~5–10. It inflates coefficient variance — signs flip, magnitudes swing between refits — so it damages interpretation and stability, usually not predictive accuracy. Fix by dropping, combining, using PCA, or using Ridge.

23. Why does R² always increase when you add a feature? What do you use instead?

An extra column can only enlarge the space the fit is chosen from, so the residual sum of squares cannot rise — and least squares will exploit chance correlation to do slightly better even with pure noise. Use adjusted R², which penalises p, or a held-out score. Note test R² is a different statistic and can be negative if you do worse than predicting the mean.

24. When would you reach for a GLM — Poisson or Tweedie — instead of linear regression?

When you know the target's distribution and it isn't Gaussian. Poisson (log link) for counts; negative binomial when counts are over-dispersed; Gamma for positive right-skewed amounts like claim severity; Tweedie for zero-inflated positive targets like insurance pure premium. You get non-negative predictions, a correct variance function and defensible coefficients — and all three objectives exist in XGBoost and LightGBM if you'd rather use trees.

7.5 Trees & ensembles

Thirteen questions — the largest block, because this is what wins on tabular data.

Q25–37 · trees, bagging, boosting
25. How does a decision tree choose a split? Gini vs entropy — does it matter?

Greedily: at each node it scans every feature and every candidate threshold, computes the impurity decrease H(parent) − Σ(nᵢ/n)H(childᵢ), and takes the maximum — then recurses, never revisiting earlier choices. Gini is 1 − Σpᵢ², entropy is −Σpᵢlog₂pᵢ. In practice they choose nearly the same splits; Gini is cheaper (no logarithm) and is the default. The honest answer is “it doesn't matter — I'd spend the budget on depth and min_samples_leaf.” For regression the criterion is variance/MSE reduction.

26. Why is a single deep tree high-variance?

Because splits are chosen greedily and hierarchically: moving a few rows can flip the root split, and everything below it changes. A fully-grown tree also drives training error to near zero by isolating individual points, so it is fitting noise. That instability is precisely the property bagging exploits — average many high-variance, low-bias trees and the variance falls while the bias stays put.

27. ⭐ Bagging vs boosting — what does each reduce, and why?

Bagging: parallel, high-variance low-bias learners on bootstrap samples, averaged. Ensemble variance is roughly ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/M, so averaging kills the second term — it reduces variance, and ρ (the correlation between members) is the ceiling on how much. Boosting: sequential weak high-bias learners, each fitted to the current ensemble's errors, so systematic error shrinks — it reduces bias, and can raise variance, which is why it overfits with too many rounds and needs early stopping.

28. How does Random Forest differ from plain bagging? What is OOB error?

RF adds random feature subsetting at each split (max_features, default √p for classification). Without it, a dominant feature is picked at the root of every tree and the trees are highly correlated — and correlation caps the benefit of averaging. OOB error: each bootstrap omits about (1−1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 36.8% of rows, so each row can be scored by the trees that never saw it, giving free validation without a holdout.

29. Can you overfit a Random Forest by adding trees? Can you overfit a GBM?

RF: adding trees does not overfit — the ensemble converges rather than degrading, so n_estimators is a compute knob, not a regularization knob. But a forest of deep unpruned trees on noisy data can overfit by memorising label noise; control that with min_samples_leaf and max_features. GBM: yes, readily — each round fits the remaining error including noise. Use a validation set with early stopping, plus shrinkage and subsampling.

30. ⭐ Explain gradient boosting. What exactly is each new tree fitted to?

Start from a constant. At each round compute the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions — the pseudo-residuals — fit a shallow tree to those, and add it scaled by the learning rate: Fₘ = Fₘ₋₁ + ν·hₘ. For squared loss the negative gradient is the ordinary residual, which is why textbooks say “fit to the residuals”; saying negative gradient is what generalises it to log loss, Poisson, quantile and everything else. AdaBoost is the special case with exponential loss.

31. XGBoost vs LightGBM vs CatBoost — the real differences, and how do you choose?

XGBoost: second-order (Newton) using gradients and Hessians, explicit λ/α/γ in the objective, sparsity-aware learned default direction for missing values, level-wise growth by default. LightGBM: leaf-wise growth plus GOSS (keep large-gradient rows, subsample the rest) and EFB (bundle mutually exclusive sparse features) — fastest at scale, native categorical splits. CatBoost: ordered boosting and ordered target statistics to remove the target leakage in naive encoding, symmetric oblivious trees for very fast inference, strong defaults. Choose: many high-cardinality categoricals + little tuning time ⇒ CatBoost; very large data + tight budget ⇒ LightGBM; maximum control and ecosystem ⇒ XGBoost. Then add that once tuned they're usually within noise of each other.

32. What is leaf-wise vs level-wise growth, and what's the risk of leaf-wise?

Level-wise expands every node at a depth before descending — balanced trees, predictable cost, some splits that gain little. Leaf-wise always expands the leaf with the largest loss reduction, reaching a lower loss with fewer leaves. The risk: it grows very deep down one branch and overfits small or noisy data. Control with num_leaves and min_data_in_leaf — and note that setting only max_depth while leaving num_leaves at default is a classic misconfiguration.

33. What problem does CatBoost's ordered boosting solve?

Prediction shift from target leakage in naive target statistics: if a row's encoding and residual use its own label, training residuals are systematically biased and the model is over-optimistic on data it has seen. Ordered boosting computes each row's statistics and residuals only from rows preceding it in a random permutation (with several permutations so early rows aren't starved) — the algorithmic version of out-of-fold target encoding.

34. How do you tune a GBM? Give me your order of operations.

(1) Fix a modest learning rate (0.05–0.1) and let early stopping pick n_estimators. (2) Tree complexity: max_depth / num_leaves, min_child_weight / min_data_in_leaf. (3) Sampling: subsample, colsample_bytree. (4) Regularization: lambda, alpha, gamma. (5) Lower the learning rate and refit with more trees. Use random or TPE search over log-uniform ranges, inside the CV loop. Giving an order rather than a parameter list is what makes this sound like experience.

35. What are monotonic constraints and when have you needed them?

They force the model's output to be non-decreasing (or non-increasing) in a given feature, enforced at split time so no split can violate the direction. You need them when a business or regulatory rule demands it — risk must not fall as debt rises; premium must not fall as claim history worsens — and they also act as a strong regularizer and make the model far easier to defend. Available in XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost; interaction constraints are the related tool for “these features may not interact”.

36. How does stacking work, and what's the leakage trap in it?

Base models' predictions become features for a meta-learner. The trap: those meta-features must be out-of-fold — each row's base prediction must come from a model that never saw it. Train the base models on all the data and predict that same data and the meta-learner learns to trust whichever base model overfits hardest. Keep the meta-learner simple (regularized linear), prize diversity over individual strength, and be honest that stacking rarely survives production review on latency and maintenance grounds.

37. ⭐ Why is impurity-based feature importance misleading? What do you use instead?

Mean decrease in impurity is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features — more candidate split points means more chances to reduce impurity by luck — and it is computed on training data, so it rewards overfitting. A random continuous ID-like column will rank high. Use permutation importance on held-out data, which measures the real metric drop when a feature is shuffled. Then state the residual caveat: with correlated features, permuting one leaves its information available through the other, so both look unimportant while jointly being essential — and SHAP has its own version of that problem.

7.6 Other models

Five questions on kNN, Naive Bayes, SVM and discriminant analysis.

Q38–42 · the rest of the zoo
38. How does kNN work, and why is scaling mandatory?

Store the training set; to predict, find the k nearest rows by some distance and take their vote or mean. Because the prediction is a distance computation, a feature in larger units dominates it entirely — income in pounds swamps age in years, so you are effectively doing 1-D nearest neighbour on income. k is a pure bias-variance dial: k=1 gives zero training error and maximum variance; k=n is the majority-class baseline. Training is free, inference is O(n·p) per query — usually disqualifying in production.

39. Why does Naive Bayes work despite a false independence assumption?

Classification needs the correct argmax, not correct probabilities. Correlated features are counted as independent evidence, which multiplies their effect and drives the posterior toward 0 or 1 — but the class ordering usually survives. So it is badly calibrated and overconfident, and its predict_proba should never be used as a probability without calibration. Add Laplace smoothing so an unseen pair doesn't zero the product, and compute in log space.

40. Explain the SVM margin, the role of C, and the kernel trick.

The SVM maximises the margin 2/‖w‖ to the nearest points — the support vectors, the only points that define the solution. The soft margin allows violations via slack; C trades margin width against violations (large C ⇒ narrow margin, low bias, high variance). The kernel trick: the dual objective touches the data only through inner products, so substituting K(xᵢ,xⱼ) gives you the geometry of a high-dimensional space — infinite-dimensional for RBF — without ever computing φ(x). Costs: O(n²)–O(n³) training, mandatory scaling, no native probabilities.

41. LDA vs PCA — what's the difference in objective?

PCA is unsupervised and maximises total variance; it never sees the labels, so it can discard exactly the direction that separates classes. LDA is supervised and maximises between-class scatter relative to within-class scatter, giving at most k−1 components. Both are linear projections; only one is trying to help your classifier. LDA-the-classifier assumes a shared covariance (linear boundary); QDA gives each class its own (quadratic boundary, more parameters, more data needed).

42. When would you still choose an SVM in 2026?

Small-to-medium data with a clear margin; high-dimensional sparse features where a linear SVM is excellent and very fast (LinearSVC on text); and cases where you want a maximum-margin rule rather than a probability. Otherwise gradient boosting wins on tabular data, trains faster, needs no scaling and handles mixed types. Note also that probability=True runs an internal 5-fold Platt calibration — slow, and often still poorly calibrated.

7.7 Unsupervised

Six questions. The t-SNE trap is the one to volunteer unprompted.

Q43–48 · clustering, PCA, anomalies
43. How does k-means work? How do you choose k? What are its assumptions?

Lloyd's algorithm: seed centroids (k-means++ spreads them out), assign each point to its nearest centroid, recompute centroids as means, repeat until assignments stabilise. It minimises inertia, which is non-convex — so the seed changes the answer, hence n_init. Assumptions: spherical, roughly equal-variance clusters and a meaningful Euclidean distance ⇒ scale-sensitive and outlier-sensitive. Choosing k: elbow, silhouette, gap statistic, Davies-Bouldin — and honestly, k is usually a business constraint.

44. k-means vs DBSCAN vs hierarchical — pick one for a given problem.

k-means for large data with roughly spherical, similarly-sized clusters and a k you can justify. DBSCAN when clusters are arbitrarily shaped, you don't know k, and you want noise labelled as noise rather than forced into a cluster — but it struggles with varying density, which is what HDBSCAN fixes (and it is the sensible modern default for exploration). Hierarchical/Ward when you want a dendrogram you can cut at any level and n is small enough for O(n²). Add GMM/EM when you want soft assignments and elliptical clusters.

45. ⭐ Explain PCA. Why must you scale first? How do you choose the number of components?

PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance — the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, equivalently the right singular vectors of the centred data matrix, with eigenvalues giving explained variance. Scaling: variance carries units, so an unscaled feature in small units dominates PC1 by measurement convention rather than information. Components: cumulative explained-variance threshold, the scree elbow, or (best) cross-validated downstream performance. Caveat: components are mixtures of every feature, so interpretability is limited.

46. PCA vs t-SNE vs UMAP — and what's the trap with t-SNE plots?

PCA is linear, deterministic, reversible and safe as preprocessing. t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear, stochastic visualisation tools. The trap: in a t-SNE plot, distances between clusters and cluster sizes are meaningless — it preserves local neighbourhoods only, and the layout depends heavily on perplexity. UMAP preserves more global structure and is faster, but is still stochastic. Never say “these clusters are similar because they're close on the plot.”

47. How do you evaluate a clustering with no labels?

Internal indices: silhouette (b−a)/max(a,b), Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz, inertia — all of which reward the geometry the algorithm optimised, so they favour k-means-shaped clusters. Stability: re-run on bootstrap samples and measure how consistent assignments are. External validation against a proxy label if one exists (ARI, NMI, homogeneity/completeness). And the honest answer: usefulness — do the segments differ on something the business acts on, and can a human name them?

48. How would you detect anomalies with no labels?

Isolation Forest as the default (anomalies need fewer random splits to isolate — scales well, few assumptions), LOF for local density anomalies, Mahalanobis/z-score for a Gaussian baseline, One-Class SVM, or autoencoder reconstruction error for high-dimensional structured data. Three points to raise: contamination is a prior you are asserting and it directly sets your alert volume; novelty vs outlier detection are different settings; and if you have labels, use a classifier — anomaly detection is what you do when you can't frame it as imbalanced classification.

7.8 Validation

Seven questions. Getting the split wrong invalidates every other number you report.

Q49–55 · CV and model selection
49. ⭐ Explain k-fold cross-validation. When is stratification necessary?

Partition into k folds; train on k−1 and evaluate on the held-out one, k times; report mean ± standard deviation. Every row is tested exactly once, which gives a lower-variance estimate than a single hold-out. Stratify when the target is imbalanced or n is small, so no fold ends up with too few positives. Choosing k is itself a trade-off: small k biases the estimate pessimistically (less training data per fold), large k raises variance through overlapping training sets — 5 or 10 is the standard compromise, and LOO is high-variance rather than “most accurate”.

50. When is a random split wrong? Give three cases.

(1) Grouped rows — many transactions per customer; a random split lets the model recognise the customer, so use GroupKFold. (2) Temporal data — a random split trains on the future to predict the past and hides drift; use TimeSeriesSplit with a gap. (3) Near-duplicates — deduplicate before splitting or copies straddle the boundary. The governing rule: your split must simulate how the model will be used.

51. What is nested cross-validation and why does it exist?

Tuning on the same CV you report is optimistically biased — the reported score is the maximum over many noisy estimates, so it is inflated even if all settings were equally good. Nested CV runs hyperparameter selection in an inner loop inside each outer training fold, so no outer test fold influences selection. Cost: k_outer × k_inner fits. It estimates the performance of the whole procedure including tuning, not of one model. Pragmatic alternative: a test set you touch exactly once.

52. How do you cross-validate time series data? What are purging and embargo?

Walk-forward / rolling-origin: train on everything up to time t, test on the window after it, then roll forward — expanding or sliding window. Purging removes training samples whose label horizon overlaps the test window (a label observed 30 days later has partly happened during the test period). Embargo additionally drops a buffer immediately after the test window before training resumes, so serial correlation and feature reporting latency can't leak across the boundary. Without both, you get an optimistic score and a production surprise.

53. Grid vs random vs Bayesian search — which and why?

Random is the sane default: with a fixed budget it probes far more distinct values of the parameters that actually matter (the Bergstra & Bengio result), and the advantage grows with dimensionality. Grid only for two or three parameters with genuinely few sensible values. Bayesian/TPE (Optuna) when each evaluation is expensive — noting it behaves like random search until its surrogate warms up. Successive halving / Hyperband when a cheap fidelity predicts the full one. And the real lesson: the search space matters more than the algorithm — log-uniform for learning rates and penalties, bounded by domain knowledge, tuned inside the CV loop.

54. Your model is 0.4% better. Is it better?

Three checks. Statistical: compare paired across the same folds, report a bootstrap CI on the difference, and use a paired permutation test or the corrected resampled t-test (naive t-tests over folds are anti-conservative because training sets overlap). Selection: if this is the best of 40 variants, part of that 0.4% is luck. Practical: what does it cost in latency, complexity, retraining and explainability? A 0.4% gain that adds a service dependency is usually not worth shipping.

55. How do you validate a model when rows are grouped by customer?

GroupKFold or StratifiedGroupKFold, so no customer's rows appear in two folds — otherwise you are measuring how well the model recognises customers it has already seen, not how it generalises to new ones. Deduplicate first. If there is also a time dimension, split by time and check that groups don't straddle the boundary. And be aware of the cost: grouped folds are less balanced, so report the fold-to-fold spread.

7.9 Metrics

Ten questions. This is the block that most often decides the outcome.

Q56–65 · metrics, thresholds, calibration
56. ⭐ Accuracy is 95% and the model is useless. Explain.

Ask for the base rate. If 95% of rows are negative, always predicting “negative” scores 95% and catches nothing — accuracy has become a majority-class detector. Report the confusion matrix at the deployed threshold, per-class recall and PR-AUC instead. Second failure: accuracy is blind to the cost asymmetry between a false positive and a false negative.

57. Precision vs recall — a business example where each dominates.

Precision dominates when acting is expensive or damaging: blocking a legitimate payment, sending an enforcement letter, opening a fraud case that costs an analyst 20 minutes. Recall dominates when missing is catastrophic: cancer screening, safety recalls, AML compliance, a security alert. The framing to use: precision is “what happens when we act”, recall is “what happens when we miss” — and the threshold (§4.9) is how you buy one with the other.

58. ⭐ ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC — when do you prefer which?

ROC-AUC plots TPR against FPR and summarises ranking quality well when classes are roughly balanced. Under imbalance it is structurally flattering: FPR divides by a huge negative count, so thousands of false positives barely move the x-axis. PR-AUC / average precision plots precision against recall, so it responds directly to false positives among your alerts, and its baseline is the base rate rather than 0.5. Prefer PR-AUC when positives are rare. And note that neither says anything about calibration.

59. What does ROC-AUC actually mean, probabilistically?

The probability that a randomly chosen positive scores above a randomly chosen negative — P(score(x⁺) > score(x⁻)), ties counted as a half. It equals the normalised Mann-Whitney U statistic. Hence it is threshold-free and invariant to any monotone transform of the scores — which is precisely why Platt scaling changes the probabilities and leaves AUC untouched.

60. Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report?

Micro pools TP/FP/FN across classes, so it is dominated by frequent classes — and in single-label multi-class it equals accuracy exactly. Macro averages per-class F1 with equal weight, so a rare class counts as much as a common one; report it when rare classes matter. Weighted averages by support and mostly reproduces micro. Report the one that matches the decision, and always say which.

61. RMSE vs MAE — what does each optimise?

Minimising squared error fits the conditional mean; minimising absolute error fits the conditional median. On a right-skewed target those are materially different numbers. RMSE penalises large errors quadratically (right when costs grow super-linearly) and is outlier-sensitive; MAE is robust and reports “typical error”. Choose from the decision: is a 10-unit error twice as bad as a 5-unit error, or four times?

62. When does MAPE break? What do you use instead?

Two failures: it is undefined and explodes as the actual approaches zero, and it is asymmetric — over-prediction is bounded at 100% error while under-prediction is unbounded, so optimising it biases forecasts low. Use sMAPE (better, still unstable near zero) or — the right answer for many series of different scales — MASE, which divides by the seasonal-naive error, is scale-free, and has the useful property that < 1 means you beat the naive baseline.

63. ⭐ What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?

Calibrated means: among cases predicted at p, a fraction p are actually positive. Measure with a reliability diagram, ECE Σ(nᵦ/n)|acc(b) − conf(b)|, and the Brier score. Fix with Platt scaling (a sigmoid on the log-odds; parametric, works with little data), isotonic regression (non-parametric, more flexible, needs more data, overfits on small calibration sets), or Venn-Abers. Always fit on held-out data. The 2026 nuance: verify it helped on a proper scoring rule — a recent benchmark found Platt and isotonic can degrade proper-scoring performance for strong tabular models.

64. Which models are typically miscalibrated, and in which direction?

Well-regularized logistic regression is usually close to calibrated — it optimises log loss, a proper scoring rule. Random Forests are under-confident at the extremes (averaging many trees pulls predictions away from 0 and 1). Boosted trees are typically over-confident. Naive Bayes is wildly overconfident because it double-counts correlated evidence. SVMs have no probabilities natively. Rule: check with a reliability diagram, never assume.

65. How do you choose a decision threshold?

Not 0.5 — that is a default, correct only when the classes are balanced and the two error costs are equal. Three legitimate sources: a cost matrix (minimise expected cost; for a calibrated model the optimum is near c_FP/(c_FP+c_FN)); an operating constraint (“analysts review 200 cases a day” fixes the alert volume); or a guarantee (the lowest t meeting a contractual precision floor). Tune it on a validation set — TunedThresholdClassifierCV does this with CV — never on test, and consider abstention for low-confidence cases.

7.10 Imbalance & leakage

Six questions. Leakage is the most examinable topic in the subject; imbalance is the one with the most wrong reflex answers.

Q66–71 · imbalance and leakage
66. ⭐ How do you handle class imbalance? Give me your order of operations.

(0) Characterise it — 1:10 or 1:10,000? separated or overlapping? (1) Tune the decision threshold on a strong baseline: cheapest experiment, no new failure modes, frequently sufficient. (2) Class weights / scale_pos_weight: no synthetic rows, no leakage risk — but it decalibrates, so recalibrate if the probabilities are used. (3) Only then resampling — SMOTE, under-sampling, Tomek links, BalancedRandomForest / EasyEnsemble — always inside the pipeline. Throughout: PR-AUC, per-class recall and cost-aligned F-β. Never accuracy.

67. What's wrong with applying SMOTE before the train-test split?

It leaks. Synthetic minority points are interpolated between real rows, and some of those real rows land in validation — so validation rows are partly reconstructions of training data, and the model is effectively being tested on things it has seen. You get an excellent CV score and a production collapse. The fix is structural: split first, and put the resampler inside the pipeline so it is refitted on each training fold only (that is exactly what imblearn's Pipeline exists for).

68. When is SMOTE actively harmful?

When classes overlap or labels are noisy. Interpolating between two minority points that sit near or inside the majority region manufactures synthetic examples right on the boundary, which pushes the boundary into majority territory and inflates false positives. It also amplifies mislabelled minority points into whole neighbourhoods of fake ones. On clean tabular data, inverse-frequency weighting gives near-identical results while being simpler and leak-proof.

69. ⭐ What is data leakage? Name four distinct kinds.

Target leakage: a feature that is a consequence of the target or recorded after it (account_closed_date, num_collection_calls). Train-test contamination: scaling, imputing, encoding, selecting features or resampling before the split. Temporal leakage: features computed from the future, or aggregates over the whole period. Group leakage: the same entity or a near-duplicate on both sides. Symptom in all four: an implausibly good validation score that collapses in production. Structural fix: everything learned from data goes inside a Pipeline, fitted inside the CV loop — plus a split that mimics deployment.

70. How would you detect leakage in someone else's notebook?

Five checks, in order. (1) Is the split the first data operation? (2) Search for fit_transform outside a pipeline. (3) Sort feature importances and ask what the top feature is and when it was recorded. (4) Re-run with a time-based split and see how far the score falls. (5) Check for duplicate IDs across train and test. Also: a 0.99 AUC on a hard problem is an alarm, not a result.

71. What is target encoding and how do you do it without leaking?

Replace a category with a statistic of the target within it. Done naively it uses the row's own label, so the feature contains the target — CV soars, production collapses, and a category seen once becomes literally its own label. Do it out-of-fold / cross-fitted (each fold's encoding computed from the other folds), smoothed toward the global mean by category count (n·ȳ_c + m·ȳ)/(n+m), with an explicit unknown bucket for serve-time categories — all inside the pipeline. CatBoost's ordered target statistics solve it structurally.

7.11 Feature engineering

Six questions. One of them is a generative exercise, not a recall exercise.

Q72–77 · features
72. Walk me through feature engineering for a transaction fraud model.

Organise by entity × window × statistic. Transaction: amount, currency, hour (cyclically encoded), channel, merchant category, is-new-country. Card/customer aggregates over 1h/24h/7d/30d: count, sum, max, std, distinct merchants, distinct countries, velocity ratios. Deviation: amount ÷ 30-day median, geo-distance ÷ elapsed time. Merchant: cross-fitted chargeback rate, relationship age. Device/graph: cards per device. Then the constraints that make it a real answer: every feature must fit the ~150 ms budget and be point-in-time correct, and aggregates must never be computed over the full history at training time.

73. How do you handle high-cardinality categoricals?

In order of preference: native handling (CatBoost ordered target statistics, LightGBM categorical splits, HistGradientBoosting's categorical_features); cross-fitted target encoding with smoothing and an unknown bucket; frequency/count encoding as a cheap partial signal; hashing for unbounded or streaming vocabularies; and grouping by a business hierarchy, which often beats any statistical trick. Avoid one-hot here — it creates thousands of sparse, weak columns and weak splits.

74. How do you encode a datetime? Why cyclical encoding?

Components (hour, day-of-week, month, week-of-year), domain flags (holiday, business day, days-to-next-holiday), and time-since-last-event recency features. Use cyclical encoding sin(2πh/24), cos(2πh/24) because on the raw integer scale 23 and 0 are the furthest apart values when they are actually one hour apart. Two traps: be explicit about timezone and DST, and never feed the raw absolute timestamp — the model will memorise a period rather than a pattern.

75. Filter vs wrapper vs embedded feature selection.

Filter: score each feature independently (correlation, chi², ANOVA F, mutual information, variance threshold) — fast, model-agnostic, blind to interactions. Wrapper: refit a model on subsets (forward/backward, RFE/RFECV) — model-aware and interaction-aware, expensive, can overfit the selection. Embedded: selection as a by-product of fitting (L1, tree importances, boosting gain) — the practical default, inherits the model's biases. All of them must be fitted inside the CV loop.

76. Why would you drop features that improve accuracy?

Because accuracy isn't the only cost. Each feature carries serving latency, acquisition cost (a paid bureau call), drift and monitoring surface, leakage risk, maintenance burden, and sometimes regulatory exposure as a proxy for a protected attribute. A feature worth 0.002 AUC and £0.30 per prediction is a bad trade. Being able to make that argument explicitly is a senior signal.

77. ⭐ What is a feature store and what problem does point-in-time correctness solve?

A shared, versioned system for computing, storing and serving features so training and inference use the same definitions. Point-in-time correctness is the hard part: a training row for March must carry the feature values that existed in March, not today's. Joining today's lifetime_spend onto a March label leaks six months of the future. The mechanism is an as-of join against time-versioned feature history. The sibling problem it solves is training/serving skew — one code path instead of two implementations that silently diverge.

7.12 Missing data & preprocessing

Four questions that are easy to answer badly and easy to answer well.

Q78–81 · missingness and scaling
78. MCAR / MAR / MNAR — define them and say which is dangerous.

MCAR: missingness independent of everything (a sensor dropped packets) — deletion is unbiased, imputation is fine, and it is rare in real data. MAR: missingness depends on observed variables (income missing more often for younger applicants) — model-based imputation (MICE, kNN) is principled; this is the common realistic case. MNAR: missingness depends on the unobserved value itself (high earners decline to state income) — the dangerous one: no imputation can recover it from the data alone, so you model the mechanism or flag the limitation.

79. How do you impute, and where in the pipeline does imputation live?

Simple constant fill (median/mode) as a baseline — noting it shrinks variance and distorts correlations; iterative/MICE or kNN under MAR; and on tabular data, often no imputation at all, letting XGBoost/LightGBM learn a default direction for missing values or using HistGradientBoosting, which takes NaN natively. Wherever it happens, it lives inside the Pipeline and is fitted on the training fold only — a median is a statistic learned from data, and computing it over the whole dataset is contamination.

80. Why can missingness itself be a useful feature?

Because the fact that a value is absent often carries signal — an applicant who declines to state income, a customer with no support history, a device that reports no location. Under MNAR the pattern of missingness is precisely what encodes the hidden value. Add a was_missing indicator alongside whatever you impute (SimpleImputer(add_indicator=True)); it costs almost nothing and is frequently one of the larger wins available.

81. Which models need feature scaling and which don't?

Need it: anything measuring distance (kNN, k-means, DBSCAN, RBF-SVM), maximising variance (PCA), or penalising coefficient magnitude (Ridge, Lasso, regularized logistic), plus neural nets for gradient conditioning. Don't: trees and every tree ensemble, because a split is a threshold on rank order and a monotone transform can't change which side of it a value falls on. Unregularized OLS also doesn't need it — the coefficient absorbs the units — but the moment a penalty appears, it does.

7.13 Interpretability

Five questions. Naming SHAP's failure modes is worth more than describing what SHAP does.

Q82–86 · explanations and fairness
82. ⭐ Explain SHAP. What are its assumptions and failure modes?

SHAP gives each feature the Shapley value of its contribution to a single prediction — averaged over all orderings in which features could be added — producing additive, locally faithful, consistent attributions that sum to the prediction minus a base value and aggregate into a global view. Failure modes: KernelSHAP assumes feature independence when marginalising, so it queries the model on unrealistic rows off the data manifold; path-dependent TreeSHAP avoids that by using the tree's own paths, but this changes the value function such that a feature with no effect can receive non-zero attribution — breaking the dummy axiom; with correlated features credit is split unintuitively; and a SHAP value is not a causal effect. Use it alongside correlation analysis and permutation importance.

83. PDP vs ICE vs ALE — what does each show?

PDP: the average marginal effect of a feature — hides heterogeneity, and evaluates unrealistic combinations when features are correlated. ICE: one curve per instance, revealing exactly that hidden heterogeneity; if half your population responds positively and half negatively, the PDP is flat and the ICE plot shows two fans. ALE: accumulates effects computed within small local windows of the feature, so it never leaves the data manifold — the correlation-robust alternative. Rule: look at ICE before trusting a PDP.

84. Three kinds of feature importance — how do they differ?

Impurity/gain (MDI): how much this feature reduced impurity while the tree was built — free, but biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features and computed on training data. Permutation: how much the held-out metric degrades when the column is shuffled — measures what you care about and works for any model, but correlated features mask each other and shuffling creates unrealistic rows. SHAP: how this prediction is divided among features — local and globally aggregable, additive, expensive, with correlation problems of its own. Three questions, not three implementations.

85. A regulator asks why an application was declined. What do you give them?

An adverse-action notice, not a plot: a small number of specific, plain-language, actionable reasons, derived reproducibly, tied to a recorded model version and input snapshot. In practice: a locally faithful attribution ranked into standardised reason codes, ideally with a counterfactual (“had utilisation been below 40%, this would have been approved”), plus the audit trail. Mention that this requirement is often why the production model is a logistic regression or a monotonically-constrained GBM.

86. Define two fairness metrics and explain why you can't satisfy all of them.

Demographic parity: equal positive-prediction rates across groups. Equal opportunity: equal TPR among those who qualify. (Also: equalised odds — equal TPR and FPR; predictive parity — equal precision, i.e. calibration within groups.) The impossibility result: when base rates genuinely differ, calibration within groups and equalised odds cannot both hold except in degenerate cases. So “make it fair” is under-specified — someone must choose which criterion, and that is a policy decision. Add that removing the protected attribute doesn't help, because proxies encode it.

7.14 Production

Ten questions. Senior interviews spend most of their time here.

Q87–96 · shipping and keeping it alive
87. Walk me through deploying a model end to end.

Frame the decision and metric → build the offline pipeline with point-in-time-correct features → validate with a split that mimics production → choose a threshold from cost or capacity → package the whole pipeline and register it with a version and lineage → shadow deploy to check latency and stability → canary at a few percent with guardrails → A/B against the incumbent → full rollout with a rollback path → monitor data quality, drift, prediction distribution and eventually performance → define retraining triggers and write the runbook. The two markers of a good answer: one feature code path, and a rollback plus an on-call playbook.

88. Batch vs real-time serving — how do you decide?

One question: do the features exist before the request arrives? If yes, precompute on a schedule into a key-value store and make prediction an O(1) lookup — cheaper, more stable, easier to monitor. If a feature only exists at request time, you need real-time. Secondary: acceptable staleness, model cost, and the p99 budget. Most real systems are hybrid.

89. ⭐ How do you detect drift when labels arrive 60 days late?

Layered proxies. Input: per-feature PSI (<0.1 stable, 0.1–0.2 moderate, >0.2 significant), KS/chi-square, Jensen-Shannon and Wasserstein as smoother alternatives to KL — weighted by feature importance. Output: prediction distribution and mean predicted probability, which move immediately. Behavioural: override rate, abstention rate, analyst agreement, downstream conversion. Partial labels: any fast-resolving subset. Data quality: schema, nulls, ranges, cardinality — which catches more incidents than drift statistics do. And the discipline: confirm a metric actually moved before retraining.

90. Data drift vs concept drift — how do the responses differ?

Data/covariate drift is P(x) moving with P(y|x) stable — a new segment or channel. The model may still be valid; reweight, retrain on recent data, or do nothing. Concept drift is P(y|x) changing — fraudsters adapt, a recession redefines risk — and only retraining helps, possibly with new features or a new target definition. Practical difference: covariate drift is detectable without labels; concept drift generally is not.

91. When do you retrain? What triggers it?

Performance-triggered when labels arrive in time; drift-triggered as the proxy, with a confirmation step; scheduled as the boring default that mostly works. The trade-off is staleness vs churn — retraining constantly makes predictions shift under users for no visible reason and makes incidents unreproducible. Gate every retrain behind champion/challenger comparison and a golden-set regression suite in CI.

92. What is training/serving skew and how do you prevent it?

Training and serving compute the same feature differently — a different window, null handling, timezone or rounding — so the model sees a distribution it was never trained on, and nothing alerts because both systems individually “work”. Prevent it architecturally with one shared code path (feature store, shared library, or a batch job whose output the API reads), plus a skew check comparing logged serving features against the training distribution. Its worse sibling is point-in-time incorrectness.

93. How do you A/B test a model? What could invalidate the test?

Randomise at the unit the effect acts on (usually the user), compute the sample size up front from the MDE and power, declare one primary metric plus guardrails (latency, revenue, complaints, fairness), and fix the duration. Invalidators: peeking and stopping at significance; novelty and primacy effects; network effects leaking treatment into control (use cluster/geo randomisation); sample-ratio mismatch; and choosing the winning metric after the fact. Use CUPED to cut variance. And state the offline-to-online gap explicitly.

94. Your model was great offline and users are complaining. Debug it.

An ordered checklist, cheapest first. (1) Leakage — was the offline number ever real? Re-validate with a time split. (2) Split mismatch — did validation mimic production (groups, time, purge)? (3) Slices — is the aggregate fine but a segment broken? (4) Skew — compare live feature distributions against training. (5) Drift. (6) Threshold — is 0.5 still deployed while the base rate moved? (7) Label quality — was the offline label the same thing users care about? (8) The metric itself — does it correlate with the user experience? Naming the ordered list is worth more than any single hypothesis.

95. How do you make an ML pipeline reproducible?

“Set the seed” is the 2018 answer. The full one: pinned dependencies (a lockfile), versioned data snapshots (DVC/lakeFS-style), captured environment, config-as-code, recorded lineage from a prediction back to a model, dataset and commit, experiment tracking, and golden-prediction tests in CI. What actually breaks reproducibility is a library minor version changing a default, or the upstream table being backfilled — seeds only control randomness you own.

96. What is the feedback-loop problem in a fraud or credit model?

The model's decisions determine its future training data. A fraud model never observes whether the transactions it blocked were fraud; a credit model never sees how rejected applicants would have performed (reject inference). The training set becomes systematically censored, the model grows confident about a shrinking slice of the world, and offline evaluation can't see it. Mitigations: a small exploration/control slice that bypasses the model, propensity weighting on observed data, and modelling the blocked population explicitly instead of dropping it.

Q97–105 · the frontier
97. ⭐ GBDT or deep learning for tabular data? Has that changed?

“Historically GBDTs, decisively — faster to train, less tuning, native missing-value and categorical handling. As of 2026 it is genuinely contested for small-to-medium data by tabular foundation models: TabPFN-2.5 reported a 100% win rate against default XGBoost on ≤10k rows / ≤500 features, and TabPFN-3 (May 2026) scales to 1M training rows, reporting that a single forward pass beats 8-hour-tuned GBDT baselines up to 1M rows / 200 features on TabArena and outperforms AutoGluon 1.5 ‘extreme’ while roughly 10× faster. Caveats: pretrained exclusively on synthetic data, benchmark selection effects, and thinner deployment/licensing/interpretability stories. So I'd benchmark both on my own data and still default to a GBDT where I need monotonic constraints, interpretability, cheap retraining and a known ops story.”

98. What is conformal prediction and what guarantee does it give?

A distribution-free, model-agnostic wrapper turning point predictions into sets or intervals with finite-sample coverage. Split conformal: fit on a training split, compute a nonconformity score (e.g. absolute residual) on a disjoint calibration split, take the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile as q̂, emit ŷ ± q̂. The guarantee is marginal coverage ≥ 1−α under exchangeability — which breaks under drift and for time series, hence adaptive/weighted variants. Note it gives marginal, not conditional, coverage unless you use a normalised score. Uses: selective prediction, deferral to a human, safe deployment.

99. Aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty.

Aleatoric is irreducible noise in the process — two identical applicants can have different outcomes. More data will not reduce it; model it with quantile regression or a predictive distribution. Epistemic is model ignorance where data is sparse — it does shrink with more data, which is exactly what active learning targets, and you estimate it with ensemble variance or bootstrap spread. The practical payoff: epistemic uncertainty tells you where to collect data; aleatoric tells you where to widen the interval and stop trying.

100. ⭐ Your model predicts churn accurately. The business asks who to give a discount to. What's wrong with using it?

The churn model answers “who will leave?”; the decision needs “who will stay because we acted?”. The highest scores are dominated by lost causes who leave regardless, budget leaks to sure things who would have stayed, and some customers are sleeping dogs for whom the offer is a reminder to cancel — a negative treatment effect. You need uplift / CATE, ideally from a randomised holdout, evaluated with Qini and uplift curves rather than AUC. The general principle: a predictive model is invalid under intervention.

101. Explain uplift modelling. Why is AUC the wrong metric for it?

Uplift estimates the conditional average treatment effect E[y|x, treat] − E[y|x, control] — the change caused by the action, not the outcome level. Estimators: T-learner (separate models per arm), S-learner (one model with treatment as a feature), X-learner (better with imbalanced arms), causal forests. AUC is wrong because there is no per-row label to be right about — you never observe both potential outcomes for the same person. Evaluation must be aggregate: rank by predicted uplift, and compare treated vs control outcomes within score deciles, which is what the Qini curve plots.

102. What is double machine learning for?

Estimating a causal effect with high-dimensional confounders while still using flexible ML. Fit two nuisance models with any learner — outcome given confounders, and treatment given confounders — take the residuals of each, and regress one on the other (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell). Two ingredients make it valid: Neyman orthogonality, so small nuisance errors don't bias the effect to first order, and cross-fitting, so a row's nuisance predictions come from a model that never saw it. The payoff is a √n-consistent effect estimate with valid confidence intervals while using gradient boosting for the messy parts. EconML implements it.

103. What is Simpson's paradox and when have you seen it?

A trend that holds within every subgroup reverses (or vanishes) when the groups are pooled, because the groups differ in both their exposure and their baseline. Where it bites in ML: a model that looks better overall but is worse on every segment; an A/B test where treatment wins overall but loses on both mobile and desktop (an allocation imbalance — usually a sample-ratio mismatch bug); and any “feature X correlates with the target” claim made without conditioning. The fix is to condition on the confounder, or randomise.

104. How do you decide between a simpler and a more accurate model?

Price the difference. What is the accuracy gain worth in business units, and what does the complex model cost in latency, infrastructure, retraining, monitoring surface, debugging time, explainability and regulatory risk? Then check whether the gain is even real — paired comparison, a CI on the difference, and an honest accounting of how many variants you tried. In regulated settings the answer is often simple-by-constraint. And say the thing most candidates won't: if the difference doesn't clear the cost, the simpler model is the better model, and that is a result worth reporting.

105. What would you do with 500 labels and a need for 10,000?

First question the premise — would 1,500 well-chosen labels do? Then a ladder: strong simple baseline with heavy regularization and cross-validation you can trust at this size; active learning to spend the next labels where uncertainty is highest, with a diversity/core-set term so you don't label 500 near-duplicates; weak supervision if domain rules exist (labelling functions + a label model); pseudo-labelling with a clean held-out set to catch confirmation bias; transfer from a related task or a pretrained representation; a cheaper proxy label; and — genuinely often the right answer — buy the labels, with clear annotation guidelines and an inter-annotator agreement check, because that agreement rate is your accuracy ceiling. Also worth naming here: TabPFN-style tabular foundation models are strongest exactly in this small-data regime.

7.21 Behavioural / context questions

Answer with a concrete story: situation → constraint → what you measured → decision → outcome → what you'd change. Prepare four stories that can be recombined to cover all twelve.

Twelve context questions
1. Tell me about a model that failed in production.

They want the process change, not the bug. Name the root cause precisely (skew? leakage? drift? a threshold nobody owned?), say how you found it, then describe what you changed so the class of failure cannot recur — a skew check, a golden-set test, a monitoring alert, a runbook. “We fixed it and moved on” is the weak version.

2. How do you measure the success of an ML project beyond model metrics?

The decision it feeds and the business quantity that decision moves — cases prevented, hours saved, margin protected, complaints avoided — measured against the pre-existing baseline, ideally with an experiment. Plus the operational half: is it maintained, is it monitored, does the team trust it, can it be rolled back.

3. A stakeholder says the model “just needs to be more accurate”. How do you turn that into a spec?

Ask what decision changes if it is. Then convert: which error is costing us — false positives or false negatives? What is each worth? What is the current operating point and capacity? That produces a metric, a threshold and a target, which is a spec. Often the real answer is not more accuracy but a different threshold, better calibration, or a different target variable entirely.

4. How do you explain a model's decision to a non-technical stakeholder or a regulator?

Two different audiences. A stakeholder wants the mechanism in their language plus one worked example: “the three factors that pushed this case over the line were X, Y, Z.” A regulator wants a reproducible, documented, auditable process — reason codes, model version, input snapshot, and the fairness evidence. Never show either a beeswarm plot as the answer.

5. How do you decide between a simple interpretable model and a more accurate black box?

Price the difference in business units, then subtract the cost: latency, infrastructure, retraining, monitoring, debugging, explainability, regulatory risk. Check whether the gain is even real (paired CI, how many variants you tried). Say the thing most candidates won't: if the gap doesn't clear the cost, the simpler model is the better model.

6. Tell me about a time you chose not to use ML.

The strongest possible answer to this exists in §1.1's four branches. Pick the one that is true for you — a writable rule, unavailable labels, unacceptable unexplainable error, or too little data — and describe what you shipped instead and how you knew it was enough.

7. How do you handle a domain expert disagreeing with your model?

Treat the disagreement as data. Get specific cases, look at them individually, and one of three things is true: the expert is right and the model has a real failure mode (a slice, a feature, a label definition); the model is right and the expert's intuition is a base-rate error; or you're both right and the metric is wrong. All three are useful. What is not useful is defending the aggregate number.

8. How do you prioritise when three teams want models and you have one quarter?

Score on: value of the decision × frequency, data readiness (labels exist? point-in-time feasible?), baseline gap (can a rule already do it?), and deployment cost. Prefer the one where a simple model shipped end-to-end creates a repeatable path for the other two. Be explicit that saying no to two of them is the job.

9. How do you handle discovering bias in a shipped model?

Quantify it per subgroup on the deployed decision before saying anything imprecise; escalate immediately with the number and the affected population; propose interim mitigation (threshold change, human review for the affected slice, or disabling) while a fix is built; and treat “which fairness criterion do we hold ourselves to” as a policy decision that needs an owner outside the modelling team.

10. How do you keep up with the field without chasing every paper?

A small number of high-signal sources, and a filter: does this change a decision I actually make? Release notes for the libraries you use, one or two benchmark suites checked at decision time rather than quoted from memory, and depth over breadth — reading one paper properly beats skimming ten. Naming something specific and recent (e.g. the tabular foundation model results) demonstrates it better than describing the habit.

11. Describe a time you found a bug in your own analysis after presenting it.

They're testing whether you disclose. The structure: found it, quantified the impact immediately, told the people who acted on it before they found out elsewhere, corrected the record, and added the check that would have caught it. Understated and specific beats dramatic.

12. How do you manage the cost of an ML system as it scales?

Measure first — cost per prediction, split across feature fetch, inference and retraining. Then the usual ladder: precompute and cache, batch, drop expensive features that don't earn their keep, right-size the model, and revisit retraining frequency, which is often the largest and least-examined line item. And say the unglamorous thing: the biggest saving is usually turning off a model nobody is acting on.
