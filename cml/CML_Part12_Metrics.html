The level most candidates fail The confusion matrix and everything derived from it, precision/recall/F-β and where each dominates, the ROC-versus-PR question with its structural explanation, micro/macro/weighted averaging, regression metrics chosen from the decision, ranking metrics that know about position, thresholds as a business decision, calibration end to end, and the correct order of operations for imbalance.

Interviews are lost here, and they are lost in a recognisable way: a number is reported that the metric cannot support. Accuracy under 1:1000 imbalance. ROC-AUC quoted as though it said something about calibration. A tuned score reported from the CV it was tuned on. A 0.5 threshold treated as a decision rather than a default. Each has a one-sentence correction, and this part gives you all of them with the tooling that makes them cheap. Two ideas are worth more than the rest combined: 0.5 is a default, not a decision, and the imbalance order of operations — threshold first, class weights second, resampling last and only if needed.

12.1 The confusion matrix ⭐⭐

Everything in this part is derived from four numbers. Being fluent in that derivation is what lets you answer any metric question from first principles.

All six numbers computed exactly from 4,000 simulated scores. Three experiments. (1) Drop the base rate to 2% and watch accuracy stay above 0.97 at every threshold while precision collapses. (2) Compare ROC-AUC and PR-AUC as the base rate falls. (3) Notice that ROC-AUC and PR-AUC don't change at all when you move the threshold — they are threshold-free, which is exactly why neither tells you what your deployed system will do.

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, both of which are computed without ever touching TN. That's usually what you want (TN is enormous and uninformative under imbalance), but it means F1 can look identical for two very different classifiers, and it's asymmetric — swap which class you call positive and F1 changes. MCC uses all four cells, is symmetric, and gives a more honest single number on imbalanced problems. Worth naming when someone asks for "one metric".

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Accuracy is 95% and the model is useless. Explain.

Ask for the base rate. If 95% of rows are negative, predicting "negative" for everything scores 95% and catches nothing — accuracy has become a majority-class detector. Report instead the confusion matrix at the deployed threshold, per-class recall, and PR-AUC. There's a second failure worth adding: accuracy is blind to the cost asymmetry between a false positive and a false negative, so even on balanced data it's the wrong objective whenever the two errors cost different amounts.

ConceptualPrecision vs recall — a business example where each dominates.

Precision dominates when acting is expensive or damaging: blocking a legitimate payment (lost sale + support contact), sending an enforcement letter, or opening a fraud case that costs an analyst 20 minutes. Recall dominates when missing is catastrophic: cancer screening, safety recalls, AML compliance, security alerts. The framing to use: precision is "what happens when we act", recall is "what happens when we miss" — and the threshold (§12.7) is the dial that buys one with the other, so the real question is always which error the business can afford.

Deep diveWhen would you use MCC instead of F1?

When you want a single honest number on an imbalanced problem. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, neither of which uses true negatives — so F1 is asymmetric (swap which class is "positive" and it changes) and can be identical for classifiers that behave very differently. MCC is essentially the correlation between predicted and true labels, uses all four cells, is symmetric, and returns 0 for a random or constant classifier regardless of the class balance. That last property makes it much harder to game than F1 or accuracy.

12.2 Precision, recall and F-β ⭐

The trade-off is mechanical: moving the threshold buys one with the other. What's not mechanical is choosing where to sit.

F-β lets you state that preference numerically: F_β = (1+β²)·P·R/(β²P + R). Setting β = 2 weights recall roughly 4× more than precision (β² = 4); β = 0.5 does the reverse. The clean way to choose β is from the cost ratio — if a miss costs 4× a false alarm, β = 2 is the corresponding F-measure.

Why the harmonic mean rather than the arithmetic

The harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value. A classifier with precision 1.0 and recall 0.01 has an arithmetic mean of 0.505 — flattering — but an F1 of about 0.02. That's the point: F1 refuses to reward a model that achieves one at the total expense of the other, which is exactly the degenerate behaviour a threshold at an extreme produces. Being able to explain why harmonic is a small but reliable differentiator.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhy is F1 a harmonic mean rather than an arithmetic one?

Because the harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value, so it refuses to reward a model that maximises one metric at the total expense of the other. Precision 1.0 with recall 0.01 gives an arithmetic mean of 0.505 but an F1 of ~0.02 — which correctly reflects that the model is useless. Since moving the threshold to an extreme trivially maximises either precision or recall alone, a mean that punishes imbalance between them is the only sensible single summary.

DesignHow do you choose β in F-β?

From the cost ratio. β² is roughly how many times more you weight recall than precision, so if a false negative costs 4× a false positive, β = 2 is the matching measure. In practice I'd rather skip F-β entirely and go straight to expected cost: assign real currency values to FP and FN, and choose the threshold that minimises total cost (§12.7). F-β is a useful summary when you need one number for comparison, but it's a proxy for the cost calculation, and if you can do the calculation directly you should.

12.3 ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC ⭐⭐

The most-asked metrics question, and it has a structural explanation that most candidates don't give.

interactive ROC-AUC flatters, PR-AUC doesn't
—ROC-AUC
—PR-AUC
—PR baseline (= base rate)
—lift over baseline

The classifier is exactly as good at every setting — the score distributions never change, only the mix. Drag from 50% down to 1% and watch ROC-AUC stay essentially flat while PR-AUC falls off a cliff. The reason is structural: the ROC's x-axis is the false-positive rate, normalised by a huge negative count, so a flood of false positives barely registers.

The structural explanation

ROC plots TPR against FPR. FPR is FP/(FP+TN) — divided by the total number of negatives. Under 1:1000 imbalance, going from 100 false positives to 1,000 moves FPR from 0.0001 to 0.001: visually nothing. But precision, TP/(TP+FP), is divided by the number of alerts, so the same change is devastating. That is the whole difference: ROC normalises by a quantity you don't care about; PR normalises by the one you do.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC — when do you prefer which?

ROC-AUC plots TPR against FPR and summarises ranking quality well when classes are roughly balanced. Its weakness under imbalance is structural: FPR divides by the total negative count, so going from 100 to 1,000 false positives on a 1:1000 problem moves it from 0.0001 to 0.001 — visually nothing. PR-AUC plots precision against recall, and precision divides by the number of alerts, so the same change is devastating. ROC normalises by a quantity you don't care about; PR normalises by the one you do. Prefer PR-AUC when positives are rare. And note its baseline is the base rate, not 0.5 — plus neither says anything about calibration.

Very commonWhat does ROC-AUC actually mean, probabilistically?

It's the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative — P(score(x⁺) > score(x⁻)), with ties counted as a half. It equals the normalised Mann-Whitney U statistic. Two consequences follow directly: it's threshold-free, so it tells you nothing about the deployed operating point; and it's invariant to any monotone transform of the scores, which is why Platt scaling changes the probabilities completely and leaves AUC untouched (§12.8).

GotchaCan you compare PR-AUC across two datasets?

Not directly, because the baseline differs — a random ranker achieves PR-AUC equal to the base rate, so 0.30 on a 2% prevalence problem is a 15× lift and excellent, while 0.30 on a 40% prevalence problem is worse than random. Report PR-AUC alongside the base rate, or report the lift over baseline. ROC-AUC is comparable across prevalence (its baseline is always 0.5), which is one of the few genuine arguments in its favour — but that same property is exactly what makes it flattering on the imbalanced problem itself.

12.4 Multi-class averaging ⭐

“F1 = 0.84” is an incomplete claim in a multi-class setting. Which averaging you used changes the number and the meaning.

AveragingHowDominated byReport it when
MicroPool TP/FP/FN across all classes, then computeFrequent classesYou care about overall correct predictions. In single-label multi-class, micro-F1 = accuracy
MacroCompute per class, then average unweightedNothing — every class counts equallyRare classes matter as much as common ones — the usual choice when imbalance is meaningful
WeightedPer class, averaged by supportFrequent classesYou want per-class detail but a support-weighted summary; mostly reproduces micro
SamplesPer row, then averaged—Multi-label only (§10.2)
Why micro-F1 = accuracy in single-label multi-class

Every prediction is either correct (one TP) or wrong — in which case it contributes exactly one FP to the predicted class and one FN to the true class. So summed across classes, ΣFP = ΣFN, which makes micro-precision = micro-recall = ΣTP/n = accuracy — and the harmonic mean of two equal numbers is that number. Being able to derive it in two sentences is a satisfying answer to a question that usually gets a shrug.

Likely interview questions
Very commonMicro vs macro F1 — which do you report?

Micro pools TP/FP/FN across classes, so it's dominated by frequent classes — and in single-label multi-class it equals accuracy exactly. Macro averages per-class F1 with equal weight, so a rare class counts as much as a common one; that's what you report when small classes matter, which under imbalance they usually do. Weighted averages by support and mostly reproduces micro. The real answer: report the one matching the decision, say which one it is, and show the per-class breakdown — the aggregate hides exactly the failure you need to see.

Deep diveProve that micro-F1 equals accuracy in single-label multi-class.

Every prediction is either right or wrong. A correct prediction contributes one TP. An incorrect one contributes exactly one FP (to the class predicted) and exactly one FN (to the true class). Summing over classes therefore gives ΣFP = ΣFN = number of errors. Micro-precision is ΣTP/(ΣTP+ΣFP) and micro-recall is ΣTP/(ΣTP+ΣFN), which are equal, and both equal ΣTP/n = accuracy. The harmonic mean of two identical numbers is that number, so micro-F1 = accuracy. Note it relies on single-label: in multi-label it no longer holds.

12.5 Regression metrics ⭐

Same principle as classification: pick from the decision. Is a 10-unit error twice as bad as a 5-unit error, or four times?

MetricOptimises / penalisesUse whenWatch out
RMSEThe mean; large errors quadraticallyBig misses are disproportionately costlyOutlier-sensitive; in target units, which is good for communication
MAEThe median; linear penalty“Typical error” is the business quantityNot differentiable at zero (rarely a practical issue)
RMSLERelative error; punishes under-prediction moreTargets spanning orders of magnitudeRequires non-negative targets
MAPEPercentage errorCommunicating to a business audienceUndefined/explodes near zero; asymmetric — biases forecasts low (§10.6)
MASEError ÷ seasonal-naive errorComparing across many series of different scaleNeeds a defined seasonal period
PinballA specific quantilePrediction intervals; asymmetric costsReport coverage alongside it
R² / adjusted R²Variance explainedCommunicating fitAlways rises with features; test R² can be negative; not comparable across datasets (§5.5)
RMSE vs MAE, stated as a decision

If the cost of an error grows faster than linearly — a stock-out that cascades, a structural failure — RMSE matches the business and you should optimise squared loss. If cost grows roughly linearly and you have outliers you cannot clean, MAE matches it. And note the consequence for the model, not just the report: optimising MSE fits the conditional mean, optimising MAE fits the conditional median (§4.1). On a right-skewed target those are materially different numbers, so this choice changes the predictions themselves.

Likely interview questions
Very commonRMSE vs MAE — what does each optimise?

Minimising squared error fits the conditional mean; minimising absolute error fits the conditional median — so on a right-skewed target they give materially different predictions, not just different scores. RMSE penalises large errors quadratically (right when costs grow super-linearly) and is outlier-sensitive; MAE is robust and reports "typical error". Choose from the decision: is a 10-unit error twice as bad as a 5-unit error, or four times? And keep the metric consistent with the training loss — reporting MAE while optimising MSE means you're measuring something you didn't ask the model to do.

GotchaYour RMSE looks fine but the business says the model is wrong. What might be happening?

Several possibilities worth checking in order. The error is concentrated in a slice that matters — high-value customers, recent periods — and the aggregate hides it (§11.6). The mean isn't the quantity they act on: if they need a typical value or a safety margin, RMSE's mean-seeking behaviour is systematically wrong for them and MAE or a quantile is right. The scale differs across the range: 10% error is fine on large values and disastrous on small ones, which RMSE weights the opposite way. Or the errors are biased in one direction — RMSE doesn't distinguish over- from under-prediction, so I'd plot residuals against fitted and check for systematic bias.

12.6 Ranking metrics

Short section, one idea: position matters, and only some metrics know it.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualPrecision@k vs nDCG vs MRR — when does each apply?

Precision@k when you only care whether relevant items are in the top k and order within it doesn't matter — a grid of results all visible at once. nDCG when position matters and relevance is graded: it discounts gain by 1/log₂(rank+1) and normalises by the ideal ordering, so rank 1 is worth ~3× rank 8. MRR when there's essentially one right answer and all you care about is how far down it is — question answering, navigational search. Default to nDCG for feeds and recommendations; it's the one that matches how people actually consume a ranked list.

12.7 Choosing a threshold ⭐⭐

0.5 is a default, not a decision. It is the right threshold only when false positives and false negatives cost the same and the classes are balanced — which is almost never.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ How do you choose a decision threshold?

Not 0.5 — that's a default, correct only when classes are balanced and both errors cost the same. Three legitimate sources: a cost matrix (minimise expected cost; for a calibrated model the optimum is near c_FP/(c_FP+c_FN)); an operating constraint ("analysts can review 200 cases a day" fixes alert volume and therefore t); or a guarantee (the lowest t meeting a contractual precision floor, then report the recall you get). Tune it on a validation set — TunedThresholdClassifierCV does this with CV — never on test, and consider abstention for low-confidence cases.

Deep diveWhy is the cost-optimal threshold c_FP/(c_FP+c_FN)?

At probability p, acting costs (1−p)·c_FP in expectation (you pay for a false positive when the case is negative); not acting costs p·c_FN. You should act when acting is cheaper: (1−p)c_FP < p·c_FN, which rearranges to p > c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN). So with FP = £8 and FN = £120, the threshold is 8/128 ≈ 0.0625. The crucial precondition: this only holds if p is a genuine calibrated probability — which is why calibration (§12.8) isn't an optional refinement but a prerequisite for cost-based decisions.

DesignWould you use different thresholds for different customer segments?

Sometimes, and it needs care. It's legitimate and often optimal when the cost structure genuinely differs by segment — reviewing a high-value transaction is worth more analyst time, so a lower threshold there maximises expected value. It's legally fraught when the segment correlates with a protected attribute, because per-group thresholds are an explicit use of that attribute and are prohibited in several jurisdictions even when they improve outcomes (§14.8). My rule: segment by cost or capacity, never by a protected characteristic or an obvious proxy, and document the justification.

12.8 Calibration ⭐⭐

Among the cases you scored 0.7, roughly 70% should be positive. If they aren't, every expected-value calculation downstream is wrong — including the threshold you just derived.

interactive Reliability diagram — miscalibrate it, then fix it
—ECE
—Brier score
—log loss
—ROC-AUC

Platt scaling is fitted by real gradient descent on a one-parameter-pair logistic model; isotonic regression is a real pool-adjacent-violators implementation. Two things to notice. ROC-AUC does not move under Platt scaling — it's a monotone transform, so the ranking is untouched while the probabilities change completely. And with a small calibration set, isotonic overfits: its ECE looks good while its log loss gets worse.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?

A model is calibrated if, among cases predicted at p, a fraction p are actually positive. Measure with a reliability diagram, ECE Σ(nᵦ/n)|acc(b) − conf(b)|, and the Brier score. Fix with Platt scaling (a sigmoid on the log-odds — parametric, works with little data), isotonic regression (non-parametric, more flexible, needs more data and overfits on small calibration sets), or Venn-Abers. Always fit on held-out data. The 2026 nuance: verify it helped on a proper scoring rule — a recent benchmark found Platt and isotonic can degrade strong tabular models. And it matters because cost-based thresholds (§12.7) are only valid if p is real.

Very commonWhich models are typically miscalibrated, and in which direction?

Well-regularized logistic regression is usually close to calibrated — it optimises log loss, a proper scoring rule, so calibration is what it's minimising. Random Forests are under-confident at the extremes: averaging many trees pulls predictions away from 0 and 1, because reaching a prediction of 1.0 requires every tree to agree. Boosted trees are typically over-confident, since boosting drives margins up. Naive Bayes is wildly overconfident because it double-counts correlated evidence (§6.2). SVMs have no probabilities natively. Rule: check with a reliability diagram, never assume.

Deep divePlatt vs isotonic — how do you choose?

Platt fits a two-parameter sigmoid to the log-odds — parametric, so it works with a few hundred calibration points, but it can only apply a sigmoid-shaped correction and will fail if the miscalibration has a different shape. Isotonic fits a free monotone step function via pool-adjacent-violators — it can correct any monotone distortion, but with too little data it interpolates noise and overfits, which shows up as good ECE and worse log loss. Rule of thumb: isotonic above ~1,000 calibration points, Platt below. And with either: fit on held-out data, and measure whether it helped on a proper scoring rule rather than assuming.

GotchaDoes calibration change your AUC?

Not for Platt scaling, and not for any strictly monotone transform — ROC-AUC depends only on the ranking, and a monotone map preserves the order of every pair. That's a useful sanity check: if AUC changes after Platt scaling, something is wrong with your implementation. Isotonic can change AUC very slightly, because it produces ties where the step function is flat, and tied scores are handled differently in the AUC calculation. The broader point: calibration and discrimination are independent axes — a model can rank perfectly and be badly calibrated, or be well-calibrated and rank poorly.

12.9 Imbalanced classification ⭐⭐

There is a correct order of operations, and reaching for SMOTE first is the single most common wrong answer in classical ML interviews.

Metrics under imbalance

Never accuracy. Report PR-AUC / average precision, per-class recall, and an F-β aligned to your costs. Be explicitly wary of ROC-AUC's optimism — the §12.3 widget shows exactly why. And always show the confusion matrix at the threshold you intend to deploy, because that is the only artefact that tells the business how many cases land in the queue tomorrow.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ How do you handle class imbalance? Give me your order of operations.

(0) Characterise it — 1:10 or 1:10,000? separated or overlapping? (1) Tune the decision threshold on a strong baseline: cheapest experiment, no new failure modes, frequently sufficient on its own. (2) Class weights / scale_pos_weight: no synthetic rows, no leakage risk — but it decalibrates, so recalibrate if you use the probabilities. (3) Only then resampling — SMOTE, under-sampling, Tomek links, BalancedRandomForest — always inside the pipeline. Throughout: PR-AUC, per-class recall, cost-aligned F-β. Never accuracy.

Very commonWhat's wrong with applying SMOTE before the train-test split?

It leaks. Synthetic minority points are interpolated between real rows, and some of those real rows end up in validation — so validation rows are partly reconstructions of training data and the model is effectively tested on things it has seen. You get an excellent CV score and a production collapse. The fix is structural rather than a judgement call: split first, and put the resampler inside the pipeline so it's refitted on each training fold only — which is exactly what imblearn's Pipeline exists for (sklearn's won't do it, because it applies transforms at predict time too).

GotchaWhen is SMOTE actively harmful?

When classes overlap or labels are noisy. Interpolating between two minority points that sit near or inside the majority region manufactures synthetic examples right on the boundary, which pushes the boundary into majority territory and inflates false positives. It also amplifies a single mislabelled minority point into a whole neighbourhood of fake ones. In high dimensions the interpolation is even less meaningful (§2.7). On clean tabular data, inverse-frequency weighting gives near-identical results while being simpler and leak-proof — which is why it's step 2 and SMOTE is step 3.

DesignFraud at 0.1% prevalence. Walk me through your approach.

First, characterise: 1:1000, and fraud is heterogeneous rather than a single tight cluster. Metric: PR-AUC plus precision-at-the-review-capacity, never accuracy or ROC-AUC alone. Model: a GBM with scale_pos_weight, but I'd start by checking whether threshold tuning alone on an unweighted model suffices. Threshold from capacity — if analysts review 200 cases/day, that fixes the alert volume and everything else follows. Validate with a time-based split, because fraud patterns drift. Then the two things people forget: calibrate if the probability feeds an expected-value decision, and plan for the feedback loop — blocked transactions never reveal their true label, so I'd hold out a small exploration slice (§15.7).
