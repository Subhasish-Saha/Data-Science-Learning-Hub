Part 9 of 17 · Unsupervised Learning

Structure without labels Lloyd's algorithm and k-means' three assumptions, every method for choosing k and the honest answer, hierarchical linkage and dendrograms, density-based clustering, Gaussian mixtures and EM, PCA from the covariance eigen-decomposition, the t-SNE trap everyone should state unprompted, anomaly detection without labels, and association rules.

Unsupervised learning is asked about more narrowly than supervised learning but very predictably, and the questions cluster around assumptions rather than algorithms. k-means assumes spherical, roughly equal-variance clusters and a meaningful Euclidean distance, which is why it is scale-sensitive and fails on rings. PCA maximises variance, which has units, which is why you must scale first. And the trap that separates people who have read about t-SNE from people who have used it: distances between clusters and cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot are meaningless. Volunteering that unprompted is worth more than describing the algorithm.

9.1 k-means ⭐⭐

Asked about far more than it is used. Know the algorithm, its three assumptions, and why the seed changes the answer.

—inertia
—silhouette
—points reassigned

Real Lloyd iterations with a real k-means++ seeding routine and a genuinely computed silhouette. Try the bad seed a few times: it converges to a visibly worse local optimum with higher inertia. That is exactly why n_init exists and why k-means++ is the default — the objective is non-convex, so the starting point changes the answer.

The algorithm and its three assumptions

Lloyd's algorithm: initialise k centroids; assign each point to its nearest centroid; update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points; repeat until assignments stop changing. Each step can only reduce inertia (within-cluster sum of squares), so it always converges — to a local optimum.

AssumptionWhat breaks when it fails
Clusters are roughly sphericalElongated or ring-shaped clusters get carved arbitrarily — k-means will cut a ring into wedges
Clusters have similar varianceA tight cluster next to a diffuse one: the diffuse one gets split and the tight one absorbs its neighbours
Clusters have similar size (count)Inertia is a sum, so a large cluster dominates the objective and a small one gets swallowed
Plus: Euclidean distance is meaningful⇒ scale-sensitive (§3.4) and degraded in high dimensions (§2.7)
Plus: every point belongs somewhereThere is no noise label — outliers are forced into a cluster and drag its centroid
k-means++ is not a minor detail

Random initialisation can place two centroids inside the same true cluster, and Lloyd's algorithm has no mechanism to fix that — it converges to a genuinely bad local optimum. k-means++ seeds centroids one at a time, choosing each with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing centroid, so they start spread out. It gives an O(log k) approximation guarantee in expectation, and combined with n_init (multiple restarts, keep the lowest inertia) it makes k-means reliable in practice. The widget above lets you see the failure directly.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ How does k-means work, and what are its assumptions?

Lloyd's algorithm: initialise centroids (k-means++), assign each point to its nearest centroid, recompute centroids as the means, repeat until assignments stabilise. It minimises inertia — within-cluster sum of squares — which is non-convex, so the seed changes the result, hence n_init. Assumptions: clusters are roughly spherical, of similar variance and similar size, and Euclidean distance is meaningful — so it's scale-sensitive and degrades in high dimensions. Plus every point must belong to a cluster: there's no noise label, so outliers drag centroids.

ConceptualWhy does k-means always converge, and does it converge to the right answer?

It always converges because each step can only reduce inertia — the assignment step reassigns points to closer centroids, the update step moves each centroid to the mean, which is the inertia-minimising point for that assignment — and there are finitely many possible assignments, so it must terminate. But it converges to a local optimum, not the global one, because the objective is non-convex. That's exactly why n_init (multiple restarts) and k-means++ seeding exist, and why "different results each run" is expected behaviour rather than a bug.

GotchaWhy does k-means fail on concentric rings?

Because it assigns by distance to a centroid, and the centroid of a ring is at its centre — the same place as the centroid of the inner circle. So the two clusters are indistinguishable to the objective, and k-means will instead cut both rings into radial wedges, which is the lower-inertia solution. It isn't failing to optimise; it's optimising a criterion that doesn't match the structure. The right tool is a density-based method (DBSCAN/HDBSCAN, §9.4), or a spectral method that clusters in a transformed space.

9.2 Choosing k ⭐

Every method here has a caveat, and the complete answer includes the non-technical half.

MethodWhat it measuresCaveat
Elbow on inertiaWhere added clusters stop reducing WCSS muchInertia always falls with k, and the elbow is often absent or ambiguous — the most-taught and least-reliable method
Silhouette(b−a)/max(a,b) per point: separation vs cohesionFavours spherical, well-separated clusters — i.e. it rewards the shape k-means assumes. O(n²) distances
Gap statisticInertia vs that of uniform random reference dataPrincipled; needs many reference samples, so it's slow
Davies-BouldinAverage similarity between each cluster and its most similar oneLower is better; same spherical bias
Calinski-HarabaszBetween-cluster vs within-cluster dispersion ratioTends to increase with k; compare shapes, not raw values
BIC / AIC (via GMM)Likelihood penalised by parameter countThe most principled option — but requires a probabilistic model (§9.5)
StabilityHow consistent assignments are across bootstrap resamplesUnderused and genuinely informative — an unstable k is a wrong k
The honest half of the answer

k is usually a business constraint. How many segments can the marketing team actually build campaigns for? How many risk tiers does the pricing system support? How many groups can an analyst meaningfully name? Giving the technical methods and then saying “in practice I'd narrow to a plausible range statistically and let the business pick within it, because a mathematically optimal 17 segments that nobody can act on is worth less than a defensible 5” is the complete answer.

Likely interview questions
Very commonHow do you choose k?

Technically: the elbow on inertia (subjective and often absent), the silhouette score, the gap statistic, Davies-Bouldin, Calinski-Harabasz, and — most principled — BIC via a GMM. I'd also check stability: re-run on bootstrap samples and see whether assignments are consistent, because an unstable k is a wrong k. And the honest half: k is usually a business decision — how many segments can actually be acted on. I'd narrow to a plausible range statistically and let the business choose within it.

GotchaWhy can't you just pick the k that minimises inertia?

Because inertia is monotonically decreasing in k — it reaches exactly zero when k = n, with every point its own cluster. So minimising it always selects the most degenerate solution. That's the entire reason for the elbow heuristic (look for diminishing returns rather than the minimum) and for penalised criteria like BIC that explicitly charge for parameters. It's the clustering analogue of R² always increasing with more features (§5.5), and the fix is the same: penalise complexity or use held-out evidence.

9.3 Hierarchical clustering

Gives you every k at once, in the form of a tree you can cut at any height — which is genuinely useful for exploration.

LinkageDistance between clustersProduces
SingleClosest pairLong, chained clusters; can follow non-convex shapes but suffers from chaining — one bridge point merges two real clusters
CompleteFarthest pairCompact, roughly equal-diameter clusters; sensitive to outliers
AverageMean pairwise distanceA compromise; the usual default outside Ward
WardIncrease in within-cluster variance from mergingThe common choice. Minimises the same quantity as k-means, so it gives similar, compact clusters — and requires Euclidean distance

Cost is the limiting factor: agglomerative clustering is O(n²) in memory for the distance matrix and O(n²log n) or worse in time, so it stops being practical in the tens of thousands of rows. Divisive (top-down) variants exist but are rarer.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhen would you use hierarchical clustering over k-means?

Three cases. When you want every k at once — the dendrogram lets you cut at any height and inspect the nesting, which is far better for exploration than refitting k-means at each k. When the data has a genuine hierarchy (taxonomies, org structures, product categories). And when you want to use a non-Euclidean distance like Gower for mixed types (§2.6), which most linkages support but Ward doesn't. The blocker is always size: it's O(n²) memory, so past tens of thousands of rows you need k-means or a sampled approach.

Deep diveWhat is the chaining effect in single linkage?

Single linkage merges the two clusters whose closest pair is nearest, so a thin bridge of intermediate points can chain two genuinely distinct clusters into one — the algorithm follows the chain rather than seeing the gap. It's the reason single linkage can find elongated, non-convex shapes (a real strength, similar in spirit to DBSCAN) and also why it's fragile to noise: one badly-placed point merges everything. Complete and Ward linkage don't chain, at the cost of preferring compact, roughly spherical clusters.

9.4 DBSCAN & HDBSCAN ⭐

Density-based clustering — the answer when clusters aren't spherical, when you don't know k, and when you want “this point is noise” to be a valid output.

Why DBSCAN fails on varying density — and what HDBSCAN does

DBSCAN uses a single global eps. If one cluster is dense and another sparse, no single value works: small eps shatters the sparse cluster into noise, large eps merges the dense ones together. HDBSCAN removes eps entirely — it builds a hierarchy across all density levels (a minimum spanning tree on mutual reachability distance), then extracts the clusters that persist most stably across that hierarchy. That's why it's the sensible modern default for exploratory clustering, and it needs essentially one parameter (min_cluster_size) that has an interpretable meaning.

Likely interview questions
Very commonk-means vs DBSCAN vs hierarchical — pick one for a given problem.

k-means for large data with roughly spherical, similarly-sized clusters and a k you can justify — it's O(nkd) per iteration and scales. DBSCAN when clusters are arbitrarily shaped, you don't know k, and you want noise labelled as noise rather than forced into a cluster — but it struggles with varying density, which is what HDBSCAN fixes and why HDBSCAN is my default for exploration. Hierarchical/Ward when you want a dendrogram you can cut at any level, or a non-Euclidean distance, and n is small enough for O(n²). And GMM when you want soft assignments and elliptical clusters.

PracticalHow do you choose eps for DBSCAN?

The standard heuristic: compute each point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (with k = min_samples), sort those distances descending, and plot them — the knee of that curve is a reasonable eps, because it's where distances start growing rapidly, i.e. where you leave dense regions. Set min_samples from domain knowledge (a common rule of thumb is 2·dim). Honestly though, if you find yourself agonising over eps, that's the signal to use HDBSCAN, which eliminates the parameter by considering all density levels at once.

9.5 Gaussian mixtures and EM ⭐

The probabilistic generalisation of k-means: soft assignments, elliptical clusters, and a likelihood you can use for model selection.

A GMM models the data as a mixture of k Gaussians with weights πₖ, means μₖ and covariances Σₖ. It is fitted by EM:

  • E-step: compute each point's responsibility γᵢₖ ∝ πₖ·N(xᵢ | μₖ, Σₖ) — a soft assignment summing to 1 across clusters.
  • M-step: re-estimate πₖ, μₖ, Σₖ as responsibility-weighted averages.

Each iteration cannot decrease the likelihood, so it converges — again, to a local optimum, so multiple restarts matter. k-means is the limiting case of a GMM with spherical, equal covariances and responsibilities hardened to 0/1, which is a satisfying connection to be able to state.

covariance_typeShapeParameters per component
sphericalCircular, one variance1 — closest to k-means
diagAxis-aligned ellipsep
tiedSame ellipse shape for all componentsp(p+1)/2 shared — the LDA assumption (§6.3)
fullArbitrary ellipse per componentp(p+1)/2 each — most flexible, most data-hungry
The GMM's real advantage: BIC

Because a GMM is a probabilistic model, it has a likelihood — so you can select k (and the covariance type) with BIC or AIC, which penalise the likelihood by parameter count. That is a genuinely principled model-selection criterion rather than a heuristic like the elbow, and it's the single strongest argument for reaching for a GMM when “how many clusters?” actually matters. The other advantage: soft assignments — “this customer is 70% segment A, 30% segment B” is often more useful and more honest than a hard label.

Likely interview questions
Deep diveDerive the EM update for a Gaussian mixture, at a high level.

E-step: given current parameters, compute each point's responsibility for each component, γᵢₖ = πₖN(xᵢ|μₖ,Σₖ) / Σⱼ πⱼN(xᵢ|μⱼ,Σⱼ) — the posterior probability that point i came from component k. M-step: re-estimate parameters as responsibility-weighted statistics: πₖ = Σᵢγᵢₖ/n, μₖ = Σᵢγᵢₖxᵢ/Σᵢγᵢₖ, and Σₖ the weighted covariance. Each iteration maximises a lower bound on the log-likelihood, so the likelihood cannot decrease — which guarantees convergence to a local optimum.

ConceptualHow does a GMM relate to k-means?

k-means is a limiting case of a GMM: take spherical covariances, all equal, and shrink the variance toward zero — then the responsibilities harden to 0/1 and the EM steps become exactly Lloyd's assign-and-update. So a GMM generalises k-means in three ways: soft assignments instead of hard, elliptical clusters instead of spherical, and a likelihood that lets you use BIC for model selection. The cost is more parameters (especially with full covariance) and therefore more data needed.

9.6 PCA ⭐⭐

Two questions dominate: why must you scale first, and how do you choose the number of components. The widget answers the first one by demonstration.

interactive PCA — and what happens if you forget to scale
—PC1 explained var.
—PC2 explained var.
—PC1 angle

The covariance matrix and its eigenvectors are computed exactly (closed form for 2×2). Set the mode to raw and stretch x₁: PC1 swings to align with it and its explained-variance share races toward 100% — not because x₁ is more informative, but because it is measured in smaller units. Switch to standardised and the axes stop moving.

What PCA actually computes

Centre the data, then find the direction v maximising the variance of the projection vᵀx. That variance is vᵀΣv, and maximising it subject to ‖v‖ = 1 gives the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix Σ, with the eigenvalue equal to the variance along it. Subsequent components are the next eigenvectors, orthogonal to those before. Equivalently — and this is how it's actually computed — they are the right singular vectors of the centred data matrix, because X = UΣVᵀ ⇒ XᵀX = VΣ²Vᵀ. SVD is preferred because forming XᵀX squares the condition number (§5.1).

Choosing the number of componentsMethodNote
Explained variance thresholdSmallest k with cumulative ratio ≥ 0.90/0.95Most common; the threshold is arbitrary
Scree plot elbowWhere eigenvalues level offSame subjectivity as the k-means elbow
Downstream CVTreat n_components as a hyperparameter in the pipelineThe right answer when PCA feeds a supervised model
Kaiser criterionKeep components with eigenvalue > 1 (on standardised data)Crude; tends to over-retain
Parallel analysisCompare eigenvalues against random dataMore principled, less common
PCA is unsupervised — and that's a risk

PCA maximises variance, which has nothing to do with your target. A low-variance direction can be the one that separates your classes, and PCA will happily discard it — which is exactly the LDA contrast in §6.3. So using PCA before a classifier is a bet that variance and signal align; validate it by cross-validating n_components against downstream performance rather than against explained variance. And relatives worth naming: TruncatedSVD/LSA works on sparse matrices without centring them (so it doesn't destroy sparsity), Kernel PCA for non-linear structure, and NMF for non-negative parts-based factors that are often far more interpretable.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Explain PCA. Why must you scale first? How do you choose the number of components?

PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance — the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, equivalently the right singular vectors of the centred data matrix, with eigenvalues giving the variance along each. Scaling: variance carries units, so an unscaled feature measured in smaller units dominates the first component purely by measurement convention rather than by information content. Components: cumulative explained-variance threshold, the scree elbow, or — best when PCA feeds a supervised model — cross-validate n_components against downstream performance. Caveat to add: components are linear mixtures of every feature, so interpretability is limited, and PCA is unsupervised so it can discard the direction that matters.

Deep diveShow PCA's equivalence to the SVD of the centred data matrix.

Write the centred data as X = UΣVᵀ. Then XᵀX = VΣᵀUᵀUΣVᵀ = VΣ²Vᵀ, which is exactly an eigendecomposition of (n−1) times the sample covariance matrix. So the right singular vectors V are the principal components, and the eigenvalues are σᵢ²/(n−1). Why it matters practically: computing the SVD of X directly is numerically better than forming XᵀX, which squares the condition number — the same argument as in §5.1 for least squares.

DesignWould you use PCA before a gradient boosting model?

Usually no. PCA gives you dense linear combinations of every feature, which destroys two things a GBM is good at: interpretability (a split on "0.3·age − 0.7·income + …" means nothing to anyone) and its ability to handle features individually, including missing values and monotonic constraints. GBMs also aren't hurt by correlated features or moderate dimensionality the way distance-based methods are. Where PCA does help before a GBM: genuinely huge feature counts where training time is the binding constraint, or dense embedding blocks that you want to compress before mixing with tabular columns.

9.7 t-SNE, UMAP and the trap ⭐

One caveat here should be volunteered unprompted, because it separates people who have read about t-SNE from people who have used it.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ PCA vs t-SNE vs UMAP — and what's the trap with t-SNE plots?

PCA is linear, deterministic, reversible and safe as a preprocessing step. t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear, stochastic visualisation tools. The trap: in a t-SNE plot, distances between clusters and the sizes of clusters are not meaningful — it optimises a neighbour-probability KL divergence that weights near pairs heavily and effectively ignores far ones, and the layout depends strongly on perplexity. UMAP preserves more global structure and is much faster, but is still stochastic. Never say "these clusters are similar because they're close on the plot".

DesignCan you use UMAP output as features for a classifier?

With real care. Three problems. It's stochastic, so the embedding isn't reproducible without fixing seeds and it changes if you refit. It's unsupervised and non-parametric in spirit — you must use the fitted transformer's transform on new data, and doing that correctly inside a pipeline (fitted on training folds only) is essential or you've leaked. And it discards information that the classifier might have used. If the goal is dimensionality reduction for a supervised model, PCA inside the pipeline is safer and LDA is more targeted; if the goal is a picture for humans, UMAP is excellent and shouldn't be feeding a model at all.

9.8 Anomaly detection ⭐

The hard part is never the algorithm. It is that you usually have no labels, so you cannot evaluate — which changes how you should design the whole system.

MethodIdeaStrength / weakness
z-score / MahalanobisDistance from the centreFast, interpretable; assumes a (multivariate) Gaussian
Isolation ForestAnomalies need fewer random splits to isolateScales well, few assumptions, no distance metric needed — the practical default
LOFLocal density relative to neighboursFinds local anomalies a global method misses; needs a neighbourhood size; poor in high dimensions
One-Class SVMBoundary around the normal regionSensitive to ν and γ; expensive; often outperformed by IForest
AutoencoderReconstruction errorGood for high-dimensional structured signals; needs enough clean data to learn "normal"
Three distinctions that make this answer senior

(1) Novelty vs outlier detection: novelty detection trains on clean data and flags anything new; outlier detection assumes the training data is already contaminated. Scikit-learn's API distinguishes them, and choosing the wrong one silently changes what “normal” means. (2) The contamination parameter is a prior you are asserting, not something the data tells you — and it directly sets your alert volume, so it's a business decision dressed as a hyperparameter. (3) If you have labels, use a classifier. Anomaly detection is what you do when you cannot frame it as highly-imbalanced classification; framing it that way when you can is throwing away supervision.

Likely interview questions
Very commonHow would you detect anomalies with no labels?

Isolation Forest as the default — anomalies require fewer random splits to isolate, it scales well, needs no distance metric and makes few assumptions. LOF when anomalies are local (unusual relative to their neighbourhood rather than globally extreme). Mahalanobis for a fast Gaussian baseline, and an autoencoder for high-dimensional structured signals. Then the three things that matter more than the algorithm: contamination is a prior you're asserting and it sets your alert volume; novelty vs outlier detection are different settings; and if you have any labels, use a classifier instead.

DesignHow do you evaluate an anomaly detector with no labels?

You can't, directly — and saying so plainly is the right answer. What you can do: have a human review the top-k flagged cases and measure precision-at-k, which is expensive but real; check stability across time windows and resamples (a detector whose flagged set changes completely week to week isn't measuring anything); use proxy labels if any exist (confirmed incidents, refunds, manual escalations), even if partial and biased; and inject synthetic anomalies of known types to measure recall on those, while being explicit that it only covers the failure modes you thought of. In production I'd instrument the review outcome so labels accumulate and the problem gradually becomes supervised.

9.9 Association rules & matrix factorization

Still asked in retail and e-commerce interviews, and the metric that matters is the one people forget.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualSupport, confidence and lift — which matters and why?

Support is how often the itemset occurs — it prunes the search space but says nothing about association. Confidence is P(consequent | antecedent), which looks convincing and is misleading on its own: if 40% of all baskets contain milk, then 40% confidence for {bread}→{milk} means bread told you nothing. Lift divides confidence by the base rate, so lift = 1 means independence, > 1 a genuine positive association, < 1 substitutes. Lift is the one that actually detects a relationship — and it's the one people omit.

GotchaWhat's the difference between explicit and implicit feedback in matrix factorization?

Explicit feedback is a stated preference — a star rating — where a missing entry genuinely means "unknown" and you fit only the observed ones. Implicit feedback is behaviour: views, clicks, purchases. The critical difference is that a zero is not a negative — not buying something usually means you never saw it. So implicit methods (ALS with confidence weighting, BPR) fit all entries but weight observed interactions with high confidence and unobserved ones with low confidence, rather than treating absence as dislike. Getting that backwards is the classic implicit-feedback mistake.
