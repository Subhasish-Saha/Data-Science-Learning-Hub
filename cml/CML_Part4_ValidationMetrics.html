Part 4 of 7 · Validation & Metrics

Where interviews are actually lost Cross-validation and its variants, nested CV, modern hyperparameter search, honest model comparison — then the metrics level: ROC vs PR, thresholds as a business decision, calibration, and the right order of operations for imbalance.

This is the part most candidates fail, and they fail it in a recognisable way: they report a number the metric cannot support. Accuracy under 1:1000 imbalance. ROC-AUC quoted as if it said something about calibration. A tuned score reported from the same CV it was tuned on. A 0.5 threshold treated as a decision rather than a default. Each of those has a one-sentence correction and this part gives you all of them, with the tooling that makes them cheap — TunedThresholdClassifierCV, reliability diagrams, purged time-series splits, and the imbalance order of operations: threshold first, class weights second, resampling last and only if needed.

4.3 Hyperparameter optimization

The search space usually matters more than the search algorithm. But the algorithm question gets asked, so here is the picture that answers it.

interactive Grid vs random search — the Bergstra & Bengio result
—grid · distinct values tried
—random · distinct values tried
—grid · best score found
—random · best score found

The response surface here depends almost entirely on one of the two hyperparameters — which is the empirically usual situation. With a budget of 16, a 4×4 grid tries only 4 distinct values of the parameter that matters; random search tries 16. That is the whole Bergstra & Bengio argument, and it gets stronger as dimensions grow.

StrategyHow it worksReach for it when
Grid searchExhaustive product of values2–3 parameters with genuinely few sensible values
Random searchSample from distributionsThe sane default — better per unit compute when few parameters matter
Bayesian / TPESurrogate model + acquisition function (Optuna)Expensive evaluations, many parameters. Note it behaves like random search until its model warms up
Successive halving / HyperbandStart many trials cheaply, kill the losers, escalate the survivorsHalvingRandomSearchCV — excellent when a cheap fidelity (fewer trees, less data) predicts the full one
Early stoppingStop a single fit when validation stallsAlways, for boosting and NNs — orthogonal to the search itself
Search spaces beat search algorithms

Use log-uniform distributions for learning rates, regularization strengths and anything else spanning orders of magnitude — a uniform sample over [0.0001, 1] puts 90% of its mass above 0.1, which is almost never where the answer is. Bound the space with domain knowledge rather than searching absurd regions. Seed the sampler for reproducibility. Prune unpromising trials. And the rule that matters most: tune inside the CV loop, never on the test set — otherwise §4.2 applies.

4.6 Classification metrics ⭐

The level where interviews are lost. Every metric here answers a different question, and quoting one that cannot support your claim is the single most common failure.

All six numbers are computed exactly from 4,000 simulated scores. Three experiments. (1) Drop the base rate to 2% and watch accuracy stay above 0.97 at every threshold while precision collapses. (2) Compare ROC-AUC and PR-AUC as the base rate falls — ROC-AUC barely moves; PR-AUC tracks reality. (3) Notice that ROC-AUC and PR-AUC don't change at all when you move the threshold: they are threshold-free, which is exactly why neither tells you what your deployed system will do.

4.7 Regression metrics

Same principle: pick from the decision. Is a 10-unit error twice as bad as a 5-unit error, or four times?

MetricOptimises / penalisesUse whenWatch out
RMSEThe mean; penalises large errors quadraticallyBig misses are disproportionately costlyOutlier-sensitive; in target units, which is good for communication
MAEThe median; linear penalty“Typical error” is the business quantityNot differentiable at zero (rarely a practical issue)
RMSLERelative error; punishes under-prediction moreTargets spanning orders of magnitudeRequires non-negative targets
MAPEPercentage errorCommunicating to a business audienceUndefined/explodes near zero, and asymmetric — over-prediction is capped at 100%, under-prediction is not
MASEError ÷ seasonal-naive errorComparing across many series of different scaleNeeds a defined seasonal period
PinballA specific quantilePrediction intervals, asymmetric costsReport coverage alongside it
R² / adjusted R²Variance explainedCommunicating fitAlways rises with features; test R² can be negative; not comparable across datasets
RMSE vs MAE, stated as a decision

If the cost of an error grows faster than linearly — a stock-out that cascades, a structural failure — RMSE matches the business and you should optimise squared loss. If cost grows roughly linearly and you have outliers you cannot clean, MAE matches it. And note the consequence for the model, not just the report: optimising MSE fits the conditional mean, optimising MAE fits the conditional median. On a right-skewed target those are materially different numbers.

4.9 Choosing a threshold ⭐

0.5 is a default, not a decision. It is the right threshold only when false positives and false negatives cost the same and the classes are balanced — which is almost never.

4.10 Calibration ⭐

Among the cases you scored 0.7, roughly 70% should be positive. If they aren't, every expected-value calculation downstream is wrong.

interactive Reliability diagram — miscalibrate it, then fix it
—ECE
—Brier score
—log loss
—ROC-AUC

Platt scaling is fitted by real gradient descent on a one-parameter-pair logistic model; isotonic regression is a real pool-adjacent-violators implementation. Two things to notice. ROC-AUC does not move under Platt scaling — it is a monotone transform, so the ranking is untouched while the probabilities change completely. And with a small calibration set, isotonic starts to overfit: its ECE looks good but its log loss gets worse.

4.11 Imbalanced classification ⭐⭐

There is a correct order of operations, and reaching for SMOTE first is the single most common wrong answer in classical ML interviews.

interactive ROC-AUC flatters, PR-AUC doesn't
—ROC-AUC
—PR-AUC
—PR baseline (= base rate)
—lift over baseline

The classifier is exactly as good at every setting — the score distributions never change, only the mix. Drag the base rate from 50% down to 1% and watch ROC-AUC stay essentially flat while PR-AUC falls off a cliff. That divergence is the entire “ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC” question, and the reason is structural: the ROC's x-axis is the false-positive rate, which is normalised by a huge negative count, so a flood of false positives barely registers.

Metrics under imbalance

Never accuracy. Report PR-AUC / average precision, per-class recall, and an F-β aligned to your costs (β > 1 when misses hurt more). Be explicitly wary of ROC-AUC's optimism — the widget above shows exactly why. And always show the confusion matrix at the threshold you actually intend to deploy, because that is the only artefact that tells the business how many cases will land in the queue tomorrow.

4.12 Interview focus for Part 4

Twelve questions from the part where most classical ML interviews are actually decided.

Part 4 · validation, metrics, thresholds and imbalance
1. ⭐ Accuracy is 95% and the model is useless. Explain.

Ask for the base rate. If 95% of rows are negative, predicting “negative” for everything scores 95% while catching nothing — accuracy has become a majority-class detector. Report instead: the confusion matrix at the deployed threshold, per-class recall, and PR-AUC / average precision. And note the second failure: accuracy is also blind to the cost difference between a false positive and a false negative.

2. ⭐ ROC-AUC vs PR-AUC — when do you prefer which?

ROC-AUC plots TPR against FPR and is a good summary of pure ranking quality when classes are roughly balanced. Its weakness under imbalance is structural: FPR divides by a huge negative count, so thousands of false positives barely move the x-axis — it flatters rare-positive problems. PR-AUC / average precision plots precision against recall, so it responds directly to false positives among your alerts, and its baseline is the base rate rather than 0.5. Prefer PR-AUC when positives are rare and you care about the alert queue. Also state that neither says anything about calibration.

3. What does ROC-AUC actually mean, probabilistically?

It is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative: P(score(x⁺) > score(x⁻)), with ties counted as a half. It equals the normalised Mann-Whitney U statistic. That is why it is threshold-free and invariant to any monotone transform of the scores — including Platt scaling, which is why calibration leaves it unchanged.

4. Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report?

Micro pools TP/FP/FN across all classes, so it is dominated by frequent classes — and in single-label multi-class it is exactly equal to accuracy. Macro averages per-class F1 with equal weight, so a rare class counts as much as a common one; report it when the rare classes matter. Weighted averages by support and mostly reproduces micro. The real answer is: report the one that matches the decision, and say which one you are reporting.

5. ⭐ What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?

A model is calibrated if, among cases predicted at p, a fraction p are actually positive. Measure with a reliability diagram, ECE, and the Brier score. Fix with Platt scaling (fit a sigmoid on held-out scores — parametric, works with little data), isotonic regression (non-parametric, more flexible, needs more data and can overfit), or Venn-Abers. Always calibrate on held-out data, and — the 2026 nuance — verify it helped, because a recent benchmark found Platt and isotonic can degrade proper-scoring performance for strong tabular models. It matters whenever the probability itself is used: expected value, pricing, ranking by risk, cost-based thresholds.

6. Which models are typically miscalibrated, and in which direction?

Well-regularized logistic regression is usually close to calibrated (log loss is a proper scoring rule and it optimises exactly that). Random Forests are under-confident at the extremes — averaging many trees pulls predictions away from 0 and 1. Boosted trees are typically over-confident. Naive Bayes is wildly overconfident because it double-counts correlated evidence. SVMs produce no probabilities at all natively. Rule of thumb: check, never assume.

7. ⭐ How do you choose a decision threshold?

Not 0.5. Three legitimate sources: (1) a cost matrix — pick the t minimising expected cost, which for costs c_FP and c_FN is around c_FP/(c_FP+c_FN) for a calibrated model; (2) an operating constraint — “analysts can review 200 cases a day” sets the alert volume and therefore t; (3) a guarantee — the lowest t meeting a contractual precision floor. Tune it on a validation set (TunedThresholdClassifierCV), never on test, and consider abstention for low-confidence cases.

8. ⭐ How do you handle class imbalance? Give me your order of operations.

(0) Characterise it — 1:10 or 1:10,000? separated or overlapping? (1) Tune the decision threshold on a strong baseline: cheapest experiment, no new failure modes, often sufficient. (2) Class weights / scale_pos_weight: no synthetic rows, no leakage risk — but it decalibrates, so recalibrate if you use the probabilities. (3) Only then resampling — SMOTE, under-sampling, Tomek links, BalancedRandomForest/EasyEnsemble — always inside the pipeline. Throughout: PR-AUC, per-class recall and cost-aligned F-β, never accuracy.

9. What's wrong with applying SMOTE before the train-test split? And when is SMOTE actively harmful?

Before the split it leaks: synthetic minority points are interpolated from real rows, and some of those real rows end up in validation — so validation rows are partly reconstructions of training data. You get an excellent CV score and a production collapse. Actively harmful when classes overlap or labels are noisy: interpolating between minority points that sit in the majority region manufactures ambiguous synthetic examples right on the boundary, which inflates false positives. On clean tabular data, inverse-frequency weighting gives near-identical results and is simpler and leak-proof.

10. ⭐ Explain k-fold CV and when stratification is necessary.

Partition into k folds; for each, train on k−1 and evaluate on the held-out one; average the k scores and report the standard deviation too. Every row is tested exactly once, which gives a lower-variance estimate than a single hold-out. Stratify whenever the target is imbalanced or you have few rows — otherwise a fold can contain very few (or zero) positives, which makes both training and the metric unstable. Add: stratification is orthogonal to grouping and to time, so it does not protect you from those (§1.15).

11. What is nested cross-validation and why does it exist?

Tuning hyperparameters on the same CV you report gives an optimistically biased estimate — the reported score is the maximum over many noisy estimates, so it is inflated even if all settings were equally good. Nested CV puts hyperparameter selection in an inner loop run separately inside each outer training fold, so no outer test fold ever influences the selection. Cost: k_outer × k_inner fits. It estimates the performance of the whole procedure including tuning, not of one model. Pragmatic alternative: a single held-out test set you touch exactly once.

12. Your model is 0.4% better. Is it better?

Three checks. Statistical: compare across the same folds (paired), give a bootstrap CI on the difference, and use a paired permutation test or the corrected resampled t-test — naive t-tests over folds are anti-conservative because training sets overlap. Selection: if this is the best of 40 variants, some of that 0.4% is luck. Practical: what does it cost in latency, complexity, retraining and explainability? A 0.4% AUC gain that adds a service dependency is usually not worth shipping — and saying so is the senior answer.
