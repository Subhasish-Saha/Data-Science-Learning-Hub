Part 6 of 17 · Distance & Probability Models

Four models, four different assumptions about the world kNN as a pure bias-variance dial with mandatory scaling; Naive Bayes and why a false assumption still classifies correctly; LDA and QDA as generative classifiers and as dimensionality reduction; and support vector machines from the margin through the soft-margin C to the kernel trick and the dual.

None of these are likely to be your production model on tabular data in 2026, and all four are asked about constantly — because each one isolates a concept with nothing else in the way. kNN isolates distance, scaling and the bias-variance dial. Naive Bayes isolates the difference between a correct argmax and a correct probability. LDA isolates supervised versus unsupervised projection. SVM isolates constrained optimization and the kernel trick — and it is the topic where candidates most often recite “it maps to a higher dimension” without being able to say where in the maths that happens. It happens in the dual, and this part shows you exactly where.

6.1 k-Nearest Neighbours ⭐

Almost never the production answer, and almost always a good interview question — because it isolates distance, scaling and the bias-variance dial with nothing else in the way.

interactive The k dial is a bias-variance dial
—train accuracy
—test accuracy
—gap

Real kNN, computed live over a 96 × 40 grid. Two things to try. k = 1: train accuracy is exactly 1.000 (every point is its own nearest neighbour) while test accuracy is much lower — the purest possible demonstration of overfitting. Then stretch x₂: as the scale grows, distance is dominated by one feature and the boundary degenerates into stripes. Nothing about the data changed — only the units.

The mechanics, and the costs

PropertyDetailConsequence
Lazy learningTraining just stores the dataTraining is free; inference is O(n·p) per query — the opposite cost profile to every other model, and usually disqualifying in production
Scaling mandatoryThe prediction is a distanceA feature in larger units dominates entirely — you end up doing 1-D kNN on whichever column has the biggest numbers
kThe bias-variance dialk=1: zero training error, maximum variance. k=n: the majority-class baseline. Rule of thumb √n, but tune it
Weightingweights='distance'Closer neighbours count more — smooths the boundary without raising k as much
Index structuresKD-tree, ball-treeHelp in low dimensions only; above roughly 15–20 features they degrade to brute-force scanning (§2.7)
Tie-breakingEven k in binary problemsUse odd k, or distance weighting, to avoid arbitrary ties
kNN still earns its place in three situations

(1) As a baseline on small, low-dimensional, well-scaled data — it's non-parametric, so it captures arbitrary boundaries with no assumptions. (2) As a recommendation neighbourhood — “customers similar to you also bought” is literally kNN, and the interpretability of “here are the five nearest examples” is a real feature. (3) As an imputation method (KNNImputer), where the same locality logic is exactly what you want. What it isn't is a scalable classifier for wide tabular data.

Likely interview questions
Very commonHow does kNN work, and why is scaling mandatory?

Store the training set; to predict, find the k nearest rows by some distance and take their majority vote or mean. Scaling is mandatory because the prediction is a distance computation — a feature measured in larger units dominates it entirely, so income in pounds swamps age in years and you're effectively doing 1-D nearest neighbour on income. k is a pure bias-variance dial: k=1 has zero training error and maximum variance; k=n is the majority-class baseline. It also suffers most from the curse of dimensionality, and its cost profile is inverted — free training, expensive inference.

ConceptualWhy does k = 1 always give 100% training accuracy?

Because every training point is its own nearest neighbour — distance zero — so it predicts its own label perfectly, by construction. That's why training accuracy is a completely uninformative metric for kNN, and it's the cleanest illustration in all of ML of why you must evaluate out-of-sample. The corollary is a nice diagnostic: if someone reports impressive training accuracy for a kNN model, they haven't understood the model.

DesignWould you ever deploy kNN in production?

Rarely as a classifier, because inference is O(n·p) per query — you carry the entire training set into production and pay for it on every request, which is the opposite of what you want. But yes in three shapes: as a recommendation neighbourhood (with an ANN index like HNSW to make it fast); as a baseline during development; and as KNNImputer inside a pipeline. If someone proposes it as the main model on wide tabular data, I'd point at latency, memory, and the curse of dimensionality, and suggest a GBM instead.

6.2 Naive Bayes ⭐

A model built on an assumption that is essentially always false, which nonetheless classifies well. The interesting question — and the one asked — is why.

VariantAssumes each feature is…Use for
GaussianNBContinuous, Gaussian within each classSmall tabular problems; a fast sanity baseline
MultinomialNBA countText with BoW/TF-IDF — see the NLP guide
BernoulliNBBinary presence/absenceShort text; binary indicator features
ComplementNBA count, with an imbalance correctionImbalanced text classification — often beats MultinomialNB there
CategoricalNBCategoricalPurely categorical tabular data
Likely interview questions
Very commonWhy does Naive Bayes work despite a false independence assumption?

Because classification needs the correct argmax, not correct probabilities. Correlated features are counted as independent evidence, so their effect is multiplied and the posterior is driven toward 0 or 1 — but the class ordering usually survives, and ordering is all a classifier needs. Consequences to state: it is badly calibrated and overconfident, so never use its predict_proba in an expected-value calculation without calibration. Add Laplace smoothing so an unseen feature-class pair doesn't zero the entire product, and compute in log space to avoid numerical underflow.

Deep diveWhat exactly does "conditional independence" mean here — and is it different from independence?

Very different, and it's the crux. Naive Bayes assumes P(x₁,x₂|y) = P(x₁|y)·P(x₂|y) — the features are independent given the class, not marginally. Two features can be strongly correlated overall while being nearly independent within each class, in which case the assumption is fine. The assumption fails when features remain correlated after conditioning on the class — which is the usual case with, say, duplicated or highly redundant columns, and it's why removing near-duplicate features can noticeably improve a Naive Bayes model.

PracticalWhy is Laplace smoothing necessary?

Because the model multiplies per-feature likelihoods, a single zero destroys the whole product: if a category never co-occurred with a class in training, P(xⱼ|y) = 0 makes the entire posterior zero regardless of how much evidence the other features provide. Additive (Laplace/Lidstone) smoothing adds α to every count so nothing is ever exactly zero. It's the same idea as the smoothing in target encoding (§3.5) — pull small-sample estimates toward a prior — and α is a genuine hyperparameter worth tuning, not a formality.

6.3 LDA & QDA ⭐

Worth knowing mostly for one question — LDA vs PCA — but the classifier itself has a nice property: it is the generative counterpart to logistic regression, and comparing them illuminates both.

LDA and logistic regression fit the same boundary shape differently

Both produce a linear decision boundary. LDA gets there generatively — model each class as a Gaussian with shared covariance, then apply Bayes' rule — while logistic regression gets there discriminatively, optimising the conditional likelihood directly. Consequence: when the Gaussian assumption roughly holds, LDA is more data-efficient (it's the §4.7 small-sample argument); when it doesn't, logistic regression is more robust because it never made the assumption. LDA is also more sensitive to outliers, since they distort the covariance estimate.

Likely interview questions
Very commonLDA vs PCA — what's the difference in objective?

PCA is unsupervised and maximises total variance; it never sees the labels, so it can discard exactly the direction that separates the classes if that direction happens to be low-variance. LDA is supervised and maximises the ratio of between-class scatter to within-class scatter, giving at most k−1 components for k classes. Both are linear projections; only one is trying to help your classifier. Practical note: LDA-as-preprocessing is fitted on training data only, exactly like any other learned transform.

ConceptualLDA vs QDA — when would you choose each?

LDA assumes a shared covariance across classes → linear boundary, few parameters, more data-efficient. QDA gives each class its own covariance → quadratic boundary, far more parameters (k·p(p+1)/2), and it needs substantially more data per class to estimate them stably. Choose QDA when you have plenty of data per class and evidence that class covariances genuinely differ (visibly different cluster shapes); otherwise LDA, whose extra bias buys a large variance reduction. Regularized discriminant analysis interpolates between them if you want to tune it.

Deep diveLDA and logistic regression both give a linear boundary. What's the difference?

How they get there. LDA is generative: model P(x|y) as Gaussian with shared covariance, model P(y), apply Bayes' rule. Logistic regression is discriminative: model P(y|x) directly by maximising the conditional likelihood. When the Gaussian assumption holds, LDA is more efficient — it converges to good performance with less data, the §4.7 argument. When it doesn't (skewed features, outliers, categorical inputs), logistic regression is more robust because it never made that assumption. LDA also gives you a supervised projection for free, which logistic regression doesn't.

6.4 Support Vector Machines — the margin ⭐

Start with the geometry, because everything else — the dual, the kernel, C — is a consequence of it.

Among all hyperplanes that separate the classes, the SVM picks the one with the largest margin — the greatest distance to the nearest point of either class. The intuition for why that generalises: a boundary sitting in the widest possible gap is the least likely to be flipped by a slightly different sample.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhat is the margin, and why maximise it?

The margin is the distance from the decision boundary to the nearest training point of either class — 2/‖w‖ for a canonical hyperplane. Maximising it means choosing the boundary sitting in the widest possible gap, which is intuitively the least likely to be flipped by a slightly different sample, and formally gives a generalisation bound that depends on the margin rather than the dimensionality. Maximising 2/‖w‖ is equivalent to minimising ½‖w‖², which turns it into a convex quadratic program with a unique solution.

ConceptualWhat are support vectors, and why do they matter?

The training points that lie exactly on the margin boundaries (plus, in the soft-margin case, those violating them) — the ones with non-zero dual coefficients. They matter because they alone determine the solution: delete every other training point and refit, and you get the identical boundary. That gives the model a sparsity no other classifier here has, keeps prediction cheap (you only evaluate kernels against support vectors), and is why the generalisation bound scales with their number rather than with the feature count — which is what makes mapping into an infinite-dimensional space via a kernel a safe thing to do.

6.5 Soft margin and C

Real data isn't separable. The soft margin allows violations and charges for them — and C sets the price.

CPenalty for violationsMarginSupport vectorsBias / variance
LargeExpensive — few violations toleratedNarrowFewLow bias, high variance — can overfit badly
SmallCheap — many violations toleratedWideManyHigh bias, low variance — can underfit

Note the connection back to §4.4: the SVM objective ½‖w‖² + C·Σξᵢ is exactly a regularized loss — ‖w‖² is an L2 penalty and Σξᵢ is the hinge loss. So C is the inverse regularization strength, which is why sklearn uses the same letter in LogisticRegression. Small C = strong regularization, the opposite convention to alpha.

C and gamma interact — tune them jointly

With an RBF kernel, gamma controls how far a single training example's influence reaches (large gamma = tight, wiggly boundaries) while C controls violation tolerance. They pull in overlapping directions, so tuning one then the other finds a poor optimum — search them jointly on a log-spaced 2-D grid, which is the classic textbook example of why a grid over a 2-D space is sometimes genuinely appropriate (§11.4). And scale your features first, or gamma's meaning changes per feature.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhat does C do in an SVM?

It sets the price of violating the margin. Large C makes violations expensive, so the optimiser prefers a narrow margin that classifies training points correctly — low bias, high variance, prone to overfitting. Small C tolerates violations, giving a wider margin and a smoother boundary — higher bias, lower variance. Structurally, the objective ½‖w‖² + C·Σξᵢ is an L2 penalty plus hinge loss, so C is the inverse regularization strength — small C means strong regularization, the opposite convention to alpha.

PracticalHow do you tune C and gamma?

Jointly, on a log-spaced 2-D grid — typically C ∈ [10⁻², 10³] and gamma ∈ [10⁻⁴, 10¹] — because they interact: high gamma with high C gives a wildly overfitted boundary, and tuning them sequentially finds a poor optimum. Scale features first or gamma's meaning varies per feature; sklearn's gamma='scale' default at least adapts to feature variance. And be aware of the cost: SVM training is roughly O(n²)–O(n³), so a full grid on a large dataset is genuinely expensive — which is an argument for random or Bayesian search (§11.4).

6.6 The kernel trick ⭐⭐

The topic where candidates most often recite “it maps to a higher dimension” without being able to say where in the maths that happens. It happens in the dual.

interactive The kernel trick, watched happening

In one dimension the two classes are interleaved and no threshold separates them. Add a second coordinate x² and a straight line does. That's the idea — and the “trick” is that the SVM's dual objective only ever needs inner products ⟨φ(x), φ(x′)⟩, which a kernel K(x,x′) computes directly.

Where exactly the kernel enters

The primal optimises over w, which lives in the feature space — so if φ maps to a million dimensions, w has a million components and you cannot store it. The Lagrangian dual instead optimises over one multiplier αᵢ per training point, and the objective depends on the data only through inner products:

max Σαᵢ − ½ΣΣ αᵢαⱼ yᵢyⱼ ⟨xᵢ, xⱼ⟩   subject to   0 ≤ αᵢ ≤ C, Σαᵢyᵢ = 0

Replace ⟨xᵢ, xⱼ⟩ with K(xᵢ, xⱼ) and you are optimising in the feature space without ever constructing a vector in it. Prediction is the same: f(x) = Σ αᵢyᵢK(xᵢ,x) + b, summed only over support vectors. That substitution is the entire trick — and being able to point at the exact term is what separates a real answer from a memorised one.

KernelFormulaImplied feature spaceWhen
Linear⟨x,x′⟩The original spaceHigh-dimensional sparse data (text) — fast, and often enough
Polynomial(γ⟨x,x′⟩+r)ᵈAll monomials up to degree dExplicit interaction terms; sensitive to d
RBF / Gaussianexp(−γ‖x−x′‖²)Infinite-dimensionalThe default. gamma sets the influence radius
Sigmoidtanh(γ⟨x,x′⟩+r)—Rarely; not PSD for all parameters, so it can misbehave
Mercer's condition, briefly

Not any function of two arguments is a valid kernel. It must produce a positive semi-definite Gram matrix for every finite set of points — Mercer's condition — because that is exactly what guarantees it corresponds to an inner product in some feature space. If it doesn't, the optimisation is no longer convex and the geometric story collapses. This is the practical reason the sigmoid kernel is rarely used: it isn't PSD for all parameter choices.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Explain the kernel trick. Where exactly does the kernel enter?

In the dual. The primal optimises over w, which lives in the feature space and may be enormous or infinite. The Lagrangian dual optimises over one multiplier per training point and depends on the data only through inner products ⟨xᵢ,xⱼ⟩. Substituting K(xᵢ,xⱼ) for that term gives you the geometry of the mapped space without ever constructing φ(x) — and prediction, f(x) = Σαᵢyᵢ K(xᵢ,x) + b, is the same substitution. With the RBF kernel the implied space is infinite-dimensional, which is only safe because the generalisation bound depends on the margin and the number of support vectors, not on dimensionality.

Deep diveWhy does the dual exist at all — what's wrong with the primal?

Nothing, if you stay in the original space — a linear SVM is usually solved in the primal, which is why LinearSVC scales far better than SVC(kernel='linear'). The dual matters for two reasons. First, it's the only formulation that admits kernels, because it's the one where the data appears solely as inner products. Second, the dual has n variables while the primal has p+1, so the dual is preferable when p ≫ n and the primal when n ≫ p. The KKT conditions also give the support-vector characterisation directly: points with αᵢ = 0 are irrelevant to the solution.

GotchaCan you use kernels with models other than SVMs?

Yes — anywhere the algorithm can be written to touch the data only through inner products. Kernel ridge regression, kernel PCA (§9.6), Gaussian processes, and kernel k-means all exist. The catch is always the same: you need the n × n Gram matrix, which is O(n²) memory and O(n³) to decompose, so these methods stop being practical in the tens of thousands of rows. The workaround is random feature approximations (Nyström, random Fourier features) that approximate the kernel with an explicit low-dimensional map — which is how kernel methods scale in practice.

6.7 When to still use these in 2026

The honest positioning question. All four models in this part have been largely displaced on tabular data — and each still has a defensible niche.

ModelDisplaced becauseStill the right answer when
kNNO(n·p) inference; curse of dimensionality; needs scalingRecommendation neighbourhoods (with an ANN index); imputation; a quick non-parametric baseline; when “here are the 5 most similar cases” is the deliverable
Naive BayesIndependence assumption; badly calibratedVery little data; extremely high-dimensional sparse text; when you need a model in ten seconds; streaming updates (it's trivially incremental)
LDAGaussian + shared-covariance assumptionsSmall data with roughly Gaussian classes; as a supervised dimensionality reducer; when you want a generative model you can sample from
SVMO(n²)–O(n³) training; no native probabilities; needs scalingHigh-dimensional sparse features where LinearSVC is excellent and very fast; small-to-medium data with a clear margin; when you want a max-margin rule rather than a probability
The SVM probability trap

SVMs produce a signed distance, not a probability. Setting probability=True in sklearn runs Platt scaling with an internal 5-fold cross-validation — which makes fitting roughly 5× slower, can produce predict_proba that disagrees with predict (they're derived differently), and is often still poorly calibrated. If you need probabilities, that's a strong argument for using logistic regression or a GBM with explicit calibration instead.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhen would you still choose an SVM in 2026?

Three cases. High-dimensional sparse features — a linear SVM on text is excellent and LinearSVC is extremely fast, because it solves the primal and scales roughly linearly. Small-to-medium data with a clear margin, where the max-margin inductive bias genuinely helps. And when you want a decision rule rather than a probability. Otherwise gradient boosting wins on tabular data: it trains faster, needs no scaling, handles mixed types and missing values natively, and gives usable probabilities. I'd also flag that probability=True is a trap — it runs an internal 5-fold Platt calibration that's slow and often still poorly calibrated.

DesignYou have 2 million rows and want to try an SVM. What do you do?

Don't use SVC — kernel SVM training is roughly O(n²)–O(n³) and 2M rows is far past practical. Options: use LinearSVC or SGDClassifier(loss='hinge'), which solve the primal and scale near-linearly; approximate the kernel with Nyström or random Fourier features followed by a linear model, which gets most of the RBF benefit at linear cost; or subsample to fit a kernel SVM and check whether it beats the linear version at all. Honestly though, at 2M rows on tabular data I'd expect LightGBM to be both faster and better, and I'd say so.
