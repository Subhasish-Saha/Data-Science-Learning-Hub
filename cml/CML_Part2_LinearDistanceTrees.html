Part 2 of 7 · Linear, Distance & Trees

The model zoo, one honest paragraph each Linear and regularized regression, logistic regression as the interpretability baseline, GLMs for counts and claims, kNN, Naive Bayes, discriminant analysis and SVMs — then the decision tree, split by split.

Interviewers do not ask you to list models; they ask you to choose one and defend it. The through-line here is the trade-off each family makes. Linear models buy you interpretability, calibration and a coefficient you can put in an adverse-action notice — which is why credit scoring still runs on logistic regression. Distance- and kernel-based models buy you flexibility and charge you scaling sensitivity and O(n²) training. The decision tree is the most important model in this part despite being the weakest, because it is the building block of everything in Part 3 — and because its feature-importance trap is one of the most reliable senior filters in the subject.

2.1 Linear regression

The model everyone can write down and almost nobody can criticise properly. The criticism is the interview.

The closed form is w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, obtained by setting ∂(Xw−y)ᵀ(Xw−y)/∂w = 2Xᵀ(Xw−y) to zero. You would rarely compute it that way: XᵀX is singular under perfect multicollinearity, inverting it costs O(p³), and it squares the condition number, so numerically you use QR or SVD — which is exactly what LinearRegression does under the hood (it calls lstsq).

2.2 Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net

You met the geometry in §1.20. Here is what it looks like as α sweeps, and how you choose it.

When Elastic Net beats Lasso

With a group of correlated features, Lasso picks one essentially arbitrarily and zeroes the rest — refit on a slightly different sample and it picks a different one. That is fine for prediction and terrible for interpretation or stability. Elastic Net's L2 component makes correlated coefficients shrink together, so the group survives or dies as a group. Also relevant when p > n: Lasso can select at most n features; Elastic Net has no such limit.

2.3 Logistic regression ⭐

The interpretability and calibration baseline. Credit scoring runs on it for reasons that are worth being able to state.

interactive Log-odds → odds ratio → probability
—odds ratio exp(β)
—log-odds z
—probability σ(z)
—dp/dx at this point

The point this makes better than any sentence: a logistic coefficient is constant in log-odds and wildly non-constant in probability. Drag x to the tails and watch dp/dx collapse toward zero while the odds ratio never moves. That is why “a one-unit increase raises the probability by β” is wrong, and “multiplies the odds by exp(β)” is right.

Practical details worth having ready

No closed form — it is fitted by IRLS or gradient methods. In scikit-learn, C is the inverse regularization strength, so smaller C means more penalty (the opposite convention to alpha in Ridge — a classic slip). Multi-class comes as one-vs-rest or multinomial/softmax; multinomial is usually better calibrated because the probabilities are normalised jointly. And the headline reason it survives: it produces a coefficient you can put in an adverse-action notice, it is nearly calibrated out of the box when well-regularized, and it is trivial to monitor.

2.5 k-Nearest Neighbours

Almost never the production answer, and almost always a good interview question — because it isolates distance, scaling and the bias-variance dial with nothing else in the way.

interactive The k dial is a bias-variance dial
—train accuracy
—test accuracy
—gap

Real kNN, computed live over a 96 × 40 grid. Two things to try. k = 1: train accuracy is exactly 1.000 (every point is its own nearest neighbour) while test accuracy is much lower — the purest possible demonstration of overfitting. Then stretch x₂: as the scale grows, distance is dominated by one feature and the boundary degenerates into vertical stripes. Nothing about the data changed — only the units.

The properties that get asked

Lazy learning: training is free (store the data), inference is O(n·p) per query — the opposite cost profile to everything else, and usually disqualifying in production. Scaling is mandatory, for the reason the widget shows. k is the bias-variance dial: k=1 is maximum variance, k=n is the majority-class baseline. Curse of dimensionality hits it hardest (§1.9) — and KD-trees or ball-trees only help in low dimensions; above roughly 15–20 features they degrade to brute force.

2.8 Support Vector Machines ⭐

Mostly displaced on tabular data, and still a favourite interview topic — because margin, the dual and the kernel trick test whether you understand optimisation or only APIs.

interactive The kernel trick, watched happening

In one dimension the two classes are interleaved and no threshold separates them. Add a second coordinate x² and a straight line does. That is the whole idea — and the “trick” is that the SVM's dual objective only ever needs inner products ⟨φ(x), φ(x′)⟩, which a kernel K(x,x′) computes directly. You get the high-dimensional geometry without ever constructing the high-dimensional vector — and with the RBF kernel that space is infinite-dimensional.

The honest 2026 positioning

“When would you still choose an SVM?” — small-to-medium data with a clear margin, high-dimensional sparse features where a linear SVM is excellent and extremely fast (LinearSVC on text), and cases where you want a maximum-margin decision rule rather than a probability. Otherwise gradient boosting wins on tabular data, trains faster, needs no scaling and handles mixed types. Also: SVMs produce no probabilities natively — probability=True runs Platt scaling with an internal 5-fold CV, which is slow and often poorly calibrated anyway.

2.9 How a decision tree is built ⭐

Everything in Part 3 is made of these. Being able to compute a split by hand is the price of entry for the ensembles conversation.

interactive Find the split — Gini, entropy and information gain, live
—parent impurity
—left child
—right child
—information gain

Everything here is computed exactly: Gini = 1 − Σpᵢ², Entropy = −Σpᵢ log₂ pᵢ, and gain = H(parent) − Σ(nᵢ/n)·H(childᵢ). Drag the threshold and watch the gain curve; the tree's greedy algorithm simply evaluates every candidate threshold on every feature and takes the maximum. Switch criteria and notice how little the chosen split moves — which is the honest answer to “Gini or entropy?”

Gini or entropy? The honest answer

They almost always choose the same splits and produce trees of nearly identical accuracy. Gini is cheaper (no logarithm) and is sklearn's default; entropy penalises impure nodes slightly more aggressively. Saying “in practice it does not matter — I'd spend the tuning budget on depth and min_samples_leaf instead” is a better answer than inventing a difference.

2.11 Properties, good and bad — and the importance trap ⭐

Trees are the most-loved and most-misused model in tabular ML. The misuse is almost always feature_importances_.

⭐ The feature-importance trap

The default feature_importances_ is mean decrease in impurity, and it has two documented biases: it favours high-cardinality and continuous features (they offer more candidate split points, so more chances to reduce impurity by luck), and it is computed on training data, so it rewards features the model overfitted to. The demonstration everyone should have seen: add a column of pure random noise with many distinct values and it will show up as an important feature. Prefer permutation importance on a held-out set — and know that it is misleading with correlated features too, because permuting one of a correlated pair leaves the information available through the other. Three importances, three questions (§5.7).

2.12 Interview focus for Part 2

Eleven questions covering the whole model zoo. If you can answer these without hedging, the “which model would you use” conversation goes well.

Part 2 · linear, distance and tree models
1. State the assumptions of linear regression and what breaks when each fails.

Linearity (in the parameters) — fails ⇒ biased predictions, visible as curvature in the residual plot; fix with splines, polynomials or a different model. Independent errors — fails with time or clustered data ⇒ standard errors are wrong. Homoscedasticity — fails ⇒ inefficient estimates and wrong CIs; fix with a log/GLM or robust standard errors. Normal residuals — needed only for inference, not prediction. No perfect multicollinearity — fails ⇒ XᵀX singular, no unique solution; near-failure inflates coefficient variance (VIF > 5–10).

2. Why can't you use MSE as the loss for logistic regression?

Two reasons. Composed with a sigmoid, squared error is non-convex in the parameters, so gradient descent has no guarantee of finding the global optimum. And log loss is the negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model — it is the MLE, and a proper scoring rule, so it yields well-behaved probabilities. MSE also barely penalises a confidently wrong prediction, which is exactly what you want punished.

3. Interpret a logistic regression coefficient. What is an odds ratio?

A one-unit increase in x increases the log-odds by β, equivalently multiplies the odds by exp(β) — the odds ratio. β = 0.4 ⇒ odds ×1.49. The effect on the probability is not constant: it is largest near p = 0.5 and vanishes in the tails. Standardise features first if you want to compare coefficient magnitudes, and remember C in sklearn is the inverse penalty strength.

4. What is multicollinearity, how do you detect it, and does it hurt?

Two or more features carrying near-identical information, so XᵀX becomes ill-conditioned and the loss surface has a flat valley. Detect with a correlation matrix, condition number, or VIF = 1/(1−R²ⱼ) above ~5–10. It inflates coefficient variance — signs flip and magnitudes swing between refits — so it damages interpretation and stability, but usually not predictive accuracy. Fixes: drop one, combine them, use PCA, or use Ridge, which literally re-conditions the problem by adding αI.

5. Why does R² always increase when you add a feature?

Adding a column can only enlarge the space the fit is chosen from, so the residual sum of squares cannot rise — even for random noise, since the coefficient can always be set to zero and least squares will do slightly better than that by chance. Use adjusted R², which penalises p, or a held-out score. Test-set R² is a different statistic and can be negative.

6. When would you reach for a GLM — Poisson or Tweedie — instead of linear regression?

When the target's distribution is obviously not Gaussian and you know its shape. Poisson (log link) for counts; negative binomial when counts are over-dispersed; Gamma for positive right-skewed amounts like claim severity; Tweedie for zero-inflated positive targets like insurance pure premium, where most policies claim nothing and a few claim a lot. You get sensible non-negative predictions, a correct variance function, and coefficients a regulator will accept — and all three objectives exist in XGBoost and LightGBM if you want trees.

7. How does kNN work, and why is scaling mandatory?

Store the training set; to predict, find the k nearest rows by some distance and take their majority vote or mean. Because the prediction is a distance computation, a feature measured in a larger unit dominates the distance entirely — income in pounds swamps age in years, so you are effectively doing 1-D nearest neighbour on income. k is a pure bias-variance dial: k = 1 has zero training error and maximum variance; k = n is the majority-class baseline. It also suffers most from the curse of dimensionality.

8. Why does Naive Bayes work despite a false independence assumption?

Because classification needs the correct argmax, not correct probabilities. Correlated features are counted as independent evidence, which multiplies their effect and drives the posterior toward 0 or 1 — but the ordering of the classes usually survives. Consequences: it is badly calibrated and overconfident, so never use its predict_proba as a probability without calibration. Add Laplace smoothing so an unseen pair doesn't zero the product, and compute in log space.

9. Explain the SVM margin, the role of C, and the kernel trick.

The SVM finds the hyperplane with the largest margin — distance 2/‖w‖ to the nearest points, the support vectors, which are the only points that define the solution. The soft margin allows violations with slack variables; C trades margin width against violations (large C ⇒ narrow margin, low bias, high variance). The kernel trick: the dual objective depends on the data only through inner products, so replacing ⟨xᵢ,xⱼ⟩ with K(xᵢ,xⱼ) gives you the geometry of a high-dimensional feature space — infinite-dimensional for the RBF kernel — without ever computing φ(x). Cost: O(n²)–O(n³) training, mandatory scaling, and no native probabilities.

10. LDA vs PCA — what's the difference in objective?

PCA is unsupervised and maximises total variance; it never looks at the labels, so it can discard exactly the direction that separates classes if that direction happens to be low-variance. LDA is supervised and maximises the ratio of between-class scatter to within-class scatter, giving at most k−1 components for k classes. Both are linear projections; only one of them is trying to help your classifier.

11. ⭐ Why is impurity-based feature importance misleading? What do you use instead?

Mean decrease in impurity is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features — more candidate split points means more chances to reduce impurity by luck — and it is computed on training data, so it rewards overfitting. A random continuous ID-like column will show up as important. Use permutation importance on held-out data, which measures the actual drop in a metric when a feature is shuffled. Then state the remaining caveat: with correlated features, permuting one leaves its information available through the other, so importance is understated — and SHAP shares that problem in a different form (§5.7–5.8). Three methods, three questions; pick the one matching the question you're actually asking.
