Where careers are made or lost The three questions EDA exists to answer, the missingness taxonomy and the indicator trick, outliers as a judgement call, who needs scaling and why, categorical encoding up to cross-fitted target encoding, the split that simulates deployment — and the four kinds of data leakage.

Almost every senior-level rejection traces back to something in this part. Not a modelling mistake — a leaked feature, a random split on grouped data, an imputer fitted before the split. This is also the most examinable material in the whole subject, because it is checkable: an interviewer can hand you a notebook and ask what is wrong with it. Two sections carry the weight: 3.6 on splitting, whose single rule is your split must simulate how the model will actually be used, and 3.7 on leakage, where you must be able to name all four kinds, their symptoms, and the one structural fix that prevents most of them.

3.1 EDA that earns its time

EDA is not a gallery of histograms. Its job is to answer three questions, and once they are answered you should stop and start modelling.

The checks worth automating

CheckWhat it catches
Row count vs distinct ID countNesting — the single most important fact for your split
Exact and near-duplicate rowsData-pipeline double-loads; the group-leakage source people forget
Cardinality per columnIDs masquerading as features; categorical columns that will explode under one-hot
Null rate per column, over timeA column that became all-null in March — the most common production incident (§15.5)
Target rate over timeDefinition drift, and whether a time split will even be comparable
Correlation with target, sortedThe top of this list is where leakage hides (§3.7)
Mutual informationNon-linear association that correlation misses entirely
Look at the target's timestamp, not just its value

The single most useful EDA question that nobody asks: when was each column recorded, relative to the target? A column that is populated only after the outcome — a closure date, a collection-call count, a resolution code — will dominate your model and will not exist at prediction time. Building a small table of column → when it becomes available takes an hour and prevents the most expensive class of mistake in this guide.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhat do you actually look for in EDA?

Three questions, then stop. What is a row? — nesting, duplicates, IDs posing as features, because this determines the split. What is the target? — base rate, skew, label noise, and critically when it was recorded. How will I split? — time index, groups, distribution shift over time, feature latency. Distributions, correlations and mutual information are inputs to those questions, not an end in themselves. I'd also automate null-rate-over-time and cardinality checks, because those become my production data-quality monitors later.

DebugOne feature has 0.94 correlation with the target. What do you do?

Celebrate cautiously and then investigate, because that is far more likely to be leakage than signal. I'd ask: what is this column, and when is it populated relative to the outcome? Is it a consequence of the target (a closure date, a collection flag), an ID proxy, or an aggregate computed over the full period including the future? Then I'd check whether the score collapses under a time-based split. Only after those three checks would I treat it as a genuine predictor — and even then I'd confirm it will exist at prediction time.

3.2 Missing data ⭐

The taxonomy is examinable, the indicator column is the trick nobody mentions, and the pipeline rule is non-negotiable.

The strategies, and what each one costs

StrategyWhat it doesCost
Listwise deletionDrop rows with any NaNUnbiased only under MCAR; can destroy most of your data, and biases everything under MAR/MNAR
Mean / median / modeConstant fillShrinks variance, distorts correlations, and asserts a certainty you don't have
kNN imputationFill from similar rowsNeeds a distance, so needs scaling; expensive; degrades in high dimensions (§2.7)
MICE / iterativeModel each column from the others, cyclingPrincipled under MAR; slow; must be fitted on the training fold only
Indicator columnadd_indicator=TrueAlmost free; use it alongside any of the above
Native handlingXGBoost/LightGBM learn a default direction per split; sklearn's HistGradientBoosting* takes NaN directlyUsually the best answer on tabular data — no imputation step to get wrong, and the model learns where missing values belong
Imputation is model-fitting

A median is a statistic learned from data. Computing it over the whole dataset before splitting leaks test information into training — the same class of error as scaling before splitting. Imputers belong inside the Pipeline, and the pipeline is fitted inside the CV loop. This is not pedantry: with a small validation set and a skewed column, whole-dataset imputation can move the CV score by a noticeable margin.

Likely interview questions
Very commonMCAR / MAR / MNAR — define them and say which is dangerous.

MCAR: missingness independent of everything observed and unobserved — deletion is unbiased, imputation is fine, and it's rare. MAR: missingness depends on observed variables (income missing more often among younger applicants) — model-based imputation like MICE is principled; this is the realistic common case. MNAR: missingness depends on the unobserved value itself (high earners decline to state income) — the dangerous one, because no imputation can recover it from the data alone. You either model the mechanism explicitly or flag the limitation. In all three cases add a missingness indicator.

PracticalHow do you impute, and where in the pipeline does imputation live?

On tabular data my first choice is often no imputation — let XGBoost/LightGBM learn a default direction for NaN, or use HistGradientBoosting, which handles it natively and usually beats hand-imputation. Otherwise: median/mode as a baseline (noting it shrinks variance), iterative/MICE under MAR, always with add_indicator=True. Wherever it happens, it lives inside the Pipeline and is fitted on the training fold only — a median is a statistic learned from data, so computing it over the whole dataset is train-test contamination.

GotchaWhy can missingness itself be a useful feature?

Because the fact that a value is absent often carries signal — an applicant who declines to state income, a customer with no support history, a device that reports no location. Under MNAR the missingness pattern is precisely what encodes the hidden value, so imputing it away destroys information. Add a was_missing indicator; it costs one column and is frequently one of the biggest single wins. Caveat worth adding: if missingness is an artefact of a system rather than the subject (a logging bug), the indicator will encode your pipeline's history and may not generalise.

DebugA column is 60% missing. Drop it or keep it?

Check three things before deciding. Is the missingness informative? Compare the target rate for missing vs present — if it differs, the indicator alone may be valuable even if the values aren't. Is it missing at random over time? A column that was fully populated until March and empty since is a pipeline break, not a data property, and the fix is upstream. Will it exist at serve time? If it's 60% missing in training and 99% missing in production, it's a liability. Often the right answer is: drop the values, keep the indicator.

3.3 Outliers & robustness

Detection is easy and mostly irrelevant. The judgement call is what the point is.

Before you touch an outlier, answer one question: is this a data error (a negative age, a misplaced decimal point), a rare but real event (a genuine £400,000 transaction), or exactly the thing you are trying to predict (fraud, equipment failure, a large claim)? Removing outliers from a fraud dataset is removing the positive class — and it happens more often than you would think, because the cleaning script ran before anyone looked at what it was deleting.

DetectionAssumptionHandlingWhen
z-score > 3Roughly Gaussian, univariateRemoveConfirmed data errors only
IQR fence (1.5×)Distribution-free, univariateClip / winsorizeHeavy tails you want bounded without deleting rows
MahalanobisMultivariate GaussianTransform (log, Yeo-Johnson)Skew rather than true outliers
Isolation ForestAnomalies are easy to isolateUse a robust modelThe default answer — trees, Huber loss, quantile regression
LOFLocal density variesRobust scaling (median/MAD)Clusters of differing density
Robust by construction beats robust by cleaning

Trees split on order, not magnitude, so a value of 10⁹ is just “the largest one” — they barely notice outliers. Huber loss is quadratic near zero and linear in the tails, so a single extreme residual has bounded influence. Quantile regression ignores magnitude entirely. Reaching for a robust loss is usually a better answer than deleting rows, because deletion is irreversible, unauditable, and silently changes what your metric means.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualHow do you handle outliers?

First classify them: data error, rare but real, or the thing I'm predicting. Errors get fixed or removed with a documented rule. Rare-but-real values get clipped or transformed if they're destabilising a linear model, or left alone if I'm using a tree. And if they are the target — fraud, failure, large claims — removing them is removing the positive class, which is a mistake I'd flag loudly. My default is to prefer a robust model or loss (trees, Huber, quantile) over cleaning, because cleaning is irreversible and changes what the metric means.

GotchaShould you remove outliers before fitting a gradient boosting model?

Usually no. Trees split on rank order, so an extreme value is simply "the largest" and has bounded influence on the split search — unlike a linear model, where one point can pivot the whole fit. The exception is the target: with squared loss, an extreme y value does distort the fit, because the gradient is proportional to the residual. There the answer is a robust objective (Huber, quantile, or a log-transformed target) rather than deleting rows.

3.4 Scaling & transformation ⭐

“Which models need scaling?” is asked in almost every interview, and the reason matters more than the list.

The transformer menu

TransformerWhat it doesReach for it when
StandardScalerZero mean, unit varianceDefault for roughly symmetric features
MinMaxScalerSquash to [0,1]Bounded inputs; very sensitive to outliers
RobustScalerMedian and IQROutliers you're keeping
MaxAbsScalerDivide by max |x|Sparse matrices — it preserves zeros, which StandardScaler destroys
QuantileTransformerMap to a uniform/normal distributionWild distributions; destroys linear relationships, so be deliberate
PowerTransformerYeo-Johnson / Box-CoxSkew, when you want to stay roughly interpretable (Box-Cox needs positive values)
NormalizerScales each row to unit normRarely what you want — it's for direction-only comparisons, not feature scaling
Log-transforming the target changes the loss

Fitting on log(y) and minimising MSE means you are optimising relative error, and exp(ŷ) gives you back the conditional median, not the mean — so summing predictions across rows will systematically under-shoot the total. That is often exactly what you wanted, but say it out loud, remember to invert before reporting error in the target's units, and if you need unbiased sums, use a Gamma or Tweedie GLM with a log link instead (§5.8), which models the same multiplicative structure without the retransformation bias.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhich models need feature scaling and which don't — and why?

Need it: anything that measures distance (kNN, k-means, DBSCAN, RBF-SVM), maximises variance (PCA), or penalises coefficient magnitude (Ridge, Lasso, regularized logistic), plus neural nets for gradient conditioning. Don't: trees and every tree ensemble, because a split is a threshold on rank order and a monotone transform can't change which side of it a value falls on. Unregularized OLS doesn't need it either — the coefficient absorbs the units — but the moment a penalty appears, it does, because otherwise you're regularizing by choice of measurement unit.

GotchaYou have a sparse matrix. Which scaler?

MaxAbsScaler, or StandardScaler(with_mean=False). The reason: subtracting a mean turns every structural zero into a non-zero, which destroys sparsity and can blow up memory by orders of magnitude — on a large TF-IDF matrix that's the difference between fitting in RAM and not. MaxAbsScaler divides by the maximum absolute value per column, so zeros stay zeros and the data stays sparse.

DebugSomeone scaled the whole dataset then split. What's wrong, and how bad is it?

That's train-test contamination: the scaler's mean and variance were computed using validation rows, so information about them leaked into training. How bad depends — with a large balanced dataset the effect is small; with a small validation set, a skewed column, or a transformer that learns more (QuantileTransformer, PCA, target encoding) it can be substantial and produce a CV score that simply doesn't reproduce. The fix is structural rather than a judgement call: split first, put the scaler inside a Pipeline, and let cross-validation refit it per fold.

3.5 Categorical encoding ⭐

One-hot is the safe default, native handling is the modern default, and target encoding is the most common source of subtle leakage in the whole field.

EncodingDimensionsGood forDanger
One-hotk (or k−1)Low cardinality, linear modelsExplodes on high cardinality; dummy trap for linear models with an intercept
Ordinal1Genuinely ordered categories; treesImposes a false order on linear models — “Paris = 3” means nothing
Frequency / count1Cheap signal from popularityCollides distinct categories that happen to share a count
Hashingfixed dStreaming, unbounded vocabulariesCollisions; no inverse ⇒ no interpretability
Target / mean1High cardinality with real signalLeaks unless cross-fitted — see below
Native0CatBoost, LightGBM, HistGradientBoosting*Usually the best answer — no manual step to get wrong
Likely interview questions
Very commonWhat is target encoding and how do you do it without leaking?

Replace a category with a statistic of the target within it. Done naively it uses the row's own label, so the feature contains the target — CV soars, production collapses, and a category seen once becomes literally its own label. Do it out-of-fold / cross-fitted (each fold's encoding computed from the other folds), smoothed toward the global mean by count (n·ȳ_c + m·ȳ)/(n+m), with an explicit unknown bucket for serve-time categories — and all of it inside the pipeline. CatBoost's ordered target statistics solve it structurally by using only rows preceding each row in a random permutation.

DesignHow do you handle a categorical column with 50,000 levels?

One-hot is out — it creates 50,000 sparse, weak columns. In order of preference: native handling (CatBoost, LightGBM categorical splits, HistGradientBoosting) which is least likely to go wrong; cross-fitted target encoding with smoothing; frequency/count encoding as a cheap partial signal; hashing if the vocabulary is unbounded or streaming; and often the best one — group by a business hierarchy (merchant → merchant category → sector), which usually beats any statistical trick because it encodes real domain structure. I'd also add an unknown bucket, because at serve time you will see levels that weren't in training.

GotchaWhat's the dummy variable trap?

With an intercept term, k one-hot columns for a k-level categorical are perfectly collinear — they sum to 1 everywhere, which is exactly the intercept — so XᵀX is singular and the coefficients aren't uniquely identified. Drop one level (drop='first') for linear models with an intercept. It's harmless for trees and for regularized linear models, where the penalty breaks the tie — though it makes the coefficients harder to interpret, since they're now relative to an implicit baseline.

3.6 Splitting — where careers are made or lost ⭐⭐

One rule covers every case: your split must simulate how the model will actually be used. Random splits are the default and they are wrong more often than people think.

interactive Split strategy visualiser — watch the leak appear

60 rows · 12 customers × 5 rows each · ordered in time left-to-right, top-to-bottom. The contaminated customers count is exact: it is the number of customers whose rows land in more than one fold, meaning the model has seen that customer's other rows while being evaluated on them.

Pick the splitter from the structure in your data

StructureThe right splitterWhat goes wrong without it
None — rows are independentKFold—
Imbalanced classesStratifiedKFoldA fold with no positives; wild fold-to-fold variance
Rows nested in entitiesGroupKFold, StratifiedGroupKFoldGroup leakage — the model recognises the customer, not the pattern
Temporal orderTimeSeriesSplit + gapPredicting the past from the future; drift invisible until production
Both group and timeTime split, then verify groups don't straddle the boundaryThe realistic case, and the one people skip
Feature reporting latencyPurge + embargo around the boundaryFeatures built from data that wasn't available at prediction time
Spatial correlationSpatial blocking (split by region/tile)Neighbouring locations on both sides — the geospatial version of group leakage
Deduplicate before you split, not after

Near-duplicate rows are the group-leakage source people forget, because they don't look like groups. The same document scraped from two sources, an augmented copy, a record re-imported after a pipeline retry — split randomly and copies land on both sides, so the model is evaluated on rows it has effectively memorised. Dedupe first, using a key that reflects the real entity rather than an exact hash, then split.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhen is a random split wrong? Give three cases.

(1) Grouped rows — many transactions per customer; a random split lets the model recognise the customer rather than the pattern, so use GroupKFold. (2) Temporal data — a random split trains on the future to predict the past and hides drift entirely; use TimeSeriesSplit with a gap. (3) Near-duplicates — deduplicate before splitting, or copies straddle the boundary. A fourth if they want more: spatial correlation, where neighbouring locations are effectively the same observation. The governing rule: your split must simulate how the model will be used.

ConceptualIs stratification a substitute for grouping?

No — they're orthogonal. Stratification balances the class distribution across folds; grouping ensures an entity appears in only one fold. You can have perfectly stratified folds that are riddled with group leakage, which the widget above demonstrates directly. When you need both, StratifiedGroupKFold approximates them jointly — approximately, because you can't always satisfy both exactly, and the fold-to-fold class balance will be less even as a result. Report the spread.

DesignHow do you validate when rows are grouped by customer AND ordered in time?

Time takes precedence, because that's what production looks like: train on everything before t, test after. Then verify no customer straddles the boundary in a way that matters — if a customer has rows on both sides, the model has seen their history, which may or may not be realistic depending on whether production scores existing customers or only new ones. Match the split to the deployment: for “score existing customers monthly”, straddling is fine and realistic; for “score new applicants”, it's leakage. Add a purge/embargo gap for feature latency either way.

GotchaHow many times can you look at the test set?

Once. Every additional look is a selection you haven't accounted for — if you evaluate ten variants on the test set and ship the best, that score is optimistically biased for exactly the reason nested CV exists (§11.3). In practice: do all development against cross-validation on the training data, and touch the test set at the end to produce the number you report. If you've already burned it, say so and treat the number as an upper bound rather than pretending.

3.7 Data leakage — the most examinable topic in ML ⭐⭐

If you learn one thing from this part, learn the four kinds, their shared symptom, and the one structural fix that prevents most of them.

interactive Leakage simulator — the CV score you'd have reported

A real simulation, not a mock-up: 800 rows are generated with a genuinely weak signal, each enabled leak adds a component to the validation-time score that was computed using the validation labels, and both AUCs are computed exactly by the Mann-Whitney rank formula. The production score never sees those channels — which is precisely what happens when the pipeline is refitted on training data alone.

The structural fix, stated precisely

Everything learned from data goes inside a Pipeline, and the pipeline is fitted inside the cross-validation loop. “Learned from data” is broader than people expect: a scaler's mean, an imputer's median, an encoder's category list, a feature selector's chosen subset, a resampler's synthetic points, PCA's components, a target encoder's per-category statistics. If any of those is computed before the split, validation information has entered training. That single rule prevents kind 2 entirely and makes kinds 3 and 4 much harder to commit by accident.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ What is data leakage? Name four distinct kinds.

Information available at training time that will not be available at prediction time. Target leakage: a feature that is a consequence of the target or recorded after it (account_closed_date, num_collection_calls). Train-test contamination: scaling, imputing, encoding, selecting features or resampling before the split. Temporal leakage: features computed over the whole period, or from the future. Group leakage: the same entity or a near-duplicate on both sides. Symptom in all four: an implausibly good validation score that collapses in production. Structural fix: everything learned from data goes inside a Pipeline, fitted inside the CV loop — plus a split that mimics deployment.

DebugYour model gets 0.99 AUC on validation and 0.62 in production. Walk me through your diagnosis.

That gap is leakage until proven otherwise. In order: (1) Sort feature importances and ask what the top feature is and when it's populated relative to the outcome — target leakage is the most common and most dramatic. (2) Check whether any transform was fitted outside the pipeline. (3) Re-run with a time-based split and see whether the score falls to the production level; if it does, you have temporal leakage or drift. (4) Check for duplicate IDs or near-duplicate rows across the split. (5) Only then consider non-leakage explanations: training/serving skew (§15.4), a base-rate shift, or a threshold that was never re-tuned.

GotchaIs it leakage to use the full dataset to decide which features to engineer?

Strictly, yes — and it's the kind everyone commits. Every time you look at a plot of feature-vs-target on the full data and decide to add an interaction, you've used validation information to make a modelling decision. It's rarely as damaging as fitting a transformer on all the data, but it's why held-out test sets get optimistic over a long project, and it's the reason for the “touch the test set once” discipline. The practical mitigation: do exploratory work on the training split only, and keep a genuinely untouched holdout for the final number.

ConceptualGive an example of temporal leakage that isn't obvious.

A "customer average order value" feature computed once, over the entire history, and joined onto every training row. For a row in January it silently includes orders from November — the future relative to that prediction. The model learns to rely on information it will never have at serve time, so validation looks strong and production doesn't. It's insidious because the feature looks perfectly reasonable and there's no timestamp in the column name. The fix is an as-of join against a time-versioned feature history (§13.7), which is the core reason feature stores exist.

3.8 Auditing someone else's pipeline

A very common practical exercise: you're handed a notebook with a suspiciously good score and asked what's wrong with it. Here is the checklist, in the order that finds problems fastest.

#CheckWhat it catches
1Is the split the first data operation? Scroll to the top and find it.Train-test contamination — the most common and easiest to spot
2Search for fit_transform and .fit( outside a PipelineScalers, imputers, encoders, selectors, SMOTE fitted on everything
3Sort feature importances. What is the top feature, and when is it recorded?Target leakage — usually one dominant, suspicious column
4Re-run with a time-based splitTemporal leakage and drift — the score should not collapse
5Check for duplicate IDs / near-duplicate rows across the splitGroup leakage
6Compare the CV score's variance across foldsVery low variance on a hard problem is itself suspicious
7Ask what the base rate and the majority baseline areAn impressive-looking accuracy that a constant predictor matches
8Look for the test set being used more than onceOptimistic selection bias (§11.3)
The drop tells you which kind

The most informative single diagnostic is comparing random CV against grouped CV against time-series CV. If grouped is much lower, you have group leakage. If time-series is much lower, you have temporal leakage or genuine drift — and distinguishing those two takes one more step: check whether the relationship changed (concept drift) or just the inputs (§15.5). If all three agree, your leak is either target leakage or contamination, and steps 1–3 will find it.

Likely interview questions
DebuggingHow would you detect leakage in someone else's notebook in five minutes?

Five checks in order. (1) Is the split the first data operation? (2) Search for fit_transform outside a pipeline. (3) Sort feature importances and ask what the top feature is and when it was recorded. (4) Re-run with a time-based split and see how far the score falls. (5) Check for duplicate IDs across train and test. Two bonus signals: unusually low variance across folds on a hard problem, and a single feature that reaches >0.90 AUC on its own. Those five catch the overwhelming majority of real leakage.

DebuggingRandom CV gives 0.91, grouped CV gives 0.68. What does that tell you?

Group leakage, and it's severe. Under random splitting the model is being evaluated on rows from entities it has already seen, so it can memorise entity-level idiosyncrasies rather than learning a transferable pattern — 0.68 is its actual ability to generalise to a new customer. Which number is right depends on deployment: if production only ever scores existing customers with history, 0.91 might be the honest estimate; if it scores new applicants, 0.68 is. That question — which population does production see? — is the one to ask before deciding.
