Part 5 of 7 · Features & Interpretability

Exposing structure, then explaining it Feature engineering that beats model choice on tabular data, selection done inside the CV loop, production feature pipelines and point-in-time correctness — then the interpretability toolkit and the fairness questions that follow it.

On tabular data, feature engineering routinely beats model choice — a model can only use structure you expose. But the two ideas that make this part senior-level are less obvious. The first is point-in-time correctness: reconstructing what a feature's value was at prediction time rather than what it is now, which is simultaneously a feature-store design problem and the deepest form of the leakage question from Part 1. The second is that there are three different feature importances answering three different questions, and that SHAP is not an oracle — KernelSHAP assumes independence, path-dependent TreeSHAP can hand non-zero credit to features that do not affect the prediction, and correlated features distort all of them.

5.1 Why feature engineering still matters

A model can only use structure you expose. On tabular data that single sentence explains why feature work routinely beats model choice.

5.2 Numeric features

Ratios, differences and group aggregates carry most of the value. Transformations matter mainly for linear models.

TechniqueExampleWhy it helps
Ratiosdebt/income, amount/avg_amount_30dOften more predictive than either input — it encodes a comparison the model would otherwise have to learn
Differencesnow − last_login, price − category_medianRecency and deviation-from-normal are the two most reusable signals in tabular ML
Group aggregatesmean / max / std / count per customer, per merchant, per dayGives a row context it doesn't have on its own — and is the most common source of temporal leakage (§1.16)
Transformationslog, sqrt, Box-Cox / Yeo-JohnsonTames skew for linear models; does nothing for trees
BinningUniform, quantile, supervised (tree-based)Injects non-linearity into a linear model; usually loses information for a GBM
Clipping / winsorizingCap at the 1st/99th percentileBounds the influence of extremes without deleting rows

The fraud-features answer, organised

Structure it as entity × window × statistic. Transaction-level: amount, currency, hour-of-day (cyclically encoded), channel, merchant category, is-the-country-new. Card/customer aggregates over 1h / 24h / 7d / 30d: count, sum, max, std, distinct merchants, distinct countries, velocity (count this hour ÷ average hourly count). Deviation features: amount ÷ customer's 30-day median, distance from the last transaction's location ÷ elapsed time. Merchant-side: chargeback rate (cross-fitted, §1.14), age of the merchant relationship. Graph-ish: number of cards seen on this device. Then close with the constraint that makes it real: every one of these must be computable within the ~150 ms budget and must be point-in-time correct (§5.5).

5.3 Categorical, datetime and text

Datetime is the one people get wrong most often, and the fix is a single line of trigonometry.

interactive Why cyclical encoding — 23:00 and 00:00 are one hour apart
—distance, raw integer
—distance, sin/cos
—true clock distance (h)

Both distances are computed exactly. The raw integer encoding says 23:00 and 00:00 are the furthest apart pair on the clock; the sin/cos encoding says they are adjacent, which is the truth. This matters for every distance- and linear-based model — and even for trees, which would otherwise need two separate splits to express “late night”.

5.4 Feature selection

The interesting question is not how to select but why — and the honest answer is usually not accuracy.

Selection is part of the model

Choosing features by looking at the whole dataset — including validation rows — and then cross-validating is textbook train-test contamination. The selection has already seen the labels it will be scored against, so the CV score is optimistic, sometimes dramatically. Feature selection must be fitted inside the CV loop, exactly like a scaler or an imputer, which in practice means it lives in the Pipeline.

5.5 Feature pipelines in production ⭐

Two ideas here are worth more than the rest of this level combined: the same code must serve training and inference, and point-in-time correctness.

The rest of the production checklist

Custom transformers via BaseEstimator + TransformerMixin (implement get_feature_names_out so downstream explanations stay readable). set_output(transform="pandas") to keep column names through the pipeline. Feature versioning and backfills — changing a feature's definition silently invalidates every model trained on the old one. And a latency budget per feature: what can be computed inline at request time versus what must be precomputed and looked up.

5.6 The interpretability taxonomy

Three axes, and knowing which box a technique sits in is most of the vocabulary you need.

AxisOption AOption B
WhenIntrinsic — the model is readable (linear coefficients, a shallow tree, a GAM/EBM)Post-hoc — explain a fitted black box afterwards (SHAP, LIME, PDP)
ScopeGlobal — how does the model behave overall?Local — why this prediction?
CouplingModel-specific — TreeSHAP, coefficients, decision pathsModel-agnostic — permutation importance, PDP/ALE, LIME, KernelSHAP
Interpretability vs justifiability

A SHAP plot explains a model to a data scientist. A regulator asking why an application was declined needs something different: a small number of specific, actionable reasons in plain language — an adverse-action notice. That is a legal artefact, not a visualisation, and the most useful form of it is usually a counterfactual: “had your credit utilisation been below 40%, this application would have been approved.” Know where you legally need this: credit, insurance, hiring, healthcare.

5.7 The three feature importances ⭐

They are not three implementations of one idea. They answer three different questions, and the point of the question is whether you know that.

interactive Correlated features break every importance method
—x₁ importance
—x₂ importance
—drop both together
—x₀ (uncorrelated)

Real permutation importance: a ridge model is fitted, then each column is shuffled on held-out data and the increase in MSE is measured. Slide the correlation to 0.98 and watch both correlated features drop toward zero importance individually — while removing them together still destroys the model. Neither is redundant; the method just cannot see joint contribution. SHAP has a version of the same problem.

5.8 PDP, ICE, ALE, LIME and SHAP ⭐

Each shows something different. The most valuable thing you can say about SHAP is what it gets wrong.

interactive PDP hides what ICE reveals

The underlying model is y = β·x·(segment) + …: raising x helps one segment and hurts the other by the same amount. The PDP is their average and is essentially flat — read alone, it says “this feature does nothing.” The ICE curves show two opposite fans. Set the interaction to zero and the two views agree, which is exactly when a PDP is safe to trust on its own.

5.9 Fairness ⭐

The question is never “is the model fair?” It is “which fairness, and what are you giving up to get it?” — because several definitions are provably incompatible.

Document it, or it didn't happen

Model cards (intended use, out-of-scope uses, per-subgroup performance, known limitations) and datasheets for datasets (how it was collected, by whom, with what consent, what is missing) are the standard artefacts. They matter for a boring reason: they make the assumptions auditable later, which is exactly what someone will ask for when a journalist calls.

5.10 Interview focus for Part 5

Ten questions on features, explanations and the fairness conversation that follows them.

Part 5 · features, interpretability and fairness
1. Walk me through feature engineering for a transaction fraud model.

Organise it as entity × window × statistic. Transaction-level: amount, hour (cyclically encoded), channel, merchant category, is-new-country. Card/customer aggregates over 1h/24h/7d/30d: count, sum, max, std, distinct merchants, distinct countries, velocity ratios. Deviation features: amount ÷ 30-day median, geo-distance ÷ elapsed time since the last transaction. Merchant-side: cross-fitted chargeback rate, relationship age. Device/graph: cards seen on this device. Then the constraints that make it a real answer: every feature must be computable inside the ~150 ms budget and must be point-in-time correct, and aggregates must never be computed over the full history at training time.

2. How do you handle high-cardinality categoricals?

In rough order: native handling (CatBoost's ordered target statistics, LightGBM categorical splits, HistGradientBoosting's categorical_features) — least to go wrong. Cross-fitted target encoding with smoothing toward the global mean and an explicit unknown bucket. Frequency/count encoding as a cheap partial signal. Hashing when the vocabulary is unbounded or streaming. Grouping by a business hierarchy (merchant → merchant category) which is often better than any statistical trick. One-hot is the thing you avoid here — it creates thousands of sparse, weak columns.

3. How do you encode a datetime? Why cyclical encoding?

Extract components (hour, day-of-week, day-of-month, month, week-of-year), add domain flags (holiday, business day, days-to-next-holiday), and add time-since-last-event recency features. Use cyclical encoding — sin(2πh/24) and cos(2πh/24) — because on a raw integer scale 23 and 0 are the furthest apart values, when in reality they are one hour apart; the sin/cos pair puts them adjacent on a circle. Two traps: be explicit about timezone/DST, and don't feed the raw absolute timestamp as a feature, or the model will memorise a period rather than a pattern.

4. Filter vs wrapper vs embedded feature selection.

Filter scores each feature independently of any model (correlation, chi², ANOVA F, mutual information) — fast and scalable, but blind to interactions. Wrapper repeatedly refits a model on subsets (forward/backward, RFE/RFECV) — accounts for the actual model and interactions, but is expensive and can overfit the selection itself. Embedded gets selection as a by-product of fitting (L1, tree importances, boosting gain) — the practical default, but it inherits the model's biases. Whichever you use: it must be fitted inside the CV loop.

5. Why would you drop features that improve accuracy?

Because accuracy is not the only cost. Each feature carries serving latency (a fetch, a join), acquisition cost (a paid third-party call), drift and monitoring surface, leakage risk, maintenance burden, and sometimes regulatory exposure (a proxy for a protected attribute). A feature that adds 0.002 AUC and £0.30 per prediction is a bad trade. Being able to make that argument explicitly is a senior signal.

6. ⭐ What is a feature store and what problem does point-in-time correctness solve?

A feature store is a shared, versioned system for computing, storing and serving features so that training and inference use the same definitions. Point-in-time correctness is the hard part it exists for: when you build a training row for March, the feature values must be the values that existed in March, not today's. Joining today's lifetime_spend onto a March label leaks six months of the future into the row. The mechanism is an as-of join against a time-versioned feature history. The sibling problem it also solves is training/serving skew — one code path instead of two implementations that drift apart.

7. ⭐ Explain SHAP. What are its assumptions and failure modes?

SHAP assigns each feature the Shapley value of its contribution to a single prediction — averaged over all orderings in which features could be added — giving additive, locally faithful, consistent attributions that sum to the prediction minus a base value, and which aggregate into a global view. Failure modes: (1) KernelSHAP assumes feature independence when marginalising, so it evaluates the model on unrealistic combinations off the data manifold. (2) Path-dependent TreeSHAP avoids that by using the tree's own paths, but this changes the value function such that a feature with no effect on the prediction can receive non-zero attribution — breaking the dummy axiom. (3) With correlated features, credit is split in unintuitive ways. (4) A SHAP value is not a causal effect. Use it with correlation analysis and other diagnostics, not as an oracle.

8. PDP vs ICE vs ALE — what does each show?

PDP shows the average marginal effect of a feature, holding others at their observed values — but it averages over instances, so it hides heterogeneity, and it evaluates unrealistic combinations when features are correlated. ICE plots one curve per instance, revealing exactly the heterogeneity the PDP averages away — if half your customers respond positively and half negatively, the PDP is flat and the ICE plot shows two fans. ALE computes effects within small local windows of the feature and accumulates them, which avoids the unrealistic-combination problem, making it the correlation-robust alternative. Rule: look at ICE before trusting a PDP.

9. A regulator asks why an application was declined. What do you give them?

Not a SHAP beeswarm. An adverse-action notice: a small number of specific, plain-language, actionable reasons, derived consistently and reproducibly, tied to a recorded model version and input snapshot. In practice that means: a locally faithful attribution method to rank reasons, translated into standardised reason codes, ideally paired with a counterfactual (“had utilisation been under 40%, this would have been approved”), plus the audit trail — model version, feature values at decision time, the policy thresholds applied. Mention that this requirement is often why the model is a logistic regression or a monotonically-constrained GBM in the first place.

10. Define two fairness metrics and explain why you can't satisfy all of them.

Demographic parity: equal positive-prediction rates across groups. Equal opportunity: equal true-positive rates among those who actually qualify. Equalised odds: equal TPR and FPR. Predictive parity: equal precision — i.e. a score means the same thing in every group. The impossibility result: when base rates genuinely differ between groups, calibration within groups and equalised odds cannot both hold except in degenerate cases. So “make it fair” is under-specified — someone must choose which criterion, and that is a policy decision. Add: removing the protected attribute doesn't help because proxies encode it, so you measure per subgroup on the deployed decision and document it in a model card.
