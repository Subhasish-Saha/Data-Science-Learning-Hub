Part 14 of 17 · Interpretability & Fairness

Explaining it, and defending the explanation The three axes of the interpretability taxonomy, glass-box models including GAMs and EBMs, the three feature importances and the three different questions they answer, PDP versus ICE versus ALE, LIME's instability, SHAP with its failure modes named, counterfactuals and adverse-action notices, and the fairness impossibility result.

The most valuable thing you can say about SHAP is what it gets wrong. KernelSHAP assumes feature independence and evaluates the model on unrealistic rows off the data manifold; path-dependent TreeSHAP avoids that by changing the value function, with the consequence that a feature which does not affect the prediction can still receive non-zero attribution; and with correlated features credit is split in ways that do not map onto causal importance. Similarly, the strongest possible answer to a fairness question is not a metric but the impossibility result — several criteria are provably incompatible when base rates differ, so “make it fair” is under-specified and someone must choose which fairness. That is a policy decision, and asking who owns it is the senior move.

14.1 The interpretability taxonomy

Three axes. Knowing which box a technique sits in is most of the vocabulary you need — and it makes the rest of this part navigable.

AxisOption AOption B
WhenIntrinsic — the model is readable by construction (linear coefficients, a shallow tree, a GAM/EBM)Post-hoc — explain a fitted black box afterwards (SHAP, LIME, PDP)
ScopeGlobal — how does the model behave overall?Local — why this prediction?
CouplingModel-specific — TreeSHAP, coefficients, decision pathsModel-agnostic — permutation importance, PDP/ALE, LIME, KernelSHAP
Interpretability vs explainability vs justifiability

Three different words for three different requirements. Interpretability: a human can follow the mechanism (a shallow tree). Explainability: a post-hoc account of a decision that is approximately faithful (SHAP). Justifiability to a regulator: a documented, reproducible, auditable reason that satisfies a legal standard — which is a process requirement, not a plot. Knowing you legally need the third in credit, insurance, hiring and healthcare is what turns this from an academic topic into a design constraint.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualGlobal vs local interpretability — when do you need each?

Global answers "how does this model behave overall?" — needed for model validation, governance sign-off, checking it hasn't learned something absurd, and communicating with stakeholders. Local answers "why this prediction?" — needed for adverse-action notices, customer disputes, analyst triage, and debugging individual failures. They need different tools (PDP/ALE and global importance vs SHAP/LIME/counterfactuals), and a common mistake is presenting a global summary when someone asked a local question, or vice versa.

14.2 Intrinsically interpretable models ⭐

The option people forget: instead of explaining a black box, use a model that doesn't need explaining. On tabular data this costs less accuracy than you'd expect.

ModelWhat you can read off itCaveat
Linear / logistic regressionA coefficient per feature — sign, magnitude, odds ratioStandardise first, and beware multicollinearity flipping signs (§5.4)
Shallow decision treeA literal rule path for every predictionOnly readable to ~depth 4; unstable across refits (§7.6)
GLM with splinesA smooth per-feature curveGenuinely underrated — non-linear and readable
GAM / EBMOne shape function per feature (plus chosen pairwise terms), all plottableCompetitive with GBMs on tabular data, and interpretable by construction. ExplainableBoostingMachine
RuleFitA sparse set of if-then rules with weightsReadable, but rule sets get long quickly
Scorecard (binned + linear)Points per attribute band — the credit-industry standardDeliberately coarse; the trade-off is accepted for defensibility
EBMs deserve to be named

An Explainable Boosting Machine is a GAM fitted by cyclic gradient boosting: it learns a separate shape function per feature (plus a small number of chosen pairwise interactions), so the model is exactly the sum of curves you can plot — no approximation, no post-hoc explanation. On many tabular benchmarks it lands within a point or two of an unrestricted GBM. Mentioning it when someone asks “how do you get interpretability?” is a genuinely strong answer because it sidesteps the SHAP-caveat conversation entirely.

Likely interview questions
DesignYou need an interpretable model for a regulated use case. What do you build?

I'd try intrinsic interpretability before post-hoc explanation, because explaining a black box is an approximation and a regulator can challenge the approximation. In order: a regularized logistic regression or GLM with splines and monotonic constraints, which gives coefficients you can put in an adverse-action notice; or an EBM/GAM, which is exactly a sum of per-feature shape functions you can plot and is usually within a point or two of a GBM on tabular data. If accuracy genuinely demands a GBM, I'd add monotonic constraints (which are both a regularizer and a compliance artefact) and pair it with SHAP plus the caveats — but I'd quantify the accuracy gap first, because it's often smaller than assumed.

ModernWhat is an EBM and why is it interesting?

An Explainable Boosting Machine is a GAM fitted by cyclic gradient boosting: it boosts on one feature at a time, producing a separate shape function per feature plus a small number of automatically-selected pairwise interactions. Because the model is the sum of those curves, the explanation is exact rather than approximate — you plot the function and that's the model. It's interesting because it lands close to unrestricted GBMs on many tabular benchmarks, so it largely dissolves the accuracy-vs-interpretability trade-off that the SHAP conversation exists to manage.

14.3 The three feature importances ⭐⭐

They are not three implementations of one idea. They answer three different questions, and the point of the question is whether you know that.

interactive Correlated features break every importance method
—x₁ importance
—x₂ importance
—drop both together
—x₀ (uncorrelated)

Real permutation importance: a ridge model is fitted, then each column is shuffled on held-out data and the increase in MSE is measured. Slide the correlation to 0.98 and watch both correlated features drop toward zero individually — while removing them together still destroys the model. Neither is redundant; the method just cannot see joint contribution. SHAP has a version of the same problem.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Three kinds of feature importance — how do they differ?

Impurity/gain (MDI): how much this feature reduced impurity while the tree was built — free, but biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features and computed on training data. Permutation: how much the held-out metric degrades when the column is shuffled — measures what you actually care about and works for any model, but correlated features mask each other and shuffling creates unrealistic rows. SHAP: how this prediction is divided among features — local and globally aggregable, additive, expensive, with correlation problems of its own. They answer three different questions and can rank features differently while all being correct.

DebuggingTwo features you know matter both show near-zero permutation importance. What's happening?

They're almost certainly correlated with each other. Permutation shuffles one column at a time, so when you scramble x₁ the model simply reads the same information from x₂ and the metric barely moves — and vice versa. Both look unimportant while jointly being essential, which the widget above demonstrates directly. Diagnostics: compute a correlation matrix alongside any importance ranking; try grouped permutation (shuffle correlated features together); or use conditional permutation, which respects the correlation structure. And note SHAP is not immune — it splits credit between correlated features in ways that are equally hard to interpret causally.

14.4 PDP, ICE and ALE ⭐

Three ways to plot “what does the model do as this feature changes?” — and one of them can be actively misleading on its own.

interactive PDP hides what ICE reveals

The underlying model is y = β·x·(segment) + …: raising x helps one segment and hurts the other by the same amount. The PDP is their average and is essentially flat — read alone, it says “this feature does nothing.” The ICE curves show two opposite fans. Set the interaction to zero and the two views agree, which is exactly when a PDP is safe to trust on its own.

PlotShowsFails when
PDPThe average marginal effect: vary the feature, hold others at their observed values, average the predictionsInteractions (opposite effects cancel) and correlation (it evaluates the model on combinations that don't exist — 25 years of tenure at age 19)
ICEOne curve per instance — the same computation without averagingCluttered with many rows; use a sample plus the centred variant (c-ICE)
ALEAccumulated local effects: computes differences within small windows of the feature and accumulates themHarder to explain; less familiar. But it is the correlation-robust answer
The rule: look at ICE before trusting a PDP

A flat PDP means one of two very different things — the feature genuinely doesn't matter, or it matters enormously in opposite directions for different subgroups. Only the ICE plot distinguishes them, and the second case is often the more important finding. Modern PartialDependenceDisplay can draw both together (kind="both"), so there is no reason not to.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ PDP vs ICE vs ALE — what does each show?

PDP shows the average marginal effect of a feature — but it averages over instances, so it hides heterogeneity, and it evaluates the model on unrealistic combinations when features are correlated. ICE plots one curve per instance, revealing exactly that hidden heterogeneity: if half your population responds positively and half negatively, the PDP is flat and the ICE plot shows two fans. ALE computes effects within small local windows of the feature and accumulates them, so it never leaves the data manifold — the correlation-robust alternative. Rule: look at ICE before trusting a PDP, and prefer ALE when features are correlated.

Deep diveWhy does correlation break a PDP specifically?

Because the PDP algorithm fixes the feature of interest at a grid value and keeps every other feature at its observed value, then averages. With correlated features that generates rows the data-generating process would never produce — age 19 with 25 years at the employer — and the model's prediction there is an extrapolation with no meaning. The averaged curve then reflects model behaviour in regions with no data. ALE fixes this by only ever comparing predictions for points already inside a small window of the feature, so it never queries impossible combinations.

14.5 LIME

A local surrogate: fit a simple interpretable model in the neighbourhood of one prediction, and read that. Intuitive, fast — and unstable in a way you should be able to name.

LIME perturbs the instance you want to explain, gets the black box's predictions for those perturbations, weights them by proximity to the original, and fits a sparse linear model to that weighted local dataset. The coefficients are the explanation.

LIME's three weaknesses, in order of importance

(1) Instability. Run it twice on the same instance and you can get materially different explanations, because the perturbations are random. That is disqualifying for any use where the explanation is a record (an adverse-action notice). (2) The neighbourhood is a free parameter — the kernel width silently determines what "local" means and therefore the answer, with no principled way to choose it. (3) The perturbation scheme is arbitrary for tabular data, and typically samples features independently, so it evaluates the model off the data manifold — the same flaw as KernelSHAP (§14.6). In practice SHAP has largely displaced it for tabular work.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualHow does LIME work, and why would you not use it?

It perturbs the instance, gets black-box predictions for the perturbations, weights them by proximity, and fits a sparse linear surrogate to that local dataset — the coefficients are the explanation. Reasons not to use it on tabular data: it's unstable (random perturbations mean two runs can disagree, which is disqualifying when the explanation is a record); the kernel width silently defines "local" with no principled way to choose it; and the perturbation scheme samples features independently, so it queries the model off the data manifold. SHAP gives a more principled decomposition with consistency guarantees, and TreeSHAP is fast and exact for tree models — so for tabular work SHAP has largely displaced it.

14.6 SHAP ⭐⭐

The most valuable thing you can say about SHAP is what it gets wrong. Everyone can describe what it does; very few can name its failure modes.

What it is

SHAP assigns each feature the Shapley value of its contribution to a single prediction — the average marginal contribution across all possible orderings in which features could be added to the model. That construction is the unique attribution satisfying four axioms: efficiency (contributions sum to the prediction minus a base value), symmetry (equal contributors get equal credit), dummy (a feature with no effect gets zero), and additivity. Being additive and local means it aggregates cleanly into a global view.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Explain SHAP. What are its assumptions and failure modes?

SHAP gives each feature the Shapley value of its contribution to a single prediction — its average marginal contribution across all orderings in which features could be added — which is the unique attribution satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy and additivity. Contributions sum to the prediction minus a base value, and being local and additive it aggregates into a global view. Failure modes: (1) KernelSHAP assumes feature independence when marginalising, so it queries the model on unrealistic rows off the data manifold. (2) Path-dependent TreeSHAP avoids that by using the tree's own paths, but changes the value function such that a feature with no effect can get non-zero attribution — breaking the dummy axiom. (3) With correlated features credit is split unintuitively. (4) A SHAP value is not a causal effect. Use it with correlation analysis and other diagnostics.

Deep diveWhat's the difference between TreeSHAP's interventional and path-dependent modes?

Interventional uses an explicit background dataset and breaks feature dependence deliberately — it satisfies the dummy axiom (irrelevant features get exactly zero) but evaluates the model on off-manifold combinations, so the attributions describe a hypothetical intervention rather than observed behaviour. Path-dependent follows the tree's own split structure using the training distribution, so it stays on-manifold and is faster, but the changed value function means a feature that never affects the prediction can receive non-zero credit if it's correlated with one that does. There is no mode that achieves both, and knowing that trade-off exists is more valuable than picking a side.

GotchaCan you use SHAP values to decide what to change?

No — that's the most consequential misuse. SHAP explains how the model composed a prediction, which reflects predictive association including through proxies, confounders and consequences of the target. High SHAP for num_collection_calls in a default model doesn't mean reducing collection calls reduces default. To decide what to change you need a causal quantity — an experiment, or an identification strategy (§16.4). What SHAP is good for: debugging, sanity-checking, leakage detection, per-case explanation of a decision as it was made, and generating hypotheses worth testing causally.

14.7 Counterfactuals & adverse-action notices ⭐

The most useful explanation for an end user is not an attribution — it is “what would need to change?”

AudienceWantsGive them
A data scientist debuggingMechanismSHAP, ALE, ICE, slice analysis
A stakeholderConfidence and a mental modelGlobal drivers in their language, plus one worked example
An end user who was declinedWhat to do nextA counterfactual: “had utilisation been below 40%, this would have been approved”
A regulatorA defensible, reproducible recordAdverse-action notice: standardised reason codes, model version, input snapshot, and the fairness evidence
An analyst triaging alertsSpeed and prioritisationTop-3 contributing features plus similar historical cases
What makes a counterfactual good

Four properties, and they conflict. Validity — it genuinely flips the prediction. Proximity — it changes as little as possible. Sparsity — it changes as few features as possible, because "change these seven things" is useless advice. Actionability — it only changes things the person can actually change: “be five years younger” is a valid counterfactual and an unacceptable one. That last constraint is why counterfactual generation needs a per-feature mutability specification, not just an optimiser.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ A regulator asks why an application was declined. What do you give them?

Not a SHAP beeswarm. An adverse-action notice: a small number of specific, plain-language, actionable reasons, derived reproducibly, tied to a recorded model version and input snapshot. In practice that means a locally faithful attribution method ranked into standardised reason codes, ideally paired with a counterfactual ("had utilisation been below 40%, this would have been approved"), plus the audit trail — model version, feature values at decision time, policy thresholds applied — and the fairness evidence. Worth adding: this requirement is often why the production model is a logistic regression or a monotonically-constrained GBM in the first place.

DesignWhat makes a counterfactual explanation good?

Four properties that trade off against each other. Validity — it actually flips the prediction. Proximity — minimal change from the original. Sparsity — few features changed, because "alter these seven things" is useless advice. And actionability — only mutable features: "be five years younger" is mathematically valid and practically insulting. That last one means counterfactual generation needs a per-feature mutability and direction specification (age immutable, income increase-only, utilisation freely changeable) rather than just an optimiser over the feature space.

14.8 Fairness ⭐⭐

The question is never “is the model fair?” It is “which fairness, and what are you giving up to get it?” — because several definitions are provably incompatible.

Document it, or it didn't happen

Model cards (intended use, out-of-scope uses, per-subgroup performance, known limitations) and datasheets for datasets (how it was collected, by whom, with what consent, what is missing) are the standard artefacts. They matter for a boring reason: they make the assumptions auditable later, which is exactly what someone will ask for when a journalist calls or a complaint escalates.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Define two fairness metrics and explain why you can't satisfy all of them.

Demographic parity: equal positive-prediction rates across groups. Equal opportunity: equal true-positive rates among those who actually qualify. Also worth naming: equalised odds (equal TPR and FPR) and predictive parity (equal precision — i.e. a score means the same thing in every group). The impossibility result: when base rates genuinely differ between groups, calibration within groups and equalised odds cannot both hold except in degenerate cases — a perfect classifier or equal base rates. It's a theorem, not a tooling gap. So "make it fair" is under-specified: someone must choose which criterion, and that's a policy decision, not a modelling one.

GotchaCan't you just remove the protected attribute?

No — this is "fairness through unawareness" and it doesn't work. Proxies encode the attribute: postcode, name, device type, education history, shopping patterns, even writing style. A model with enough features will reconstruct it, and you've now lost the ability to measure disparate impact while doing nothing to prevent it. The correct approach is the opposite: retain the attribute for measurement (in a governed way), measure outcomes per subgroup on the deployed decision, and mitigate deliberately at whichever stage is appropriate — pre-processing, in-processing, or post-processing.

DesignYou discover a shipped model has disparate impact. What do you do?

Quantify it precisely first — per subgroup, on the deployed decision, with confidence intervals, before saying anything imprecise. Then escalate immediately with the number and the affected population, because delay is the thing that becomes indefensible later. Propose interim mitigation while a fix is built: a threshold change, human review for the affected slice, or disabling the model if the harm is serious. Then treat "which fairness criterion do we hold ourselves to" as a policy decision needing an owner outside the modelling team — legal, compliance, or the product owner — because the impossibility result means it's a value judgement, not a technical one. Finally, document the whole thing in the model card.
