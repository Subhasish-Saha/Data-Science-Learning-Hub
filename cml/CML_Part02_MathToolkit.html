Part 2 of 17 · The Mathematical Toolkit

The maths you will genuinely be asked about Vectors, norms, eigenvectors and the SVD; gradients, convexity and second-order methods; Bayes and the base-rate fallacy; the MLE/MAP bridge that turns every loss into a probabilistic statement; the statistics of comparing models; and why distance stops meaning anything in high dimensions.

You will not be asked to prove a theorem. You will be asked what an eigenvector has to do with PCA, why XGBoost bothers with second derivatives, and what a p-value is not. The single highest-value item here is the MLE/MAP bridge: least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise, log loss is maximum likelihood under a Bernoulli, Ridge is MAP with a Gaussian prior and Lasso with a Laplace prior. Once you see that, regularization stops being a trick that stops overfitting and becomes a stated belief about the coefficients — which is exactly the framing that makes §4.4 easy.

2.1 Linear algebra

You will not be asked to prove anything. You will be asked what an eigenvector has to do with PCA, and what multicollinearity does to a coefficient — and both answers live here.

ObjectWhat it means hereWhere it shows up
Dot productProjection / unnormalised similarityEvery linear model; cosine similarity; kernels (§6.6)
Matrix × vectorA linear map — rotate, scale, projectXw is “apply the model to every row at once”

The one derivative you should be able to write

Least squares in matrix form is L(w) = (Xw − y)ᵀ(Xw − y). Its gradient is ∂L/∂w = 2Xᵀ(Xw − y). Set that to zero and you get XᵀXw = Xᵀy — the normal equations, and therefore w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy whenever XᵀX is invertible. Everything in §5.1 follows from those three lines, including why nobody actually computes the inverse.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhat do eigenvectors have to do with PCA?

PCA looks for the directions of maximum variance in centred data. Variance along a unit direction v is vᵀΣv, and maximising that subject to ‖v‖=1 gives exactly the eigenvector of the covariance matrix Σ with the largest eigenvalue — and the eigenvalue is the variance along it. Equivalently, they are the right singular vectors of the centred data matrix, which is how it's actually computed (SVD is numerically better than forming Σ). Details in §9.6.

ConceptualWhy does anyone care whether a matrix is positive semi-definite?

Three places. A covariance matrix must be PSD, so an estimate that isn't signals a numerical or sampling problem. A kernel must produce a PSD Gram matrix (Mercer's condition) or it doesn't correspond to an inner product in any feature space and the SVM's convexity guarantee evaporates (§6.6). And a Hessian that is PSD everywhere means the function is convex, which is what gives you a unique global minimum (§2.2).

DebugYour linear model's coefficients flip sign when you refit on a slightly different sample. What's happening?

Almost certainly multicollinearity. Correlated columns make XᵀX ill-conditioned, so the loss surface has a long flat valley in which many coefficient combinations fit almost equally well — small sample changes move the optimum a long way along that valley. Diagnose with a correlation matrix, the condition number, or VIF = 1/(1−R²ⱼ) above ~5–10. Note it damages interpretation and stability, usually not predictive accuracy. Fix by dropping one, combining them, or using Ridge, which re-conditions the problem directly.

2.2 Calculus & optimization ⭐

One idea does most of the work — follow the negative gradient. Two follow-ups separate candidates: convexity, and why XGBoost bothers with second derivatives.

interactive Gradient descent — watch the learning rate decide everything
—final loss
—distance to optimum
—condition number
—behaviour

Real gradient descent on a real quadratic. Three experiments. η too small — it crawls and never arrives in the step budget. η too large — it oscillates across the valley or diverges outright. Conditioning 20:1 with a moderate η — it zig-zags, because the gradient points across the valley rather than along it. That last one is the reason you scale features before any gradient-based model (§3.4).

Convexity: what it actually buys you

A convex function has one basin: every local minimum is the global minimum. That is a guarantee about the answer, not about speed. Its practical consequence is reproducibility — with a convex loss, the optimiser's starting point does not change the result, so “I get a different model every run” is alarming for logistic regression and completely expected for k-means or a neural net.

The optimiser zoo, and where each one appears

MethodThe updateWhere you meet it

Coordinate descentOptimise one coefficient at a timeHow Lasso is actually fitted — the soft-threshold step is what produces exact zeros (§5.6)

Why second-order, in one sentence

The gradient tells you which way is downhill; the Hessian tells you how the slope is changing, so you can jump straight to the bottom of a quadratic approximation instead of taking a fixed-size step. XGBoost exploits this: expanding the loss to second order gives a closed form for the optimal leaf weight, w*ⱼ = −Gⱼ/(Hⱼ + λ), and a matching split-gain formula — which is why it needs no learning-rate tuning within a tree and why λ appears naturally as regularization rather than being bolted on.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhy does convexity matter, and which of your models are convex?

Convexity guarantees that any local minimum is global, so the optimiser's starting point doesn't change the answer — you get reproducibility, and you can trust that a converged fit is the best fit. Convex: linear regression, Ridge, Lasso, logistic regression, linear SVM (all with their standard losses). Non-convex: decision trees (the split search is combinatorial), k-means, GMM/EM, neural nets. Practical consequence: n_init and seeds matter for the second group; different results across runs of logistic regression indicate a bug.

Very commonExplain gradient descent and how you'd choose the learning rate.

Repeatedly step in the direction of the negative gradient: w ← w − η∇L(w). Too small and it crawls, wasting budget and possibly stopping short; too large and it oscillates across the valley or diverges. In practice: use a schedule (decay, or one-cycle), monitor the loss curve rather than picking blind, and — most importantly for classical ML — scale your features first, because a badly conditioned surface makes the gradient point across the valley instead of along it, and no single learning rate works well for all directions.

Deep diveWhy does XGBoost use second-order gradients?

Expanding the loss to second order around the current predictions gives a quadratic in each leaf's output, which can be minimised in closed form: w*ⱼ = −Gⱼ/(Hⱼ + λ), where G and H are the summed gradients and Hessians in that leaf. That yields a principled split-gain formula ½[G_L²/(H_L+λ) + G_R²/(H_R+λ) − G²/(H+λ)] − γ, so the tree structure is chosen by the same objective the model optimises rather than by a proxy impurity measure. It also means the regularization terms λ and γ fall out of the derivation instead of being bolted on afterwards.

GotchaHow is Lasso actually fitted, given the L1 term isn't differentiable at zero?

By coordinate descent: cycle through coefficients, optimising one at a time with the others fixed. For each, the update has a closed form — the soft-thresholding operator S(ρ, λ) = sign(ρ)·max(|ρ|−λ, 0) — and that max(·, 0) is exactly what sets coefficients to precisely zero rather than merely small. So the sparsity isn't an artefact of a tolerance; it comes out of the optimiser's update rule. Sub-gradient methods also work but converge more slowly and don't give exact zeros as cleanly.

2.3 Probability & the base-rate fallacy ⭐

One worked example here transfers directly to fraud, screening and every imbalanced problem in Part 12 — which is why interviewers keep asking it.

The vocabulary, with the trap attached

  • Sensitivity = recall = TPR, specificity = 1 − FPR. These are properties of the test and do not depend on prevalence.
  • Precision (PPV) depends on prevalence — which is exactly why it changes when your model meets production traffic with a different base rate than your training sample.
  • Independence vs conditional independence. Two features can be dependent overall and independent given the class — the assumption Naive Bayes makes (§6.2). They can also be independent overall and dependent given a third variable, which is what a collider is, and conditioning on one is a classic way to manufacture a spurious association.
Base rates move, and your precision moves with them

A model validated on a 10%-positive sample and deployed into 2%-positive traffic will have far worse precision than you measured, even though nothing about the model changed — sensitivity and specificity held, but PPV is a function of prevalence. This is prior/label drift (§15.5), and the response is usually to re-threshold rather than retrain. Knowing that distinction is worth a lot in a production interview.

Likely interview questions
Very commonA test is 99% sensitive and 95% specific for a disease with 1% prevalence. You test positive. What's the probability you have it?

About 17%. Work it in counts, not fractions — it's faster and less error-prone. In 10,000 people: 100 sick, of whom 99 test positive; 9,900 healthy, of whom 5% = 495 test positive. So 594 positives, 99 real ⇒ 99/594 ≈ 16.7%. The lesson to state: with a rare condition, false positives from the huge negative population swamp the true positives, so a highly accurate test still fires wrongly most of the time. That is precision under imbalance, and it's the same arithmetic as a fraud alert queue.

GotchaYour model's precision dropped in production but sensitivity and specificity are unchanged. What happened?

The base rate fell. Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the classifier and don't depend on prevalence; precision does. If positives went from 10% to 2% of traffic, the same FPR now generates far more false positives relative to true ones, so PPV collapses without the model changing at all. This is prior/label drift, and the right response is usually to re-threshold (§12.7) rather than retrain — retraining fixes nothing if the relationship P(y|x) is stable.

ConceptualIndependence vs conditional independence — why does the distinction matter?

Because models make claims about one and not the other. Naive Bayes assumes features are independent given the class — not marginally — which is why it can work on obviously correlated features (§6.2). And the reverse case matters for causal reasoning: two independent variables can become dependent when you condition on a common effect (a collider), so adding a control variable can create a spurious association rather than removing one. That's the standard trap in §16.4.

2.4 Maximum likelihood & MAP — the bridge ⭐

The highest-value few minutes in this part. Every loss function you use is a maximum-likelihood statement in disguise, and every regularizer is a prior.

Maximum likelihood asks: which parameters make the observed data most probable? Write down an assumption about the noise, take the log, flip the sign, and a familiar loss falls out. This is not a coincidence or an analogy — it is a derivation, and being able to run it is what turns “we use MSE” into “we are assuming Gaussian noise, which is why outliers hurt us so much.”

What this buys you in an interview

Three answers become one idea. Why does MSE punish outliers so hard? Because it assumes Gaussian noise, whose tails are thin, so a large residual is enormously improbable and the fit contorts to explain it. Why use log loss instead of accuracy? Because log loss is the Bernoulli likelihood — a proper scoring rule, minimised by the true probability. What does α actually control in Ridge? The ratio σ²/τ² — how tight your prior belief is that coefficients are near zero, relative to the noise. Saying any one of those unprompted lands well.

Likely interview questions
Deep dive⭐ Show that Ridge is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior.

With Gaussian noise the likelihood is p(y|X,w) ∝ exp(−‖y−Xw‖²/2σ²). Put a Gaussian prior p(w) ∝ exp(−‖w‖²/2τ²). The posterior is proportional to their product, so taking −log gives ‖y−Xw‖²/2σ² + ‖w‖²/2τ². Maximising the posterior means minimising that, which is exactly the Ridge objective with α = σ²/τ². Swap the Gaussian prior for a Laplace prior exp(−‖w‖₁/b) and the penalty becomes ‖w‖₁ — Lasso. A tighter prior (small τ) means stronger regularization, which gives α an interpretation rather than leaving it as a knob.

ConceptualWhy is least squares the "right" loss for regression?

It isn't, universally — it's the right loss if you believe the noise is Gaussian, because then minimising squared error is maximum likelihood. That framing tells you when it's wrong: heavy-tailed or contaminated noise makes the Gaussian assumption bad, and a single outlier will drag the fit a long way, which is why Huber (Gaussian centre, Laplace tails) or MAE exist. It also explains the deeper fact that MSE recovers the conditional mean while MAE recovers the median — different summaries of the same conditional distribution.

ConceptualWhat's the difference between MLE and MAP?

MLE maximises p(data | parameters); MAP maximises p(parameters | data) ∝ p(data|parameters)·p(parameters). The only difference is the prior, and in log space a prior is exactly a penalty term. So MAP = MLE + regularization, and as data grows the likelihood dominates and the two converge — which is the formal version of "regularization matters most when you have little data". Neither is fully Bayesian: both return a point estimate rather than a posterior distribution.

2.5 The statistics you actually use

Not hypothesis testing for its own sake — the statistics of comparing two models honestly and of not being fooled by a subgroup.

The two resampling tools that do the real work

  • The bootstrap. Resample your evaluation set with replacement, recompute the metric, repeat 1,000 times, take percentiles. That gives a confidence interval for any metric — including ones with no closed-form standard error, like AUC or a business KPI. For comparing two models, bootstrap the difference, resampling paired (same indices for both models), or your interval will be far too wide.
  • The permutation test. Shuffle the labels (or the model assignment) many times and see how often you'd see a difference this large by chance. Assumption-light, and it directly answers "could this gap be noise?"
What a p-value is not

It is not the probability the hypothesis is true, not the probability the result was chance, and not a measure of effect size. It is the probability of data at least this extreme under the null. If you evaluated forty model variants, several will clear p < 0.05 by construction — so report effect sizes with confidence intervals and correct for multiple comparisons. And note the sample-size trap in both directions: with a million rows, a meaningless difference is “significant”; with two hundred, a large real effect is not.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhat is a p-value, and what is it not?

It's the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is not P(hypothesis is true), not P(result was chance), and not an effect size. Two practical consequences: with enough data any trivial difference becomes significant, so always report the effect size and a confidence interval; and if you tested many variants, some will pass by construction, so correct for multiple comparisons or treat the winner as a hypothesis to confirm rather than a result.

Very commonHow would you test whether model B is genuinely better than model A?

Paired comparison on the same folds or the same test rows — never independent samples, because that throws away the pairing and inflates the variance. Then a bootstrap confidence interval on the difference (resample indices once and score both models on the same resample), or a paired permutation test. If comparing across CV folds, note that plain t-tests are anti-conservative because training sets overlap, so use the corrected resampled t-test. And finish with practical significance: a 0.002 AUC gain that costs 10× the latency is not an improvement.

GotchaWhat is Simpson's paradox and when have you seen it in ML?

A trend present in every subgroup reverses or vanishes when the groups are pooled, because the groups differ in both exposure and baseline. In ML you meet it as: a model that beats the incumbent overall while losing on every segment (the mix changed); an A/B test where treatment wins overall but loses on both mobile and desktop — which is nearly always a sample-ratio mismatch bug rather than a real effect; and univariate feature screening that reverses sign once you condition. The fix is to condition on the confounder or randomise, and to always look at slices (§11.6).

2.6 Distance & similarity

Every distance-based method inherits two properties from its metric: it is meaningless without scaling, and it degrades in high dimensions.

MetricFormula / ideaUse it whenWatch out
Euclidean (L2)√Σ(xᵢ−yᵢ)²Continuous, comparable scalesDominated by the largest-variance feature if unscaled
Manhattan (L1)Σ|xᵢ−yᵢ|High dimensions, grid-like dataOften more robust than L2 when p is large
MinkowskiThe L_p familyTuning p as a hyperparameterRarely worth the search
Mahalanobis√((x−y)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−y))Correlated features — it whitens by the covarianceNeeds a well-conditioned Σ estimate; fails when p ≈ n
CosineAngle, not magnitudeDirection matters, length doesn't (text, embeddings)Identical to L2 on unit-normed vectors
Jaccard / HammingSet overlap / bit differencesBinary vectors, sets, categorical codesIgnores counts entirely
GowerPer-feature normalised, then averagedMixed numeric + categorical rowsThe pragmatic answer for clustering real tabular data
The Mahalanobis intuition worth having

Euclidean distance treats a 1-unit move in every direction as equally surprising. Mahalanobis asks “how many standard deviations is this, accounting for how the features co-vary?” — so a point that is far along the axis of natural variation is not unusual, while a point that breaks the correlation structure is. That is exactly why it is a good multivariate outlier detector (§3.3), and why it reduces to plain Euclidean distance when Σ is the identity, i.e. after whitening.

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhen would you use Mahalanobis distance over Euclidean?

When features are correlated and on different scales. Mahalanobis normalises by the covariance matrix, so it measures distance in units of natural variation — a point far along the main axis of the data isn't unusual, while one that violates the correlation structure is. That makes it a good multivariate outlier score. The cost: you need a stable estimate of Σ and its inverse, which fails when p is close to n or features are collinear — use a shrinkage estimator or fall back to a robust method like Isolation Forest.

PracticalHow do you compute distance for a table with both numeric and categorical columns?

Gower distance is the standard answer: compute a per-feature distance in [0,1] — normalised absolute difference for numeric, 0/1 mismatch for categorical, Jaccard for sets — then average (optionally weighted). Alternatives: one-hot the categoricals and use Euclidean, which implicitly weights a category by how many levels it has and is usually worse; or embed everything and use cosine. And the honest option: on tabular data, prefer a tree-based method that needs no metric at all.

2.7 The curse of dimensionality ⭐

Not a slogan — a measurable phenomenon, with a specific mechanism and a specific list of victims.

interactive Distance concentration, measured
—mean distance
—relative spread (σ/μ)
—d_near / d_far
—is kNN meaningful?

Real uniform points, real pairwise Euclidean distances. Drag p from 2 to 100 and watch the histogram collapse into a spike while d_near/d_far climbs toward 1. When that ratio approaches 1, “nearest neighbour” has stopped carrying information — every point is about the same distance from every other. Note that adding samples barely helps: the cure for the curse is fewer dimensions, not more rows.

Three distinct effects, often conflated

EffectMechanismWho suffers
Distance concentrationRelative spread of pairwise distances → 0, so near and far become indistinguishablekNN, k-means, DBSCAN, RBF-SVM, LOF
Empty spaceVolume grows exponentially; a fixed sample covers a vanishing fraction of it, so every region is sparseAny local method; density estimation
Capacity / overfittingMore features ⇒ more ways to fit noise ⇒ more varianceEverything, including trees — this is the one that matters most in practice

The third is why “more features can't hurt” is a trap answer (§17.16), and it applies even to models that are immune to the first two. A gradient boosting model is fine with distance concentration and still degrades if you hand it a thousand noisy columns — because each one gives the split search another chance to find a spurious threshold.

Why trees mostly escape it

A tree never computes a distance. It asks “is feature j above threshold t?” — a one-dimensional question at every node — so distance concentration and empty space simply don't apply. What does apply is the capacity effect, plus a subtler one: with many irrelevant features, random feature subsampling (max_features) starts picking mostly noise columns at each split, which weakens Random Forest specifically. That's a good argument for feature selection even on tree models (§13.5).

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhat is the curse of dimensionality and which models suffer most?

Three things bundled under one name. Distance concentration: as p grows, pairwise distances converge to a common value, so nearest and farthest neighbours become nearly equidistant and "local" stops meaning anything. Empty space: volume grows exponentially while your sample doesn't, so every neighbourhood is sparse. Capacity: more features means more ways to fit noise, hence more variance. The first two hit kNN, k-means, DBSCAN and RBF-SVM hardest; trees and tree ensembles escape them because they only ever ask one-dimensional threshold questions — but they still suffer the third.

GotchaDoes adding more training rows fix the curse of dimensionality?

Barely, for the geometric part. The volume of the space grows exponentially in p while your sample grows linearly, so to maintain the same density you'd need exponentially more data — which is why distance concentration persists across any realistic n (the widget above lets you verify this). More rows do help the capacity/overfitting effect. The real fixes are dimensionality reduction (PCA, §9.6), feature selection (§13.5), using a model that doesn't rely on distance, or embedding into a lower-dimensional space where the structure is genuinely lower-dimensional.

DebugYour kNN model works well on 8 features and collapses on 80. Why?

Distance concentration, most likely combined with irrelevant features diluting the metric. In 80 dimensions the distance between a point and its true nearest neighbour is nearly the same as to a random point, so the "neighbourhood" is arbitrary — and every irrelevant feature adds noise to the distance without adding signal. KD-trees and ball-trees won't save you either; above roughly 15–20 dimensions they degrade to brute-force scanning. Fixes: aggressive feature selection, a supervised metric (LDA projection, metric learning), PCA first, or switch to a tree ensemble that doesn't use a distance at all.
