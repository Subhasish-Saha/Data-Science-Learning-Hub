The models you must be able to defend line by line Linear regression from the normal equation to why nobody computes it that way; every assumption, what breaks when it fails, how to detect it and how to fix it; the full residual-diagnostic toolkit; multicollinearity and VIF; R² and its traps; the regularized family; logistic regression and the odds ratio; GLMs for counts, claims and skew; and quantile regression.

This is the deepest part of the subject and the one interviewers use to separate people who have used a linear model from people who have fitted one. The centrepiece is §5.2: five assumptions, each with what it means, what breaks when it fails, how you detect it, and what you do about it — because “state the assumptions of linear regression” is asked constantly and answered badly. 5.1 The model, and how it is actually fitted ⭐

Everyone can write y = Xw + ε. The interview is in the three follow-ups: how it's solved, why nobody solves it that way, and what “linear” actually constrains.

From loss to normal equations

Minimise L(w) = (Xw − y)ᵀ(Xw − y). The gradient is 2Xᵀ(Xw − y); setting it to zero gives the normal equations XᵀXw = Xᵀy, hence w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy when XᵀX is invertible. Geometrically, Xŵ is the orthogonal projection of y onto the column space of X — which is why the residual is perpendicular to every column, and therefore why residuals always sum to zero when you include an intercept.

Why nobody computes the inverse

Problem with (XᵀX)⁻¹ConsequenceWhat's used instead
Singular under perfect collinearityNo unique solution — the inverse doesn't existSVD / pseudo-inverse returns the minimum-norm solution
Cost is O(p³) plus O(np²) to form XᵀXProhibitive for large pQR decomposition, or iterative methods for huge problems
Forming XᵀX squares the condition numberYou lose roughly half your significant digits before you startQR or SVD on X directly — which is what lstsq does

Scikit-learn's LinearRegression calls scipy.linalg.lstsq, which uses an SVD-based driver. So the closed form is the right thing to derive and the wrong thing to implement — and saying exactly that is the complete answer.

“Linear” means linear in the parameters

A very common misunderstanding. y = w₀ + w₁x + w₂x² + w₃log(x) + w₄(x₁·x₂) is still a linear model — it is linear in w, which is all the maths requires. What is not linear is y = w₀·x^w₁. This matters practically: you can fit curves, interactions and splines with ordinary least squares simply by expanding the feature matrix, which is exactly what PolynomialFeatures and SplineTransformer do.

Likely interview questions
Very commonDerive the normal equation, and say why you'd avoid computing (XᵀX)⁻¹.

Minimise (Xw−y)ᵀ(Xw−y); the gradient is 2Xᵀ(Xw−y); setting it to zero gives XᵀXw = Xᵀy, so w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy. Three reasons not to compute it that way: it's singular under perfect collinearity; it costs O(p³); and critically, forming XᵀX squares the condition number, so you lose roughly half your significant digits before solving. Use QR or SVD on X directly — which is what lstsq, and therefore sklearn, actually does.

ConceptualIs a model with x² and log(x) terms still a linear model?

Yes. "Linear" refers to linearity in the parameters, not the features — y = w₀ + w₁x + w₂x² + w₃log(x) is linear in w, which is all least squares needs. That's why you can fit arbitrary curves, interactions and splines by expanding the design matrix and still use OLS. A genuinely non-linear model would be something like y = w₀·x^{w₁}, where a parameter appears in an exponent.

Deep diveWhat's the geometric interpretation of least squares?

Xŵ is the orthogonal projection of y onto the column space of X — the closest point in the space your features can reach. The residual vector is therefore perpendicular to every column of X, which is exactly what the normal equations Xᵀ(y − Xŵ) = 0 state. Two immediate consequences: residuals sum to zero whenever an intercept is included (since the intercept is a column of ones), and residuals are uncorrelated with every fitted feature — which is why a pattern in a residual plot always signals a missing structure rather than a fitting failure.

5.2 The assumptions, one by one ⭐⭐

“State the assumptions of linear regression” is asked constantly and answered badly. Here is each one with what it means, what breaks when it fails, how you detect it, and what you do about it — which is the answer that finishes the question.

Real OLS on real generated data. The point of the middle panel: the residual-vs-fitted plot has a distinct signature for each violation, which is why it is the first plot you draw. Note also what the numbers do — some violations bias the coefficient, others leave the coefficient fine and corrupt only the standard error. Knowing which is which is the substance of this section.

The five assumptions

A1 Linearity — the relationship really is linear in the parameters
Means
E[y|X] = Xw. The conditional mean is a linear combination of your features (which may themselves be non-linear transforms).
What breaks
Biased predictions and biased coefficients. This is the only violation that makes the model wrong rather than merely inefficient — you are estimating the best linear approximation to something that isn't linear, and it will be systematically off in whole regions of the feature space.
Detect
Residual vs fitted plot showing curvature; partial-residual (component-plus-residual) plots per feature; a RESET test; or simply comparing against a GBM — if a tree model beats your linear one by a lot, you're missing structure.
Fix
Add polynomial terms, splines (SplineTransformer — usually better behaved than high-degree polynomials), interactions, or transform the target. Or accept it and use a non-linear model.
A2 Independence of errors — no autocorrelation or clustering
Means
Cov(εᵢ, εⱼ) = 0 for i ≠ j. Each observation contributes independent information.
What breaks
Coefficients stay unbiased, but standard errors are badly wrong — usually far too small, because your effective sample size is much less than n. Every p-value and confidence interval is then overconfident. In ML terms: your cross-validation is optimistic for exactly the same reason (§3.6).
Detect
Residuals plotted in time order showing runs or waves; the Durbin-Watson statistic (≈2 is fine, <1.5 or >2.5 suspicious); an ACF plot of residuals; or knowing structurally that rows are nested (many rows per customer).
Fix
Cluster-robust standard errors; add lag features or model the time structure explicitly; use a mixed-effects model for nesting; and for ML purposes, GroupKFold or TimeSeriesSplit.
A3 Homoscedasticity — constant error variance
Means
Var(ε|X) = σ², the same everywhere. Errors on large predictions are no bigger than errors on small ones.
What breaks
Coefficients stay unbiased but inefficient (no longer minimum-variance), and standard errors are wrong — so inference is unreliable. Prediction intervals are also wrong, being too wide where variance is low and too narrow where it is high, which is often exactly where the expensive mistakes are.
Detect
The classic fan or cone shape in the residual-vs-fitted plot; a scale-location plot (√|standardised residual| vs fitted) with a rising trend; Breusch-Pagan or White's test.
Fix
Robust (heteroscedasticity-consistent) standard errors — HC3 — if you only need valid inference; a log or Box-Cox transform of the target; weighted least squares if you know the variance structure; or a GLM with a variance function that matches (Gamma or Tweedie, §5.8), which is usually the principled answer when variance grows with the mean.
A4 Normality of errors — only needed for inference
Means
ε ~ N(0, σ²). Note this is about the residuals, not about y or the features — a very common confusion.
What breaks
Nothing, for prediction. Gauss-Markov gives you BLUE (best linear unbiased estimator) without it. Normality is needed for exact t- and F-tests and confidence intervals in small samples — and by the CLT even that requirement fades as n grows. This is the assumption most candidates over-weight.
Detect
Q-Q plot of residuals (points off the diagonal in the tails), a histogram, Shapiro-Wilk — though with large n every normality test rejects, so read the plot rather than the p-value.
Fix
Usually do nothing if you only care about prediction. If you need inference: transform the target, use bootstrap confidence intervals (assumption-free), or switch to a GLM with the right family. Heavy tails often really mean outliers (A5) or a missing feature (A1).
A5 No perfect multicollinearity — and no high leverage you haven't noticed
Means
No feature is an exact linear combination of the others, so XᵀX is invertible and the coefficients are identified.
What breaks
Perfect collinearity: no unique solution at all. Near-collinearity: coefficients remain unbiased but their variance explodes — signs flip between refits, magnitudes swing wildly, and individual t-tests become insignificant while the model as a whole is highly significant. Predictions are usually fine; interpretation is destroyed.
Detect
VIF = 1/(1−R²ⱼ) above ~5–10; correlation matrix; the condition number of X; the tell-tale pattern of a high overall F-statistic with no individually significant coefficients (§5.4).
Fix
Drop one of the pair, combine them into a ratio or index, use PCA, or use Ridge — which re-conditions the problem directly by adding αI. Also check leverage and Cook's distance for single points with outsized influence (§5.3).
The two facts that separate a strong answer

(1) Normality is for inference, not prediction. If you're building a predictive model and someone asks whether your residuals are normal, the correct answer is that it doesn't affect the prediction — it affects the p-values you probably aren't using. (2) Only violated linearity biases the coefficients. Autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity leave the estimates unbiased and corrupt the uncertainty. Knowing which failures cost you accuracy and which cost you only inference is the substance of the question, and almost nobody separates them.

The one nobody lists: exogeneity

Strictly, the assumption that makes OLS unbiased is E[ε|X] = 0 — the errors are uncorrelated with the features. Omitted-variable bias, measurement error in a feature, and simultaneity all violate it, and no residual plot will reveal it because the residuals are orthogonal to X by construction. It is invisible to diagnostics and only addressable by design — which is precisely the gap that instrumental variables and the rest of §16.4 exist to fill. Mentioning it marks you out.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ State the assumptions of linear regression and what breaks when each fails.

Linearity — fails ⇒ biased predictions and coefficients, visible as curvature in the residual plot; fix with splines, polynomials, interactions, or a non-linear model. Independent errors — fails with time or clustered data ⇒ coefficients unbiased but standard errors far too small; detect with residuals in time order or Durbin-Watson; fix with cluster-robust SEs, lag features, or GroupKFold/TimeSeriesSplit for ML. Homoscedasticity — fails ⇒ unbiased but inefficient, wrong SEs and wrong prediction intervals; detect via a fan shape or Breusch-Pagan; fix with robust SEs, a log transform, WLS, or a GLM with the right variance function. Normality of residuals — needed only for exact inference in small samples, not for prediction; detect with a Q-Q plot; usually do nothing, or bootstrap. No perfect multicollinearity — fails ⇒ no unique solution; near-failure inflates coefficient variance so signs flip; detect with VIF > 5–10; fix by dropping, combining, or using Ridge. And the one people miss: exogeneity, E[ε|X]=0, which is what actually makes OLS unbiased and is invisible to every diagnostic.

GotchaDo the residuals need to be normally distributed?

Only if you need exact inference — t-tests, F-tests and confidence intervals in small samples. For prediction it doesn't matter at all: Gauss-Markov gives you the best linear unbiased estimator using only linearity, exogeneity, homoscedasticity and no perfect collinearity — normality is nowhere in it. And by the CLT, even the inference requirement fades as n grows. Two clarifications worth adding: the assumption is about the residuals, not y or the features; and heavy-tailed residuals usually signal an outlier or a missing feature rather than a genuine distributional problem.

DebuggingYour residual plot shows a clear fan shape. What is it, and does it matter?

Heteroscedasticity — error variance grows with the fitted value, which is extremely common for positive, right-skewed targets like spend or claim amounts. Consequences: coefficients stay unbiased, but they're no longer minimum-variance and the standard errors are wrong, so inference is unreliable — and prediction intervals are too narrow exactly where the values are largest. Fixes in order of preference: a GLM with a matching variance function (Gamma or Tweedie with a log link) since variance genuinely scales with the mean; a log transform of the target; robust HC3 standard errors if I only need valid inference; or weighted least squares if I know the variance structure.

DebuggingResiduals show a clear wave pattern when plotted in time order. What's the problem?

Autocorrelation — the errors aren't independent, so consecutive observations carry overlapping information. The coefficients remain unbiased, but the effective sample size is much smaller than n, so standard errors are far too small and everything looks more significant than it is. Confirm with Durbin-Watson (≈2 fine, <1.5 suspicious) or an ACF plot of residuals. Fix by modelling the time structure (lags, seasonal terms), using cluster/HAC-robust standard errors, or — for the ML version of the same problem — switching from random CV to TimeSeriesSplit with a gap, because a random split is optimistic for exactly this reason.

Deep diveWhich assumption violations bias the coefficients, and which only affect inference?

Bias the coefficients: violated linearity (you're fitting the wrong functional form) and violated exogeneity (E[ε|X] ≠ 0 — omitted variables, measurement error, simultaneity). Leave coefficients unbiased but corrupt inference: heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation, both of which make standard errors wrong while the point estimates stay centred on the truth. Neither, exactly: non-normal residuals, which affect only small-sample exact tests. Multicollinearity is its own case — unbiased but enormously high variance, so individual coefficients become unreliable while predictions stay fine. Separating these four cases is what makes the answer sound like you've used the model rather than read about it.

5.3 Residual diagnostics ⭐

Four plots, each with a distinct signature. Being able to name the plot and what a bad one looks like is the practical half of §5.2.

Likely interview questions
PracticalWhich diagnostic plots do you look at, and what does each tell you?

Residuals vs fitted — the first one: curvature means non-linearity, a fan means heteroscedasticity, random scatter means you're fine. Scale-location (√|standardised residual| vs fitted) — the cleanest heteroscedasticity signal, because it removes the sign. Normal Q-Q — tails off the diagonal mean non-normal residuals, which matters for inference only. Residuals vs leverage with Cook's distance — finds single points that are moving the whole fit. For time-structured data I'd add residuals plotted in time order, which is the only way to see autocorrelation.

ConceptualWhat's the difference between leverage, outlier and influence?

An outlier has a large residual — it's far from the fitted line in the y direction. Leverage measures how unusual a point's features are (the hat-matrix diagonal h_ii) — it's about x, not y, and it quantifies the potential to move the fit. Influence is the actual effect: how much the fit changes if you delete the point. A high-leverage point that follows the trend has little influence; a high-leverage point with a large residual has enormous influence. Cook's distance combines both, which is why it's the practical number — flag anything above roughly 4/n, and investigate rather than automatically delete.

5.4 Multicollinearity & VIF ⭐

The assumption violation with the most counter-intuitive consequences: your predictions are fine, your coefficients are nonsense, and your F-test disagrees with all your t-tests.

The tell-tale signature

SymptomWhy it happens
High overall F-statistic, no individually significant coefficientsThe features jointly explain a lot; individually none is identifiable, because another feature can substitute for it
Coefficient signs flip when you add or remove a featureThe loss surface is a flat valley — many coefficient combinations fit almost equally well (§2.1)
Enormous standard errorsVar(β̂ⱼ) = σ²/((1−R²ⱼ)·Σ(xⱼ−x̄ⱼ)²) — the (1−R²ⱼ) in the denominator is the VIF
Coefficients change wildly between CV foldsSame cause; and it's a good practical detector even without computing VIF
A coefficient with an economically implausible signClassic with correlated pairs: one absorbs the effect and pushes the other negative

VIF for feature j is 1/(1 − R²ⱼ), where R²ⱼ comes from regressing xⱼ on all the other features. It is literally the factor by which that coefficient's variance is inflated relative to an uncorrelated world. VIF = 10 means R²ⱼ = 0.9 and the standard error is √10 ≈ 3.2× larger. Rules of thumb: > 5 worth a look, > 10 a problem — but these are conventions, not thresholds.

Does it matter? Ask what the model is for

For prediction, multicollinearity is usually a non-issue: the fitted values are stable even when the individual coefficients aren't, because the correlated features move together in new data too. For inference or explanation it's fatal — you cannot say “a one-unit increase in X raises y by β” when X never moves alone in your data. So the correct answer to “does multicollinearity hurt?” is “it depends what you're using the model for”, and then give both halves. One caveat for prediction: if the correlation structure changes in production, unstable coefficients become unstable predictions.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhat is multicollinearity, how do you detect it, and does it hurt predictions or interpretation?

Two or more features carrying near-identical information, so XᵀX becomes ill-conditioned and the loss surface has a long flat valley. Detect: correlation matrix, condition number, and VIF = 1/(1−R²ⱼ) above ~5–10 — plus the signature of a high overall F-statistic with no individually significant coefficients, and signs that flip between refits. Does it hurt? It inflates coefficient variance, so it destroys interpretation and stability while usually leaving predictions fine — the correlated features move together in new data too. Caveat: if the correlation structure changes in production, the predictions become unstable as well. Fixes: drop one, combine into a ratio, PCA, or Ridge, which re-conditions the problem by adding αI.

GotchaYour F-test is highly significant but no coefficient is. What's going on?

The classic multicollinearity signature. The F-test asks whether the features jointly explain variance — they do. Each t-test asks whether a feature adds anything given all the others — and when two features are near-duplicates, neither adds anything the other doesn't already provide, so both look insignificant. The information is there; it just can't be attributed. Check VIFs, and note this is a good example of why you should never do feature selection by dropping individually-insignificant coefficients one at a time.

DesignWould you use VIF on a gradient boosting model?

Not for the same reason. Trees don't solve a linear system, so there's no coefficient variance to inflate and no numerical instability — a GBM handles correlated features without complaint. But correlation still matters for interpretation: permutation importance and SHAP both split credit unpredictably between correlated features, so two genuinely important features can each look unimportant (§14.3). So I'd compute correlations to inform how I read the explanations, and potentially drop redundant features for latency and stability reasons — not because the model needs it.

5.5 R², adjusted R² and friends

A metric everybody quotes and few can criticise. Three facts about it are commonly asked and commonly missed.

FactWhyWhat to do instead
R² never decreases when you add a featureAdding a column can only enlarge the space the fit is chosen from, so RSS can't rise — and least squares will exploit chance correlation to do slightly better even with pure noiseAdjusted R², which penalises p: 1 − (1−R²)(n−1)/(n−p−1), or a held-out score
Test-set R² can be negativeIt's 1 − SS_res/SS_tot using the training mean as the baseline; a model worse than predicting that mean scores below zeroReport it anyway — a negative test R² is a useful, loud signal
R² is not comparable across datasetsIt depends on the variance of y in that sample. A high-noise target caps R² regardless of model qualityCompare models on the same data; use RMSE in target units for communication
Anscombe's quartet, in one sentence

Four datasets with identical means, variances, correlations, regression lines and R² — and radically different shapes, one of which is entirely driven by a single outlier. It is the canonical argument for plotting your data before trusting any summary statistic, and it's a nice thing to name if a discussion of R² starts going in circles.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhy does R² always increase when you add a feature? What do you use instead?

An extra column can only enlarge the space the fit is chosen from, so the residual sum of squares cannot increase — and with a column of pure noise, least squares will still exploit chance correlation to reduce RSS slightly. Use adjusted R², which penalises the parameter count, or better, a held-out score. Two follow-on facts worth volunteering: test R² can be negative (a model worse than predicting the training mean), and R² is not comparable across datasets because it depends on the variance of y in that particular sample.

GotchaModel A has R² = 0.85 on dataset 1, model B has R² = 0.45 on dataset 2. Which is better?

Unanswerable as posed. R² measures the fraction of that dataset's variance explained, so it's bounded by how much of the target is predictable in the first place — a noisy target caps R² no matter how good the model is. Dataset 2 might simply be harder. To compare, I'd need both models on the same data, or a scale-free comparison against a shared baseline — which is exactly what MASE does for forecasting (§10.6) and what "lift over baseline" does generally.

5.6 Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net in practice ⭐

The theory is in §4.4. This is what the coefficient paths look like, how you choose α, and the practicalities that come up.

PracticalityDetail
Standardise firstNon-negotiable — otherwise the penalty is applied in arbitrary units (§4.4). The intercept is not penalised.
sklearn's alphaLarger = more regularization for Ridge/Lasso. But LogisticRegression uses C = 1/alpha, so smaller C = more regularization — an inverted convention that catches people out.
Lasso pathFitted by coordinate descent; lars_path gives the exact piecewise-linear path if you want to see where each feature enters.
Ridge with p > nWorks fine — the penalty makes the problem well-posed even when XᵀX is rank-deficient. Lasso caps out at n selected features.
Group LassoZeroes whole groups of coefficients together — the right tool when one categorical's dummies should enter or leave as a unit.
Likely interview questions
PracticalHow do you choose α?

Cross-validation over a log-spaced grid — RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV do this efficiently, and Lasso's path algorithms make it nearly free. Then apply the one-standard-error rule: pick the most regularized model whose CV error is within one SE of the minimum, which costs almost nothing in accuracy and buys a simpler, more stable model. Two cautions: the CV must be inside a pipeline with the scaler, and if you tune α and then report that same CV score, it's optimistically biased (§11.3).

GotchaIn sklearn, does a larger C mean more or less regularization?

Less. LogisticRegression and SVC use C as the inverse regularization strength, so small C means a strong penalty — the opposite convention to alpha in Ridge and Lasso, where larger means more. It's a genuine API inconsistency inherited from libsvm, and getting it backwards in a tuning grid is a common and embarrassing bug that produces a search over exactly the wrong region.

ConceptualYou have 5,000 features and 300 rows. Which regularizer?

p ≫ n, so unregularized OLS has no unique solution at all and something must constrain it. If I believe few features matter, Lasso — but note it can select at most n = 300 features by construction, and it will pick unstably among correlated ones. If I believe many small effects, Ridge, which handles p > n gracefully and keeps everything. In practice I'd start with Elastic Net, which spans both and keeps correlated groups together, and I'd cross-validate l1_ratio alongside α. I'd also be honest that with 300 rows the CV estimate itself will be noisy, so I'd use repeated CV and report the spread.

5.7 Logistic regression ⭐⭐

The interpretability and calibration baseline. Credit scoring runs on it for reasons you should be able to state — and the coefficient interpretation is the thing people most often fumble.

interactive Log-odds → odds ratio → probability
—odds ratio exp(β)
—log-odds z
—probability σ(z)
—dp/dx at this point

The point this makes better than any sentence: a logistic coefficient is constant in log-odds and wildly non-constant in probability. Drag x to the tails and watch dp/dx collapse toward zero while the odds ratio never moves. That is why “a one-unit increase raises the probability by β” is wrong and “multiplies the odds by exp(β)” is right.

The pipeline, and where each term attaches

Why it survives in regulated industries

Three concrete reasons, worth having ready. (1) Each coefficient is a defensible, monotone, sign-interpretable contribution you can put in an adverse-action notice. (2) It is nearly calibrated out of the box when well-regularized, because log loss is a proper scoring rule and it optimises exactly that — so predict_proba can be used in an expected-value calculation with far less anxiety than a GBM's (§12.8). (3) It is trivial to monitor: coefficient drift is directly inspectable, and the model has no capacity to encode exotic interactions you haven't sanctioned.

Likely interview questions
Very common⭐ Interpret a logistic regression coefficient. What is an odds ratio?

A one-unit increase in x raises the log-odds by β, equivalently multiplies the odds by exp(β) — the odds ratio. β = 0.4 ⇒ odds ×1.49, a 49% increase in odds. The effect on the probability is not constant: it's β·p(1−p), maximal near p = 0.5 and vanishing in the tails. That's the distinction people fumble — "raises the probability by 0.4" is simply wrong. Standardise features first if you want to compare coefficient magnitudes, and remember sklearn's C is the inverse penalty strength.

Very commonWhy can't you use MSE as the loss for logistic regression?

Two reasons. Composed with a sigmoid, squared error is non-convex in the parameters, so gradient descent has no guarantee of reaching the global optimum. And log loss is the negative log-likelihood of the Bernoulli model — the MLE, and a proper scoring rule — so it produces well-behaved probabilities and has a principled derivation rather than being an arbitrary choice. There's a practical third: MSE barely penalises a confidently wrong prediction (the gradient vanishes as the sigmoid saturates), which is exactly the error you most want punished.

ConceptualHow is logistic regression fitted, given there's no closed form?

Iteratively. The classic statistical method is IRLS (iteratively reweighted least squares) — a Newton method, since the log-likelihood's Hessian is available in closed form. Modern libraries use quasi-Newton solvers (lbfgs, the sklearn default) or coordinate descent (liblinear, and saga for L1/elastic-net at scale). Because the loss is convex, all of them reach the same optimum — so different solvers changing your coefficients materially means a convergence failure or an under-tuned max_iter, not a modelling choice.

DesignMulti-class: one-vs-rest or multinomial?

Multinomial (softmax) by default. It optimises a single joint likelihood so the probabilities are normalised across classes and are usually better calibrated; OvR fits k independent binary models whose outputs must then be normalised post hoc, which distorts them. OvR is worth considering when you want independent per-class thresholds — for instance, when each class feeds a separate downstream decision with its own cost — or when classes are genuinely not mutually exclusive, in which case you actually want multi-label (§10.2) rather than either.

GotchaYour logistic regression fails to converge and one coefficient is enormous. Why?

Almost certainly perfect (or quasi-perfect) separation — some feature or combination separates the classes exactly. The MLE then doesn't exist: the likelihood keeps improving as the coefficient goes to infinity, so the optimiser marches off and never converges. Causes: a leaked feature (§3.7), a categorical level that appears with only one class, or simply very few rows. Fixes: add regularization (any L2 penalty makes the solution finite and is why sklearn regularizes by default), use Firth's penalised likelihood, or merge/drop the offending level. Worth flagging that a perfectly separating feature is itself a leakage alarm.

5.8 GLMs — link functions and families ⭐

A GLM is a linear model plus two choices: a distribution for the target and a link function. Knowing which pair to reach for is one of the clearest practical signals available.

XGBoost and LightGBM both ship Poisson, Gamma and Tweedie objectives, and sklearn has PoissonRegressor, GammaRegressor and TweedieRegressor plus HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="poisson"). So the choice of distributional family is genuinely independent of the choice between a linear model and a GBM — and using a GBM with a squared-error loss on a claims target is leaving accuracy on the table for no reason.

Likely interview questions
Very commonWhen would you reach for a GLM — Poisson or Tweedie — instead of linear regression?

When you know the target's distribution and it isn't Gaussian. Poisson (log link) for counts; negative binomial when counts are over-dispersed (variance > mean, which is the usual real case); Gamma for positive right-skewed amounts like claim severity; Tweedie for zero-inflated positive targets like insurance pure premium. You get non-negative predictions, a correct variance function (so heteroscedasticity is handled by design rather than patched), multiplicative effects, and coefficients a regulator will accept. And you don't have to give up trees — XGBoost, LightGBM and sklearn's HistGB all support these objectives.

DesignWhy does insurance pricing use Tweedie rather than modelling claims directly?

Because pure premium — expected cost per policy — has a point mass at zero (most policies never claim) and a heavy right tail (a few claim a lot). A Gaussian model predicts negative premiums and fits the tail terribly. Tweedie with 1 < p < 2 is exactly a compound Poisson-Gamma: a Poisson number of Gamma-distributed claims, which is the actual data-generating process. The alternative — and it's a good answer too — is to decompose into frequency × severity: a Poisson model for claim count and a Gamma model for size, multiplied. That's more interpretable and lets you diagnose which component is driving a change.

ConceptualWhat does the link function actually do?

Two things. It maps the unbounded linear predictor Xw onto the target's valid range — a log link forces positive predictions, a logit link forces (0,1) — so the model can't produce impossible values. And it determines whether effects are additive or multiplicative: with a log link, a coefficient means "multiplies the expected value by exp(β)", which is usually the right structure for rates, counts and amounts. The canonical link for each family (logit for Bernoulli, log for Poisson) also gives nicer mathematical properties, though you're not obliged to use it.

Two more members of the linear family that solve problems the standard version cannot: asymmetric costs, and contaminated data.

Quantile regression gives you intervals for free

Fit at τ = 0.05 and τ = 0.95 and you have a 90% prediction interval that adapts to the input — wider where the data is noisy, narrower where it isn't. That's something a constant ±1.96σ band cannot do. Two cautions: quantiles fitted independently can cross (the 95th below the 5th somewhere), so check monotonicity or use a method that enforces it; and the coverage is only as good as the model, which is exactly the gap conformal prediction closes with a distribution-free guarantee (§16.3).

Likely interview questions
ConceptualWhat is quantile regression and when would you use it?

It minimises the pinball loss at level τ, which recovers the conditional τ-quantile rather than the mean. Three uses: prediction intervals that adapt to the input (fit at 0.05 and 0.95); asymmetric costs, where over- and under-prediction aren't equally bad — the optimal τ is c_under/(c_under+c_over); and robustness, since the median (τ=0.5) is insensitive to outliers. Cautions: independently fitted quantiles can cross, and the coverage is only as reliable as the model — conformal prediction gives you a distribution-free guarantee instead.

DesignYou need prediction intervals. Quantile regression, bootstrap, or conformal?

Depends what guarantee you need. Quantile regression is cheap and gives adaptive, heteroscedasticity-aware intervals, but coverage relies on the model being right. Bootstrap captures uncertainty in the parameters but not the irreducible noise, so it typically under-covers for a new observation unless you add the residual distribution back. Conformal prediction gives a finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantee under exchangeability and wraps any model — which is why it's my default when the interval carries a real commitment (§16.3). The strongest practical answer is usually conformalised quantile regression: adaptive width from the quantile model, guaranteed coverage from the conformal wrapper.
