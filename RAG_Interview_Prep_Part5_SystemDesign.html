Phase 6 · System Design Integration

The whole machine, and its trade-offs

Everything in Parts 1–4 was a component. System design is the assembly: two pipelines, a scaling story, a cost model, a stack choice, and the judgment to know when RAG is the wrong answer entirely. This is what senior rounds actually test.

5.1 The two-pipeline architecture

A production RAG system is really two systems that share one artifact. The ingestion pipeline (offline, batch, latency-tolerant) builds the index; the serving pipeline (online, synchronous, latency-bound) reads it. Conflating them is the most common junior mistake.

They have opposite requirements, so you build, scale, and deploy them independently:

  • Ingestion runs when documents change. It parses (§1.3), chunks, embeds, de-duplicates, versions, and upserts into the index + metadata store. It can be async, batched, and retried; throughput matters, latency doesn't.
  • Serving runs per user query under a strict SLA: embed → retrieve → rerank → augment → generate → cache. It's synchronous, high-QPS, and latency-critical.
  • The shared artifact is the vector index + metadata store (and caches). It's the contract between the two halves.
Likely interview questions
System designSketch the architecture of a production RAG system.

Two decoupled pipelines sharing an index. Ingestion (offline, event- or batch-driven): parse with layout/OCR, chunk, embed, de-duplicate, version, and upsert into the vector index + metadata store; it's async, retryable, throughput-oriented. Serving (online, per-query, SLA-bound): embed the query, retrieve (hybrid + filters), rerank, augment with a grounded prompt, generate, and cache; wrap with auth/ACL, guardrails, and logging. The shared vector index + metadata store is the contract. Add observability (log retrieved chunks, latency, feedback) and a scheduled golden-set eval. They scale and deploy independently.

5.2 Scaling to millions (and billions) of vectors

A single HNSW index in RAM is fine to ~a few million vectors. Past that, you shard, quantize, and rethink re-indexing. The interviewer's favorite follow-up — "what changes at 10× the volume?" — is really asking whether you know these levers.

  • Sharding — partition the index across nodes, by namespace/tenant/topic when queries are scoped (route to the right shard) or randomly with scatter-gather (query all shards, merge top-k). Scoped sharding also cuts search space and enforces isolation.
  • Compression for scale — at hundreds of millions/billions, full-precision HNSW won't fit in RAM; use IVF+PQ (§1.6), disk-backed indexes (DiskANN), or binary/scalar quantization, with a full-precision rescoring pass to recover recall.
  • Incremental vs full re-index — HNSW supports live inserts (incremental); IVF's cluster centroids drift as data grows, so you periodically retrain; a major embedding-model change forces a full re-embed + rebuild. Plan for zero-downtime rebuilds (build new index, atomic swap).
Likely interview questions
System designHow would your design change at 10× the document volume?

Retrieval, not generation, is what feels 10× — so: shard the index (scoped by tenant/topic if queries allow, else scatter-gather); switch from in-RAM full-precision HNSW to IVF+PQ or a disk-backed index with a rescoring pass to fit memory/budget; move re-indexing to incremental upserts with periodic retraining and zero-downtime atomic swaps; and scale ingestion throughput (parallel embedding). Costs shift toward storage and embedding; query latency stays roughly flat if sharded well. Serving compute scales with QPS independently. Also revisit caching hit-rates, which improve at scale.

5.3 Freshness & update pipelines

RAG's headline advantage over fine-tuning is freshness — but only if your update pipeline is real. Naive systems append new docs and never remove old ones, so contradictory versions pile up and retrieval degrades (the drift from §4.11).

The mechanics of keeping an index fresh:

  • Incremental upsert. When a document changes, don't rebuild — locate its existing chunks by doc_id, delete those vectors, re-chunk/re-embed the new version, and insert. HNSW handles live inserts/deletes.
  • Versioning & deletes. Stamp chunks with a version/updated_at; on update, remove the old version (hard delete or a tombstone filtered at query time) so old and new never both surface. This single discipline prevents a major class of "answers contradict each other" bugs.
  • TTLs for inherently time-bound content (promotions, status pages) so stale facts expire automatically.
  • Triggering via change-data-capture / webhooks / scheduled crawls, feeding the ingestion pipeline (§5.1). And invalidate caches (§4.9) for the affected content.

5.4 Cost modeling

Cost splits into three buckets that scale with completely different things. Model them separately or you'll optimize the wrong one — teams routinely agonize over embedding cost while generation quietly eats 90% of the bill.

BucketWhen paidScales withTypically…
Embedding (ingest)Once per doc + on changeCorpus size × re-index frequencyOne-time-ish; cheap if self-hosted
RetrievalPer queryQPS × index sizeNear-negligible
RerankingPer queryQPS × shortlist sizeModerate (a model pass)
GenerationPer queryQPS × (input + output tokens)Dominant — often 80–95%

So the levers that actually move the bill are on generation: a smaller/cheaper model where quality allows, fewer output tokens, shorter context (rerank + compress so you send 5 chunks not 20), prompt caching for the static prefix, and semantic caching to skip generation entirely on repeat questions. Agentic loops (§3.6) multiply the generation bucket 3–10×, which is why adaptive routing is a cost lever, not just a quality one. Embedding/storage costs matter mainly at very large corpora — there, quantization (§1.6) is the lever.

Likely interview questions
System designWalk through the cost model of a RAG system and where you'd optimize.

Three buckets: embedding (ingest — scales with corpus size × re-index frequency, one-time-ish, cheap if self-hosted), retrieval (per query, near-negligible), and generation (per query, scales with QPS × tokens — usually 80–95% of cost). So optimize generation first: smaller model where quality permits, fewer output tokens, shorter context via rerank+compress, prompt caching for the static prefix, and semantic caching to skip repeats. Agentic loops multiply this bucket, so route adaptively. Embedding/storage cost only dominates at very large corpora, where quantization is the lever. Always measure the split before optimizing.

5.5 Choosing a vector database

Almost all of them use HNSW under the hood, so the decision is rarely about raw search quality — it's about operations: managed vs self-hosted, scale, filtering power, hybrid support, and what stack you already run.

Likely interview questions
System designPinecone vs Weaviate vs Qdrant vs Milvus vs pgvector — how do you choose?

By operational fit, since they're all HNSW-class on quality. If you're already on Postgres and at modest scale, pgvector keeps everything in one system. If you want zero ops, Pinecone (managed serverless). For billions of vectors / horizontal scale, Milvus. For heavy metadata filtering and cost-effective self-hosting, Qdrant. For built-in hybrid search and vectorizer modules, Weaviate. For prototyping, Chroma or FAISS. Decide on managed-vs-self-hosted (privacy, cost, ops burden), scale, filtering needs, hybrid support, and existing stack — not benchmark deltas.

5.6 When NOT to use RAG

The most senior answer in any RAG interview is sometimes "I wouldn't use RAG here." Reaching for retrieval when the problem is behavior, computation, or a tiny static fact set is a real and common design error.

Decide by asking what the requirement actually is:

Likely interview questions
System designRAG or fine-tuning — how do you decide?

Separate knowledge from behavior. If the requirement is factual knowledge that's large, changing, private, or must be cited → RAG (update by re-indexing, get citations for free). If it's about how the model behaves — format, tone, a classification skill, domain phrasing → fine-tuning. They're complementary: a common stack fine-tunes the generator for format/faithfulness and the embedder for the domain, while RAG supplies the facts. Use fine-tuning-for-knowledge only when the knowledge is small, static, and latency forbids retrieval. And sometimes the answer is neither — pure computation is a tools/SQL job, a tiny static FAQ just goes in the prompt.

GotchaGive a scenario where adding RAG would make a system worse.

A latency-critical classifier or a fixed-format transformer (e.g., "rewrite this in our house style") — the knowledge is already in the model or the task is behavioral, so retrieval only adds latency, cost, and a new failure mode (bad retrieval) for no accuracy gain. Also a purely computational query ("sum this column") where RAG returns text about numbers instead of computing them — text-to-SQL/tools is correct. And a tiny, stable knowledge set that fits in the system prompt: an index is over-engineering.

5.7 Mock system design — a support assistant

The classic prompt: "Design a customer-support assistant over 500K docs, updated daily, sub-2s latency, with RBAC so users only see their org's documents, and answers must cite sources." Here's how to work it — clarify, map each requirement to a decision, then defend the trade-offs.

Step 1 — clarify. Confirm scale (500K docs ≈ ~5M chunks), QPS, the ~2s budget is p95 end-to-end, RBAC granularity (per-org), freshness (daily is fine → batch, not streaming), and that grounded citations are mandatory. Step 2 — map requirements to decisions:

RequirementDecisionWhy
500K docs (~5M chunks)HNSW in Qdrant; shard by orgFits RAM at this scale; org-sharding shrinks search + isolates data
RBAC (org isolation)Mandatory org_id/acl filter at retrievalEnforce in the query, never in the prompt (§4.10)
Daily updatesNightly CDC → incremental upsert + version-deleteReplace stale chunks; avoid contradictions (§5.3)
Sub-2s latencyHybrid retrieve → rerank top 50→5 → streamed LLM + semantic cacheBudget fits; cache makes repeats instant (see below)
Must cite sourcesCarry doc_id/URL metadata; grounded prompt with [Sn]Verifiable answers; abstain if weak (§1.7, §4.7)
Cost / privacySelf-hosted BGE embeddings + reranker; managed LLMEmbeddings cheap+private at scale; LLM quality where it counts

Step 3 — name the trade-offs & failure modes. Self-hosted embeddings trade ops burden for cost/privacy. Org-sharding trades cross-org search (which you don't want anyway) for isolation and speed. The reranker adds ~120ms for ordering quality — worth it here. Wrap it all with a golden-set eval (§4.5), RAGAS in CI, and production logging + drift alerts (§4.11). Then volunteer the failure modes before you're asked (next section).

Interview tip · the structure is the score

In a system-design round, how you proceed matters more than any single choice: (1) clarify requirements and constraints, (2) draw the two pipelines, (3) map each requirement to a decision with a one-line rationale, (4) call out the latency/cost budget, (5) name eval + monitoring, (6) volunteer failure modes and mitigations. Candidates who jump straight to "use Pinecone and GPT-4" without this structure read as junior even when the components are right.

5.8 Design interview focus

A reusable framework for any RAG system-design question, plus the two follow-ups that always come: scaling and single points of failure.

The framework (works for "design X over Y docs"): Clarify → Two pipelines → Decisions-with-rationale → Budget (latency + cost) → Eval & monitoring → Failure modes → Scale story. Walk it out loud; it demonstrates seniority regardless of the specific stack.

The design follow-ups
System designWhere's the single point of failure in your design, and how do you mitigate it?

Usually two candidates. (1) The LLM provider — a managed API is a hard dependency; mitigate with a fallback model/provider, timeouts with a graceful degraded response (return retrieved sources without a synthesized answer), and circuit breakers. (2) The vector store — mitigate with replicas/HA, health checks, and (in the worst case) a keyword/BM25 fallback so the system degrades to lexical search rather than failing. Also the ingestion pipeline: if it silently breaks, the index goes stale — monitor freshness (last-successful-index age) and alert. The theme: identify hard dependencies, add fallbacks/replicas, and prefer graceful degradation over hard failure.

System designHow do you approach a RAG system-design question you've never seen?

Run the framework. Clarify scale, latency SLA, freshness, security, and citation needs first. Sketch the two pipelines (ingestion vs serving). Then make each decision — parser, chunker, embedder, index/DB, hybrid+rerank, prompt, LLM — with a one-line "because." State the latency and cost budget and where the time/money goes. Add eval (golden set + RAGAS) and monitoring (log chunks, drift alerts). Volunteer failure modes and mitigations. Close with the scale story ("at 10× I'd shard and quantize"). The structure signals experience; the specific components can be swapped.

System designWhat are the most common ways a RAG system design goes wrong?

Conflating ingestion and serving (no clean re-index path); dense-only retrieval (misses exact terms); enforcing access control in the prompt instead of the retrieval filter; appending updates without deleting stale versions (drift); no evaluation (can't tell if changes help); reranking or agentic loops with no latency/cost budget; and no abstention path (hallucinates on unanswerable queries). Naming these unprompted shows you've operated RAG, not just built a demo.

The deliverable that matters most is the before/after evaluation report — "naive RAG scored 0.61 faithfulness / 0.55 context-recall; adding hybrid+rerank took it to 0.78 / 0.81; here's the RAGAS table and three example queries." That single artifact demonstrates you can measure, improve, and justify — exactly what the whole resource has been training.

How to talk about it

In interviews, lead with the decisions and measurements, not the framework. "I routed by complexity to control cost, used hybrid+RRF because exact SKUs were failing, and proved the reranker helped with a +0.15 context-precision delta on my golden set" beats "I used LangChain and Pinecone." The capstone's value is the story of trade-offs it lets you tell.

5.11 Core papers to know by name

Five papers cover the arc from "what is RAG" to the 2024 techniques. You don't need every detail — you need the one idea each contributed and why it mattered, so you can name-drop them accurately (tracker items 198–202).

PaperThe one ideaMaps to
Lewis et al., 2020 — "Retrieval-Augmented Generation…"The original RAG: combine a parametric LLM with a non-parametric retriever, trained end-to-end. Defined the pattern.§1.1–1.2
Asai et al., 2024 — Self-RAGTrain the model to emit reflection tokens: retrieve on demand, judge passage relevance, and self-check that its output is supported.§3.7
Yan et al., 2024 — Corrective RAG (CRAG)A lightweight retrieval evaluator grades results and triggers a web-search fallback when they're insufficient.§3.8
Microsoft, 2024 — GraphRAGBuild a knowledge graph + community summaries so RAG can answer multi-hop and corpus-wide "global" questions.§3.1
Anthropic, 2024 — Contextual Retrieval (blog)Prepend LLM-generated context to each chunk before embedding (and BM25), sharply cutting retrieval failures.§2.8
Interview tip · cite the idea, not just the title

Saying "Self-RAG adds reflection tokens so the model decides when to retrieve and whether its output is grounded" is far stronger than "there's a paper called Self-RAG." Anchor each name to its one contribution and the problem it solved — that's what signals you've read (or at least genuinely understood) the work.
