The questions product companies actually ask Active-user metrics, funnel conversion and the ordered funnel, cohort retention triangles, day-N retention, the new/retained/churned/reactivated decomposition, sessionisation, an A/B readout, attribution and basket pairs.

Meta, Amazon, Uber, Stripe and every consumer company built on an event table ask from this list, and they ask it because the SQL is a proxy for whether you can define a metric. “What is the conversion rate?” has at least four defensible answers depending on the denominator and on whether the steps must happen in order — and the demo event table here deliberately contains a user who checked out before adding to cart, so the naive funnel and the ordered funnel disagree. Getting the query right is table stakes; saying which definition you chose and why is the actual answer.

7.1 DAU, WAU, MAU and stickiness ⭐

The simplest metrics in the book, and the ones most often computed with the wrong denominator.

The definitions: DAU is distinct users active on a day; WAU and MAU are distinct users active in a trailing window, not in a calendar week or month. That distinction matters — “MAU for March” and “MAU as of 31 March” are different numbers, and dashboards mix them constantly.

Stickiness = DAU/MAU, the fraction of monthly users who show up on a given day. Roughly: 10% is a monthly-habit product, 50%+ is a daily-habit product. It is a ratio of two distinct counts, so it cannot be derived from a sum — you have to count both.

Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonCompute DAU and MAU. What is stickiness?
              
DAU is COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) per day; MAU is the same over a trailing 28 or 30 days. Stickiness is DAU/MAU.
Three clarifications that separate a good answer. Trailing window, not calendar month — “MAU” almost always means the last 28 days as of a date, and computing it per calendar month gives a different, non-comparable series. Define “active” — any event, or a meaningful action? A login is not engagement, and the metric is only as good as the event you count. Deduplicate — COUNT(*) over an events table counts events, not users, and the two diverge exactly when engagement rises.
Then the practical note: MAU cannot be summed from DAU, because it is a distinct count over a window. At scale you precompute daily HyperLogLog sketches and merge them, which is the only way to get rolling uniques without rescanning.

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.2 Funnel conversion ⭐⭐

        
Count distinct users at each step and divide. The SQL is easy; the definition is the interview.


        
Two rates, both called “conversion”: step-to-step (of those who added to cart, how many checked out) and overall (of those who viewed, how many purchased). A funnel report should show both, and if you present one without labelling it you have guessed which the stakeholder meant.

Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonBuild a funnel from an events table.
              
Count distinct users per step, then divide. Keep the step list in a VALUES table joined LEFT so a step with zero users still appears, and use window functions for the rates: LAG for step-to-step, FIRST_VALUE for overall.
Before writing anything, ask three questions. Must the steps be in order? A user who checked out before adding to cart counts in the naive version and should not (§7.3). Within what time window? A view in January and a purchase in June are not one funnel — real funnels are bounded, usually by session or by a fixed lookback. Distinct users or events? Counting events lets one user inflate a step.
The presentation point: show step-to-step and overall rates, plus the absolute drop-off, because the drop-off is what a product manager acts on. And name the largest single drop explicitly — that is the answer to “what would you tell the team?”

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.3 The funnel that must be ordered in time ⭐⭐

        
The follow-up that catches the naive funnel. A user who reached step 3 without ever completing step 2 is not a converted user, and counting them inflates every rate.


        
The technique: collapse each user to their first timestamp per step, then require each step's timestamp to be strictly later than the previous one. That turns “did it happen” into “did it happen in order”.

Expected: the naive checkout count is 4, the ordered count is 3. User 5 checked out at 08:03 and added to cart at 08:05 — two minutes after — so they never really traversed the funnel. On a 7-user sample that is a 14-point error in the conversion rate. On real event data, out-of-order events are common (client-side clock skew, retries, batched sends), which is why the ordered version is the one to ship.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
GotchaHow do you make sure funnel steps happened in the right order?
              
Reduce each user to their first timestamp per step with conditional MIN, then require strict ordering: t_cart > t_view, t_check > t_cart, and so on. Without that, a user who hit a later step first is counted as having converted.
Add a time bound too, because an unbounded funnel is meaningless: either the steps must occur in the same session, or within N days of the first step. That is AND t_buy < datetime(t_view, '+7 days') or a session join.
Why out-of-order events are common enough to matter: client-side clocks drift, mobile SDKs batch and retry, and server-side and client-side events arrive on different paths. So this is not a contrived edge case — it is the normal state of an event table.
If the engine has a native funnel function — ClickHouse's windowFunnel, Snowflake's MATCH_RECOGNIZE, BigQuery's session functions — name it, because it does exactly this with the ordering and the window built in.

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.4 Cohort retention as a triangle ⭐⭐

        
The canonical analytics question. Three steps, and the third one is where people get lost.


        
Step 1: assign each user a cohort — usually the period of their first activity. Step 2: list every period each user was active. Step 3: compute the offset between the two, and count distinct users per (cohort, offset). That offset column is what makes the triangle; grouping by absolute period instead gives you a calendar table, which is a different and less useful chart.

        
The offset arithmetic is the fiddly bit. Do not subtract date strings — convert to a monotonic period number (year*12 + month) and subtract those. In PostgreSQL it is cleaner: (DATE_TRUNC('month', act) - DATE_TRUNC('month', cohort)) divided by an interval, or just EXTRACT the parts.

Month 2 being higher than month 1 is not an error — it is resurrection, a customer returning after a gap, and a retention curve that only ever falls has usually been computed as “still active every month since” rather than “active in month N”. Those are different metrics and §7.6 separates them.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonWrite a monthly cohort retention query.
              
Three CTEs. Cohort assignment: each user's first-activity month. Activity: distinct user-months. Grid: join them and compute month_n as the difference in months. Then count distinct users per (cohort, month_n) and divide by the cohort size.
Details that matter. Compute the offset from a monotonic period number (year*12 + month), never by subtracting formatted strings. Get the denominator from the cohort table, not from month 0 of the grid — they are the same here but diverge if your activity definition differs from your cohort definition. And use DISTINCT on users, or a heavy user inflates their cohort.
Two things to volunteer. Incomplete cohorts: the most recent cohort has had less time to be retained, so its later columns are empty rather than zero — flag or exclude them, because a naive chart reads that as a collapse in retention. And the metric definition: “active in month N” allows resurrection and can rise; “active in every month up to N” is survival and only falls. Say which you built.

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.5 Day-N retention and the denominator question ⭐

        
“What is our day-7 retention?” has at least three answers, and the number can differ by a factor of two.


        

          
Day-N exact
Active on exactly day 7. Strictest, and low by construction. Standard in mobile gaming.

          
Day-N bounded
Active on day 7 or later, within a window. More forgiving, and what most B2B products mean.

          
Unbounded / rolling
Active at any point in days 1–7. Highest number. Often quoted without saying so.

        


        
The denominator matters as much as the numerator. Is it everyone who signed up, or everyone who completed onboarding, or everyone who had the opportunity to reach day 7 — that is, signed up at least 7 days ago? Including users who signed up yesterday in a day-7 denominator drags the metric down mechanically as you grow, which makes a growing product look like a churning one.


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonWhat is day-7 retention, and how would you compute it?
              
Start by defining it, because the term is genuinely ambiguous: active on exactly day 7, active on day 7 or later, or active at any point within the first 7 days. Those give very different numbers and all three are called “D7” in the wild.
Then the denominator, which is where the real mistake lives: it must be users who had the opportunity to reach day 7 — signed up at least 7 days before the reporting date. Including recent signups makes the metric fall automatically as acquisition grows, which is a growth signal masquerading as a churn signal.
The query shape: join signups to activity on activity_date = signup_date + 7 (or a range for the bounded version), count distinct retained users, divide by the eligible cohort size. Drive from the signup table with a LEFT JOIN so non-retained users are zeros rather than missing rows.
The point to close on: whichever definition you choose, write it in the column name — d7_exact_retention_pct — so nobody downstream has to guess. Metric ambiguity causes more disagreement in analytics than wrong SQL does.

Why it is worth the effort: flat MAU can hide a business that is churning as fast as it acquires. Check the identity: new + retained + reactivated should equal active_total in every row, because the three states partition the active users. If it does not, two of your CASE conditions overlap — and running that check is the habit worth taking away, not the query.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonMAU has been flat for six months. What do you look at?
              
Decompose it. Flat MAU is consistent with a healthy business and with one that is churning exactly as fast as it acquires, and those need opposite responses. Split each period into new, retained, reactivated and churned and the two cases look completely different.
The query is a set of EXISTS flags per user per period — active now, active in the previous period, ever active before — and a conditional count over the four combinations. Drive from a user × period grid so churned users (not active now) still get a row.
Then check the identity active(t) = active(t-1) + new + reactivated − churned. If it balances, your definitions are mutually exclusive; if not, they overlap and the report is double-counting somebody.
What I would actually report: the trend in each of the four components, plus retention by cohort (§7.4), because a falling retention curve with rising acquisition is the classic pattern behind flat MAU and it tells you to fix the product rather than buy more traffic.

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.7 Sessionisation ⭐⭐

        
Group an event stream into sessions by inactivity gap. Three lines, and the same three lines solve every problem in §8.


        
The pattern: LAG the previous timestamp, flag rows whose gap exceeds the threshold as a new session boundary, then take a running SUM of the flags — the running sum is the session id. That flag-then-cumsum move is the most reusable idiom in analytical SQL.

The path column is a bonus that falls out for free and is genuinely useful: it shows the event sequence per session, which is how you spot the out-of-order checkout in user 5 without writing the §7.3 query at all.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonGroup events into sessions with a 30-minute inactivity timeout.
              
Three steps. LAG(ts) per user to get the previous event time. Flag a new session where the gap exceeds 30 minutes, or where there is no previous event. Running SUM of that flag, partitioned by user and ordered by time — the cumulative sum is the session number.
Say the general form out loud, because it is what makes this transferable: flag the boundary, then cumulative-sum the flags to get a group id. The same three lines do gaps-and-islands (§8.1), streak detection, interval merging and run-length encoding. Recognising that one idiom covers all of them is worth more than any individual query.
Details worth raising. Make the session id globally unique if it will be joined — concatenate user_id || '-' || session_no. Add a tie-break to the ORDER BY if timestamps can repeat, or two simultaneous events land unpredictably. And consider whether sessions should also be capped by absolute duration, since a user clicking every 29 minutes for eight hours is arguably not one session.
If the engine has native support — Snowflake's CONDITIONAL_TRUE_EVENT, BigQuery's session functions, ClickHouse's sessionize — name it, but the portable version above is the one to be able to write cold.

Expected: control 3/10 = 30%, treatment 5/10 = 50%, a relative lift of 66.7% — and standard errors around 0.145 and 0.158, which are enormous relative to the 20-point difference. With n=10 per arm this result is indistinguishable from noise, and saying so is the answer. The sample-ratio check is the second habit: a 50/50 assignment that comes back 52/48 on large n means the assignment or the logging is broken, and you investigate before analysing.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonRead out an A/B test in SQL.
              
Per arm: sample size, conversions, conversion rate, and the primary metric's mean. Then absolute and relative lift versus control. The standard error of a proportion, SQRT(p*(1-p)/n), is computable in SQL and worth including because it immediately shows whether the difference is meaningful.
The two checks to run before believing anything. Sample ratio mismatch — if you assigned 50/50 and observe a materially different split on large n, assignment or logging is broken and the result is void. Pre-experiment balance — the arms should look alike on metrics that predate the treatment.
Then the boundary of what SQL should do: a p-value or confidence interval is better computed in Python or R, and more importantly the decision is not statistical. Report the effect size with an interval, not a bare significance verdict, and say whether the lift is economically worth shipping. A statistically significant 0.1% lift on a metric nobody cares about is not a result.
Also flag peeking: repeatedly querying a running experiment and stopping when it looks good inflates the false-positive rate badly. Fixed horizon, or a sequential method designed for continuous monitoring.

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.9 First-touch and last-touch attribution

        
Credit a conversion to a touchpoint. The SQL is FIRST_VALUE and LAST_VALUE; the interesting part is that every model is a choice, not a fact.


        
First-touch credits the channel that acquired the user; last-touch credits the one that closed. Both are one window function over the touchpoints preceding a conversion. Linear, time-decay and position-based models spread the credit; data-driven attribution fits a model. None of them is true — attribution is an allocation convention, and the honest framing is that incrementality tests are what actually measure causal effect.

Note FIRST_VALUE(... ORDER BY ts DESC) for last-touch rather than LAST_VALUE — the §5.7 idiom, avoiding the default-frame trap. And note that only users who purchased appear at all: attribution is conditioned on conversion, which is precisely why it cannot measure incrementality. The users who saw the same touchpoints and did not convert are invisible to it.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
ConceptualFirst-touch or last-touch attribution — which is right?
              
Neither, and that is the answer. Both are allocation conventions, not measurements. First-touch over-credits awareness channels and ignores everything that closed the sale; last-touch over-credits branded search and retargeting, which often capture demand that already existed.
The SQL is trivial either way — FIRST_VALUE over the touchpoints before the conversion, ordered ascending or descending. So what is being tested is whether you understand the limitation.
The structural problem: attribution only looks at converters. It has nothing to say about users who saw the same touchpoints and did not convert, so it cannot distinguish a channel that caused conversions from one that merely appeared alongside them. That is why incrementality is measured with geo holdouts, PSA tests or MMM, not with attribution.
What I would recommend in practice: report last-touch for operational routing because it is simple and stable, report first-touch alongside it so the acquisition channels are visible, and treat both as directional while insisting on a holdout test before reallocating real budget.

            

          

        

      


      

        
7.10 Co-purchase pairs and basket analysis

        
“What is bought together?” A self-join with one inequality, and a real scale caveat.


        
The query: self-join the order-items table on the same order with a.product_id < b.product_id. The strict inequality keeps each unordered pair once and excludes self-pairs — the same trick as §2.7.

        
The caveat to raise: an order with k items produces k(k−1)/2 pairs, so this grows quadratically in basket size. A 50-item basket is 1,225 rows. On real retail data you cap the basket size, sample, or move to a purpose-built frequent-itemset algorithm — and volunteering that is better than volunteering the query.


        

          
Run itco-purchase pairs with support and liftidle

          
          

            
            
            
            ⌘/Ctrl + Enter
          

          

          
Read lift, not together. Raw co-occurrence counts just rank the popular items — of course the best-seller appears in the most pairs. Lift divides observed co-occurrence by what independence would predict, so lift > 1 means a genuine association. With a basket this small the numbers are noisy, which is itself the lesson: association rules need a minimum support threshold before they mean anything.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
CodingFind the products most often bought together.
              
Self-join the line-items table on order_id with a.product_id < b.product_id, group by the pair, count. The strict inequality is load-bearing: <> would return every pair twice in both orders, and no condition at all would pair each product with itself.
Then improve the metric, because raw counts are misleading — they rank by popularity. Support is the fraction of orders containing the pair; confidence is the conditional probability of B given A; lift is support divided by the product of the individual supports, so lift > 1 means the pair co-occurs more than independence predicts. Lift is what you actually rank on.
The scale caveat to volunteer: pairs grow as k(k−1)/2 in basket size, so a large-basket retailer generates a combinatorial explosion. Mitigations are a minimum-support pre-filter, capping basket size, sampling, or using FP-Growth / Apriori outside SQL. Also mention that lift on low-support pairs is extremely noisy — two items bought together once can show enormous lift and mean nothing, which is why a support floor comes first.
