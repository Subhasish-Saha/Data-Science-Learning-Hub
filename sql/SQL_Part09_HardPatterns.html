9.1 Median without a percentile function ⭐⭐

The classic derivation question. Two standard approaches, and one of them silently returns the wrong answer on tied values.

Why it is asked: the median is not an aggregate you can compose out of SUM and COUNT — it depends on order — and MySQL and SQLite have no percentile function at all. So you have to build it, and building it forces you to think about even versus odd row counts and about ties.

Method 1 — (n+1)/2 and (n+2)/2
Number the rows, take those two positions, average them. Odd n: both point at the middle row. Even n: they straddle it. Robust.
Method 2 — number from both ends
Number ascending and descending, keep rows where the two numbers are within one. Elegant — and wrong unless the ordering is a total order, because ties are numbered arbitrarily in each direction.

The both-ends trick assumes a + d = n + 1 for every row, which only holds if the ordering is unambiguous — and with four tied pairs in this table the ascending and descending numberings disagree about which tied row is which. Add the id tie-break and it is correct. This is §4.10 being not merely tidy but load-bearing.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonCalculate the median without a built-in percentile function.
              
Number the rows in order, count them, and average the values at positions (n+1)/2 and (n+2)/2 using integer division. For odd n both expressions give the same middle position, so the average is that single value; for even n they give the two straddling positions. One formula, no CASE on parity.
SELECT AVG(x) FROM (SELECT x, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY x, id) rn, COUNT(*) OVER () n FROM t) WHERE rn IN ((n+1)/2, (n+2)/2)
Include a tie-break in the ORDER BY. The popular “number from both ends and keep where they meet” trick relies on the two numberings being exact mirrors, which fails when values tie — it returns a wrong median silently. That is a genuinely good thing to raise, because most people have only seen the trick presented as correct.
Where the engine helps: PostgreSQL and Oracle have PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY x), which interpolates, and PERCENTILE_DISC, which returns an actual data value. BigQuery, Snowflake and Spark have APPROX_QUANTILES/MEDIAN. MySQL and SQLite have nothing, which is why this question exists.
The follow-up is always “median per group”: add PARTITION BY to both windows. And the reason it matters: for skewed distributions — income, order value, latency — the median is the honest central tendency and the mean is not.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.2 Percentiles, discrete and continuous ⭐

        
Two definitions of “the 90th percentile” that give different numbers, and knowing which one your engine used is the whole answer.


        

          
PERCENTILE_DISC
Returns an actual value from the data — the smallest value whose cumulative distribution reaches the fraction. Use for “which customer is at p90”.

          
PERCENTILE_CONT
Interpolates between the two neighbouring values. Returns a number that may appear nowhere in the data. Use for SLO thresholds and latency budgets.

        


        
Why it matters operationally: a p99 latency target computed with the discrete definition is an observed request; computed with the continuous one it is a blend of two. On small samples they can differ substantially, and two teams quoting “p99” from different tools will disagree. Also worth knowing: warehouse APPROX_QUANTILES functions trade a small error for a large speed-up, and their error is rank-based, so p50 is accurate and p99.9 much less so.

Likely interview questions

          

            
ConceptualPERCENTILE_CONT vs PERCENTILE_DISC?
              
PERCENTILE_DISC returns a value that actually exists in the data — the first value whose cumulative distribution reaches the requested fraction. PERCENTILE_CONT interpolates linearly between the two surrounding values, so its result may not appear in the data at all.
Both are ordered-set aggregates, written PERCENTILE_CONT(0.9) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY x), and they exist in PostgreSQL, Oracle, SQL Server (as window functions) and Snowflake. MySQL and SQLite have neither, so you compute them by hand.
Which to use: DISC when the answer must be a real observation (“which order is at the 90th percentile”), CONT for thresholds and SLOs where a synthetic boundary is fine. The practical warning is that two tools reporting “p95” may be using different definitions, which is a common source of disagreement between a dashboard and an alerting system.
At scale, mention APPROX_QUANTILES / t-digest / KLL sketches: exact percentiles need a global sort, approximate ones are mergeable and near-linear, and their error is on the rank rather than the value — so extreme tails are the least accurate part.

The most frequent value. Easy, except that the mode is frequently not unique — and returning one of several arbitrarily is the bug.

Expected: four modal salaries — 210000, 145000, 120000 and 98000 — each appearing twice. There is no single mode in this data, and the ROW_NUMBER version picks one of the four without telling you the other three exist. That is the difference between reporting a distribution and asserting a fact about it.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
CodingFind the most common value in a column.
              
GROUP BY the value, count, then rank by the count descending and keep rank 1. Use RANK, not ROW_NUMBER, because the mode is often not unique and returning one arbitrary winner hides that.
PostgreSQL has it as an ordered-set aggregate: MODE() WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY x) — though note it returns only one of the tied values, so if multimodality matters you still write it by hand.
Two clarifications worth asking about. NULLs: should “unknown” be able to win the mode? COUNT(*) per group counts them; excluding them is a deliberate choice either way. Multimodality: if the top two frequencies are 101 and 100, calling the first “the mode” is technically right and analytically misleading — reporting the top few counts is more useful than reporting a single winner.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.4 Weighted averages and compressed histograms ⭐

        
When each row represents many observations, AVG is the wrong function — and the mistake is easy to miss because it returns a plausible number.


        
The rule: a weighted average is SUM(value * weight) / SUM(weight), never AVG(value). If your table has a quantity or count column, AVG treats a row representing 1,000 units the same as a row representing 1.

        
The same applies to averaging averages. AVG(daily_avg) over 30 days is not the monthly average unless every day had the same number of observations. You need SUM(daily_total) / SUM(daily_count) — this is Simpson's-paradox territory, and it appears in real dashboards constantly.

Whenever you see AVG applied to a column that is itself an average, that is the bug.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
GotchaWhy is the average of the daily averages not the monthly average?
              
Because AVG of averages weights every day equally regardless of how many observations each day contained. A Tuesday with 10,000 orders and a Sunday with 100 contribute the same amount to AVG(daily_avg), which is almost never what anyone means.
The pooled mean is SUM(total) / SUM(count) — equivalently AVG over the raw rows. The two coincide only when every group is the same size.
This is the same structure as Simpson's paradox: an aggregate over unequal groups can point the opposite way from the groups themselves, so a conversion rate can rise in every segment and fall overall. Naming that connection is what makes this a strong answer rather than a formula.
The general rule: keep the numerator and denominator, and divide at the end. Store SUM(x) and COUNT(x) in intermediate tables rather than a precomputed ratio, because sums are re-aggregatable and ratios are not. That is also why a well-built metrics layer materialises sums and counts, never averages.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.5 Combinations and pairs ⭐

        
A self-join with a strict inequality, and a quadratic growth rate you should mention before you are asked.


        
a.id < b.id gives each unordered pair exactly once and excludes self-pairs. <> gives every pair twice; no condition also pairs each row with itself. For triples, chain two inequalities: a.id < b.id AND b.id < c.id.

        
The growth: n choose k. Pairs from 1,000 rows is about 500,000; triples is about 166 million. So “all combinations” on anything sizeable is a query to push back on, and saying so is better engineering than running it.

The habit worth having is computing that number before running the join.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
CodingFind all pairs of items that appear in the same order.
              
Self-join on the order id with a.product_id < b.product_id. The strict inequality does two jobs at once: it excludes self-pairs and it keeps only one ordering of each pair, so you do not get (A,B) and (B,A).
Then volunteer the cost, because that is the senior part of the answer: a basket of k items yields k(k−1)/2 pairs, so the output grows quadratically in basket size and a handful of large baskets dominates the whole result. On real retail data you cap the basket size, apply a minimum-support filter first, sample, or move to a frequent-itemset algorithm outside SQL.
And the metric point: rank the pairs by lift, not by raw co-occurrence, or you simply rediscover which products are popular (§7.10).

That difference between “span of control” and “organisation size” is exactly what the recursive version buys you over a self-join.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonCount everyone in each manager's reporting line, at any depth.
              
Recursive CTE that pairs each ancestor with all descendants: anchor every employee to themselves, then repeatedly attach employees whose manager is already in the set, carrying the original root through. Group by root and count, subtracting one for the root itself.
Say why a self-join cannot do it: each join adds exactly one level, so you would need as many joins as the tree is deep, and the depth is data-dependent. That is precisely what recursion is for.
Three details that show experience. Cycle guard — carry a depth counter or a path and cap it, because a manager loop makes the recursion non-terminating; SQL Server caps at 100 by default, PostgreSQL and SQLite do not. UNION ALL, not UNION — the deduplication is wasted work and can hide a cycle. Carry a path string if you want to render the tree or order it hierarchically, since ORDER BY path gives a correct depth-first ordering for free.
The alternative worth naming for read-heavy workloads: materialise a closure table of (ancestor, descendant, depth) pairs, or store a materialised path like /1/8/10/. Both make subtree queries a simple prefix match at the cost of maintenance on writes.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.7 Running maximum and drawdown ⭐

        
A running MAX over an expanding frame, and the gap between it and the current value. Peak-to-trough in three lines.


        
MAX(x) OVER (ORDER BY t ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) is the high-water mark to date; x / peak - 1 is the drawdown. The maximum drawdown is the minimum of that series. It is the same shape as the “worst decline from a peak” question in finance and the “largest drop in a metric” question in analytics.

Note the explicit ROWS frame. With the default RANGE frame (§5.2) and duplicate dates, the running maximum would include every row sharing the current date, so the “peak so far” could include a value from later the same day — a small look-ahead. Aggregating to one row per day first, as here, also removes the ambiguity.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
CodingCompute the maximum drawdown of a daily series.
              
Running maximum with an expanding frame, then the percentage gap: rev / MAX(rev) OVER (ORDER BY d ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) - 1. The most negative value of that series is the maximum drawdown, and MIN gives it.
Three points worth making. Write the ROWS frame explicitly, because the default is RANGE and with duplicate timestamps the “peak so far” would peek at same-key rows. Aggregate to one row per period first, both for that reason and because drawdown on raw transactions is not a meaningful series. And report the dates — a drawdown number without the peak date and trough date is not actionable.
The natural extension is the recovery: the first date after the trough where the series regains the old peak, which is a MIN(d) over rows after the trough with rev >= peak. That turns one number into a story, and it is usually what the question is really after.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.8 Near-duplicate detection

        
Exact duplicates are §10.1. This is the harder version: records that refer to the same real thing without matching on any column.


        
The strategy is always the same shape: build a blocking key that is cheap and coarse, compare only within blocks, and score the candidates. Comparing every pair is quadratic and unnecessary — blocking on the first three characters of a surname plus a postcode prefix reduces a billion comparisons to a few million.

        
What SQL can do: normalisation (case, whitespace, punctuation), SOUNDEX and DIFFERENCE in SQL Server, levenshtein and similarity via PostgreSQL's fuzzystrmatch and pg_trgm, and trigram indexes to make similarity search fast. What it cannot: proper probabilistic record linkage, which belongs in a dedicated tool.

The blocking step is the important idea: without it you compare all pairs, and with a coarse key you compare only plausible ones. Note also that blocking is a recall/cost trade — a key that is too tight will miss a genuine duplicate whose email prefix differs.

        


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
ConceptualHow would you find customer records that are the same person but do not match exactly?
              
Three stages, and naming them is the answer. Normalise — casefold, trim, strip punctuation, canonicalise phone and postcode formats. Block — build a coarse key (postcode prefix, surname initial plus year of birth, email domain) and only compare records within the same block, because all-pairs comparison is quadratic. Score — compute a similarity per field and combine into a match probability, then set a threshold and route the middle band to human review.
What SQL is good for: the normalisation and the blocking, plus exact-after-normalisation matching, which usually catches most of the volume. What it is bad for: the scoring. Levenshtein and trigram similarity exist as extensions, but proper probabilistic linkage (Fellegi–Sunter, or a tool like Splink) belongs outside SQL.
The point I would emphasise: this is a precision/recall problem, not a correctness problem. There is no threshold that is simply right — merging two real customers is a different kind of harm from failing to merge one person's two records, and which error you prefer is a business decision. Presenting it that way, with a review queue for the uncertain band, is what distinguishes an experienced answer.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.9 SCD Type 2 and slowly changing history ⭐⭐

        
The dimensional-modelling pattern that makes point-in-time correctness possible. Reading it is §2.9; building it is a run-length encoding.


        
What Type 2 is: instead of overwriting a changed attribute, you close the old row (set its valid_to) and insert a new one. The result is a history table with non-overlapping half-open validity windows per key, which is exactly what an as-of join needs.

        
Type 1 overwrites and loses history. Type 3 keeps a “previous value” column and so remembers exactly one change. Type 2 is the one worth knowing because it is the only one that supports “what did we believe on this date”.

Likely interview questions

          

            
Very commonExplain slowly changing dimensions and how you would query one.
              
Type 1 overwrites the attribute — simple, and history is gone. Type 2 closes the current row and inserts a new one, giving a full history with valid_from/valid_to and usually an is_current flag. Type 3 adds a “previous value” column, remembering exactly one change.
Querying Type 2 is the point of the question. For the current state, filter is_current = 1. For point-in-time, join on the key and the validity window with half-open bounds: ON f.key = d.key AND f.event_ts >= d.valid_from AND f.event_ts < d.valid_to. Using BETWEEN on both ends makes the boundary instant match two versions and duplicates the fact.
Two practical details. Use a sentinel like '9999-12-31' rather than NULL for the open-ended valid_to, because NULL fails the comparison and silently drops the current version. And validate the table: for each key, consecutive windows should tile exactly — a LEAD(valid_from) check catches both overlaps, which duplicate facts, and gaps, which lose them.
Building one from snapshots is run-length encoding: flag where the attribute changes, cumsum to get runs, take each run's first date as valid_from, and use LEAD to set valid_to.

            

          

        

      


      

        
9.10 The query you should refuse to write

        
A category of answer that is not SQL at all, and is sometimes the most valuable thing you can say in an interview.


        
Some requests are better declined, redirected or renegotiated. Recognising them is a seniority signal, and interviewers who ask open-ended questions are often listening for exactly this.


        

          

            
The requestWhy it is a problemWhat to propose instead

            
              
“All combinations of these two large tables”Quadratic; will not finish, and nobody can read the outputAsk what decision it feeds; almost always an aggregate or a top-N answers it

              
“SELECT * from the events table into Excel”Scanned-bytes cost, transfer time, and a stale copy that will be used as a source of truthAn aggregated extract, or a view they can query

              
“Just DELETE the duplicates in production”Irreversible, and “duplicate” is undefined until someone chooses a ruleAgree the key and the survivor rule, SELECT the victims, review, then delete in a transaction

              
“Make this dashboard query real-time”Often a request for an architecture, disguised as a query optimisationAsk what latency is actually needed; incremental models usually beat streaming

              
“Add this one column, it is urgent” (to a 400-line query nobody owns)Each such addition compounds; the query becomes unmaintainableAdd it, and log the refactor — but say the cost out loud rather than absorbing it silently

              
“Compute the p99 across the whole history, live”Exact percentiles need a global sortApproximate quantile sketches, or pre-aggregated windows

            
          

        


        
How to say it. Not “no”. The move is to ask what decision the output supports, and then offer something that supports that decision more cheaply. “I can run that, and it will take four hours and produce 40 million rows — can I ask what you'll do with it? If it's to find the worst offenders, I can have the top 100 in a minute.” That is a better answer than either compliance or refusal, and it is the answer a good interviewer is hoping for.


        

          
Likely interview questions

          

            
BehaviouralA stakeholder asks for a query you think is a bad idea. What do you do?
              
Ask what decision it feeds, before arguing about the query. Most bad requests are a reasonable goal expressed as a specific mechanism, and the goal usually has a cheaper implementation. “All transactions for the year in a spreadsheet” is often really “which accounts are anomalous”.
Then be concrete about the cost, in their units: runtime, money, or the risk of a stale extract becoming an unofficial source of truth. “This scans 3 TB and costs about £15 per run” is a fact they can act on; “that's inefficient” is not.
Then offer the alternative and let them choose. If they still want the original, and it is not destructive, do it — you flagged the trade-off and the decision is theirs. That last part matters: the goal is to be the person who surfaces costs, not the person who blocks work.
The exception is anything irreversible on production data. There, insist on the process — a SELECT of exactly what will change, a review, a transaction, a rollback plan — because a wrong DELETE is not a slow query, it is an incident (§10.2).
