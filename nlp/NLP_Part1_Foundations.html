Part 1 of 7 · Foundations & Classical NLP

The layer everything else is built on Why language is hard, the task taxonomy that decides your architecture, the linguistic pipeline, and the classical representations — BoW, TF-IDF, LSA and LDA — that modern NLP is a reaction to.

Do not skip this part because “everything is an LLM now.” Interviewers use it as a filter: it is where they find out whether you understand why the modern stack looks the way it does. Almost every trap question in this subject is a classical question wearing modern clothes — and the honest 2026 answer to “is classical NLP dead?” is that it moved down the stack. You still tokenize, still normalise, still run regex and gazetteer precision passes, still report entity-level F1. This part covers the task taxonomy (name the task type and the architecture family follows), the full linguistic pipeline, and the classical representation ladder up to the distributional hypothesis — the single sentence that every embedding method implements.

1.1 Why language is hard

Every technique in this subject exists to work around one of four properties of language. Name the property and the technique stops being arbitrary.

Interviewers rarely ask “why is NLP hard” directly. They ask it obliquely — “why can't you one-hot encode words and use a random forest?” — and the answer is a list of properties, not a list of tools.

PropertyWhat it meansWhat it forces into existence
AmbiguityLexical (“bank”), syntactic (“I saw the man with the telescope”), semantic scope, pragmatic reference (“it”)Contextual representations; parsing; coreference
DiscretenessWords are symbols with no natural metric. Pixels have distance; “cat” and “dog” do notEvery embedding method ever invented
Compositionality & distanceMeaning depends on structure, and on tokens arbitrarily far awayRecurrence, then attention
Zipf's lawFrequency ∝ 1/rank — a handful of words dominate, and the tail never endsSubword tokenization, smoothing, the <UNK> problem
MorphologyEnglish is morphologically poor; Turkish, Finnish and Tamil are notLemmatization, character n-grams, SentencePiece
VariationTypos, code-switching, transliteration, domain driftRobustness testing, byte-level tokenizers, drift monitoring
The answer they're looking for

“Why not one-hot + random forest?” One-hot vectors are mutually orthogonal, so the cosine similarity between any two distinct words is exactly 0 — the representation encodes no similarity at all. Add |V| ≈ 10⁵–10⁶ dimensions, no word order, and no way to represent a word you didn't see in training, and you have described every limitation the next 1.13–1.17 sections fix.

1.2 The task taxonomy ⭐

The single most useful habit in an NLP interview: before proposing anything, name the task type. It determines the architecture family, the metric, and the labelling cost — all three, automatically.

Candidates who skip this step end up recommending a decoder LLM for a job an encoder does better and 50× cheaper, or reporting accuracy on a task where accuracy is meaningless. Candidates who do it sound senior in one sentence.

Watch the framing

“Extract the customer's issue from this email” can be any of four task types — classification into a taxonomy, span extraction, seq2seq generation, or structured extraction into JSON — and each choice implies a different metric and a different annotation budget. Clarifying which one is the first thing a strong candidate does, and it is usually the difference between a good design answer and a rambling one.

1.3 The five eras — and why all five still get asked

Interview questions are archaeological. A question about HMMs is really a question about generative vs discriminative models; a question about TF-IDF is really about sparse vs dense representation.

The senior answer to “is classical NLP dead?”

No — it moved down the stack. You still tokenize, still normalise, still write regex and gazetteer passes for precision, still report entity-level F1, and you frequently distil an LLM into a small encoder because an LLM at 40 ms and 1,000 QPS is unaffordable. What died is hand-built feature engineering as the primary source of signal, not the classical layer itself.

1.4 Data before models

Most NLP failures are data failures wearing a modelling costume. Two of the items below cause more “great offline, terrible online” incidents than every architecture choice combined.

Your label guidelines are the real model specification. If two annotators disagree on 25% of cases, no architecture will exceed that ceiling — and your test set is measuring noise. Before touching a model, a strong candidate asks: who labelled this, against what guideline, and how much did they agree?

Data issueSymptomFix
Near-duplicate leakageExcellent test metrics, poor production metricsMinHash/LSH dedup before splitting — not after
Random split on grouped dataSame customer/document in train and testSplit by document, user, or time
Label noiseMetric plateaus below the achievable ceilingAudit 200 random labels; confident learning (Cleanlab)
Class imbalanceHigh accuracy, useless recall on the class you care aboutMacro-F1, class weights, threshold tuning
Low annotator agreementCeiling on any measurable improvementRewrite the guideline; consider soft labels
Licensing / PII in the corpusA legal problem discovered post-launchProvenance manifest, de-identification pass
The classic interview trap

“More data always helps.” Not with a 3% label error rate, not with near-duplicate leakage between train and test, and not with a drifted distribution. A smaller, clean, correctly-split dataset routinely beats a larger dirty one — and it makes every subsequent experiment interpretable.

1.5 Normalization

The step where well-intentioned cleaning quietly destroys signal — and where the correct amount depends entirely on what comes next.

The toolkit: Unicode normalization (NFC/NFKC), casefolding, accent stripping, whitespace and punctuation handling, HTML/boilerplate removal, encoding-error repair (mojibake), contraction expansion, spelling correction, emoji handling, and placeholder substitution for URLs, numbers and dates.

1.6 Classical tokenization

Splitting text into units, before subwords existed. This is where the OOV problem is born — which makes it the setup for the whole of Part 3.

Approaches: whitespace splitting, regex/Penn-Treebank rules, and learned models. Sentence segmentation is a model, not a split('.') — abbreviations (Mr., e.g.), decimals, ellipses and quoted speech all break the naive version. Languages without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai) turn word segmentation into its own supervised task.

Where this leads

Word-level tokenization implies a fixed vocabulary, which implies out-of-vocabulary words, which implies <UNK> and all its downstream damage. Subword tokenization (and §3.9–3.15 in Part 3) exists precisely to dissolve that chain.

1.7 Stemming vs lemmatization

A guaranteed interview question, usually asked together with tokenization — and the interesting half is when you would use either in 2026.

TokenizationStemmingLemmatization
PurposeSplit text into unitsCrude affix removalMap to a dictionary lemma
OutputTokensA root, possibly not a wordA real base word
NeedsRules or a modelRules onlyLexicon + POS + morphology
SpeedFastFastestSlowest
ErrorsBoundary errorsOver-/under-stemmingWrong POS → wrong lemma
2026 useAlwaysLexical search fields, quick baselinesMorphologically rich languages, rule-based extraction
Over-stemming vs under-stemming

Over-stemming collapses words that should stay apart: universal, university and universe all become univers. Under-stemming fails to collapse words that should merge (datum / data). Aggressive stemmers (Lancaster) do more of the first; conservative ones (Snowball) do more of the second.

1.8 Stopwords

Removing the most frequent words reduces noise and dimensionality — and in four common situations destroys the thing you were trying to measure.

Why remove them: Zipf's law means a few dozen types carry a huge share of tokens, contributing dimensions with almost no discriminative power. When not to:

SituationWhy removal breaks it
Sentiment / negation“not good” and “good” become the same document
Phrase or exact-match search“to be or not to be” is entirely stopwords
POS tagging / parsingFunction words are the syntactic skeleton
Any transformer inputRemoving them deviates from the pretraining distribution
The better answer than a stopword list

Use max_df in your vectorizer. It removes terms that appear in more than x% of documents — which is a data-driven, domain-adapted, language-agnostic stopword list computed from your corpus rather than imported from NLTK. In a corpus of insurance claims, “claim” is a stopword and no downloaded list will tell you that.

1.9 POS tagging

Labelling each token with its grammatical category. Asked partly for itself, and partly as a vehicle for the HMM/CRF question in Part 2.

Tagsets: Penn Treebank (fine-grained, English-specific — NN, VBZ, JJR) vs Universal Dependencies (17 coarse cross-lingual tags — NOUN, VERB, ADJ). Open classes (nouns, verbs) grow; closed classes (determiners, prepositions) don't.

What POS tagging is still for: input to lemmatization, candidate generation for aspect terms in ABSA, linguistic filtering for keyphrase extraction, features for rule-based high-precision extraction, and negation/scope detection in clinical text.

1.10 Syntactic parsing

Recovering sentence structure. Two formalisms, two metrics, and a handful of uses that genuinely survive into 2026.

Uses that survive: relation-extraction features, ABSA opinion-target linking, high-precision rule-based extraction, negation and scope detection in clinical text, and schema-linking heuristics for text-to-SQL. Non-projective arcs (crossing dependencies) matter for freer-word-order languages.

1.11 NER and BIO tagging

The classical framing of extraction — and the tagging scheme that turns “find the entities” into a per-token classification problem you can actually train.

Classical recipe: gazetteers and rules for precision, a learned model for recall. That split is still the right production architecture — a fine-tuned model with a curated allow-list on top beats either alone.

1.14 TF-IDF ⭐

Know the formula, not the vibe. “It weights important words higher” is not an answer — the interviewer wants the two components and the reason each exists.

Always run this first

This nine-line baseline takes minutes, trains on a laptop, serves in microseconds, and is fully interpretable. If your fine-tuned transformer can't beat it by a margin that justifies the GPU, that is a finding worth reporting — and saying so out loud in an interview reads as judgment, not laziness.

Also know: CountVectorizer (raw counts) vs TfidfVectorizer vs HashingVectorizer. The hashing trick maps terms into a fixed number of buckets via a hash function — no vocabulary to fit or persist, streaming-friendly, constant memory — at the cost of collisions and no inverse transform (so no interpretability).

1.15 Similarity and distance for text

Four measures, four different questions. Choosing the wrong one is a silent bug.

MeasureAnswersUse forWatch out
CosineSame direction?The default for text — scale-invariant, so document length doesn't dominateOn sparse vectors, zero overlap ⇒ 0 even for paraphrases
Dot productSame direction and magnitude?Embeddings trained with dot-product objectives; magnitude can encode “importance”Long documents win by accident if not normalised
JaccardHow much do the token sets overlap?Near-duplicate detection; with MinHash + LSH it scales to billionsIgnores frequency entirely
Edit distanceHow many character edits apart?Fuzzy name/address matching, OCR repair (Levenshtein, Damerau, Jaro-Winkler)O(nm); meaningless for sentence semantics
The vocabulary-mismatch problem

“How do I cancel my subscription?” and “I want to end my membership” share almost no terms, so their TF-IDF cosine similarity is near zero despite being the same request. No amount of tuning fixes this — it is a property of sparse lexical representation, and it is the single strongest motivation for dense embeddings.

1.16 LSA, NMF and LDA

Three ways to find latent structure in a document-term matrix. LDA is asked most often — not because you'll use it, but because it tests whether you understand generative models.

1.17 The distributional hypothesis

One sentence that every embedding method in the rest of this subject is an implementation of.

Firth, 1957

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” If two words appear in the same contexts, they mean similar things. Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText, BERT and every embedding model in production are engineering answers to that single claim.

1.18 Interview focus for Part 1

The questions this part is designed to make automatic. Answer out loud before opening the answer.

Foundations · 9 questions
Tokenization vs stemming vs lemmatization — and when would you use each in 2026?

Tokenization splits text into units (one text → many tokens). Stemming chops affixes by rule; fast, no lexicon, output may not be a word (happiness → happi). Lemmatization maps to a dictionary lemma using POS and morphology; slower, always a real word (better → good).

The part that earns marks: tokenization always; stemming/lemmatization only for lexical features (TF-IDF, BM25 fields, keyword rules) and morphologically rich languages. Never before a transformer — subword tokenizers already share morphology, and normalising away casing and function words moves your input off the pretraining distribution while destroying negation.

Explain Bag of Words and its three main limitations.

A document becomes a vector of counts over the vocabulary — a multiset, order discarded.

(1) No word order — “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are identical vectors. (2) No semantics — “excellent” and “superb” are as unrelated as “excellent” and “tractor”. (3) High-dimensional sparsity — one dimension per vocabulary term, almost all zero, and the vocabulary drifts at serve time. Add a fourth for credit: every term is weighted by raw count, so a word in every document counts as much as a discriminative one.

Write down TF-IDF. Why does IDF help, and where does TF-IDF break?

tfidf(t,d) = tf(t,d) × idf(t), with idf(t) = log(N/df(t)) — in scikit-learn the smoothed log((1+N)/(1+df)) + 1 — followed by L2 normalization of each document vector so that cosine equals dot product.

Why IDF helps: it down-weights terms that appear everywhere (no discriminative power) and up-weights terms concentrated in few documents. Where it breaks: no semantics (vocabulary mismatch means paraphrases score ~0), still sparse and high-dimensional, unstable IDF on tiny corpora, and rare typos or IDs can receive enormous weight. Also: tf grows without bound, which is what BM25's k₁ saturation fixes.

TF-IDF vs BM25 — what exactly does BM25 add?

Two things. Term-frequency saturation via k₁: the 20th occurrence of a term adds far less than the 2nd, whereas TF-IDF's tf term is linear and unbounded. Document-length normalization via b: without it, long documents accumulate matches and win by size alone.

BM25 is also derived from a probabilistic relevance model rather than assembled heuristically. It remains the lexical half of every serious hybrid retrieval system in 2026 — the dense half never fully replaced it, because exact term matching on names, codes and rare identifiers is something embeddings are bad at.

Why is a split('.') sentence splitter wrong, and why is POS tagging still useful?

Sentence splitting: abbreviations (Dr., e.g., Inc.), decimals (3.14), ellipses, quoted speech and URLs all contain periods that don't end sentences — and some languages don't use periods at all. It is a learned component in spaCy for a reason.

POS tagging in 2026: input to lemmatization; candidate generation for ABSA aspect terms; linguistic filters for keyphrase extraction; features for high-precision rule-based extraction; negation and scope detection in clinical text. Quote the baseline too — ~97% accuracy sounds impressive until you note that assigning each word its most frequent tag gets ~90%.

Constituency vs dependency parsing — and one production use for either.

Constituency parsing produces a phrase-structure tree (S → NP VP), typically via CKY over a PCFG; evaluated with labelled bracket F1. Dependency parsing produces labelled head→dependent arcs between words, via transition-based (arc-eager) or graph-based (biaffine) methods; evaluated with UAS (arcs correct) and LAS (arcs and labels correct).

Production use: dependency arcs for relation extraction and for linking an opinion word to its target in aspect-based sentiment; constituency phrases for noun-phrase candidate extraction. Both also power negation-scope rules in clinical NLP, where a false negative is a safety issue and you want deterministic behaviour.

What are BIO tags, why does NER use them, and what can't they express?

Begin / Inside / Outside per token turns span-finding into a token-classification problem you can train with a standard softmax. The B/I distinction is what distinguishes two adjacent single-word entities from one two-word entity. BIOES adds End and Single for sharper boundaries at the cost of more classes.

What it can't express: nested entities (Bank of [America]), overlapping types on the same token, and discontinuous spans. Those need span-based enumeration or generative extraction. And critically: evaluate with entity-level F1 (exact boundary + type, via seqeval) — token accuracy is inflated by the dominant O class.

LSA vs LDA vs NMF, how do you choose k, and why coherence over perplexity?

LSA is truncated SVD of the term-document matrix — fast, deterministic, handles synonymy, but components can be negative and aren't interpretable as topics. NMF factorizes into non-negative parts, often giving the most readable topics, with no probabilistic story. LDA is generative: documents are Dirichlet mixtures over topics (α), topics are Dirichlet distributions over words (β), inferred by collapsed Gibbs sampling or variational EM.

Choosing k: sweep and evaluate with topic coherence (C_v or UMass), not held-out perplexity — the well-known result is that perplexity can anti-correlate with human interpretability. And say the modern part: for short text, embeddings + HDBSCAN + c-TF-IDF (BERTopic-style) is the better default; LDA still earns its place on long, clean documents where you want interpretable priors.

State the distributional hypothesis, and connect the counting and prediction routes.

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth) — words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Every embedding method implements this.

The counting route: build a co-occurrence matrix, reweight with PPMI (raw counts over-reward frequent words), factorize with SVD to ~300 dimensions. The prediction route: Word2Vec's skip-gram trains vectors to predict context. The connection worth stating — Levy & Goldberg showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix, so these are two views of one computation rather than competing philosophies.
