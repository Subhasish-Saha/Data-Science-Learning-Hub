Part 4 of 7 · Transformers & Pretraining

Attention, and everything built on it The transformer block component by component — including why √d_k exists and the full positional-encoding lineage up to RoPE and YaRN — then pretraining objectives, the model zoo, scaling laws and sentence embeddings.

This is the densest part of the subject and the highest-yield. Two ideas carry it. First, attention as content-based routing: `softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V`, why the scale factor is not cosmetic, why multiple heads mean subspaces rather than capacity, and the KV cache — the single most useful concept for answering any serving question, because it explains why decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound. Second, the objective determines the family: masked LM gives you a strong per-token representation, causal LM gives you a samplable generator, denoising gives you transduction. Getting that mapping right is how you answer the question this subject exists for — should this be a fine-tuned encoder or a prompted LLM?

A live debate to mention, not resolve

ModernBERT is the natural answer to “which encoder in 2026?” — but a published comparison found DeBERTaV3 remains competitive, suggesting a good deal of ModernBERT's advantage comes from data scale and efficiency rather than architecture alone. The correct interview answer is therefore: “ModernBERT for its 8k context and throughput; benchmark it against DeBERTaV3 on my task before committing.” Naming the uncertainty is stronger than asserting a winner.

Also know: the curse of multilinguality — as you add languages to a fixed-capacity model, per-language quality eventually degrades because capacity is diluted; and domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT/TAPT), the general recipe behind BioBERT, PubMedBERT, SciBERT, FinBERT, LegalBERT and CodeBERT.

4.12 Sentence embeddings ⭐

Where NLP hands off to retrieval. This section owns the modelling half; how the vectors get indexed and searched is the RAG subject.

On MTEB and leaderboards

MTEB / MMTEB is the standard benchmark family and the right starting shortlist — but treat any specific ranking as stale on sight. Leaderboards are overfit, the task mix is not your domain, and they ignore licence, model size and latency. The 2026 landscape includes commercial (Cohere embed-v4, Voyage voyage-3-large) and open-weight (BGE-M3 + bge-reranker-v2, Qwen3-Embedding, jina-embeddings-v3) options. Always re-rank the shortlist on your own labelled set — saying that is worth more than naming the current leader.

4.13 Interview focus for Part 4

Ten questions. If you can answer these cold, you can handle most of a transformer round.

Transformers & pretraining · 10 questions
⭐ Write scaled dot-product attention. Why √d_k?

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) V, with Q = XW_Q, K = XW_K, V = XW_V. Query = “what am I looking for”, key = “what do I offer”, value = “what I contribute if selected”.

Why √d_k: if q and k components have unit variance, their dot product sums d_k such terms, so its variance is d_k. With d_k=64 the logits swing wide enough that softmax saturates into a near one-hot distribution — and the gradient of a saturated softmax is close to zero, so learning stalls. Dividing by √d_k restores unit variance and keeps softmax in its responsive regime. Mentioning the gradient is what makes this a complete answer.

Why multi-head rather than one big head?

Not for capacity — d_model is split, so h heads of d_model/h dimensions cost roughly the same as one d_model head. The gain is representation subspaces: a single head must average every kind of relation into one attention distribution, whereas separate heads can specialise — previous-token/positional heads, syntactic dependency heads, coreference heads, delimiter heads.

Nuance worth volunteering: many heads turn out to be redundant and can be pruned with little quality loss, which is evidence that the benefit is diversity of relations rather than raw parameter count.

⭐ Why is positional encoding necessary, and why did the field move to RoPE?

Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: permute the input and the output permutes identically. Without position information, “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are the same input.

The lineage: sinusoidal absolute (parameter-free, relative offsets are linear functions of it, extrapolates somewhat) → learned absolute (BERT/GPT-2; cannot extrapolate at all past the trained maximum) → relative (Shaw, T5 buckets, Transformer-XL; generalises across lengths because distance is what matters) → RoPE, which rotates Q and K by a position-dependent angle so that qᵢᵀkⱼ depends only on i−j.

Why RoPE won: relative by construction, zero added parameters, compatible with KV caching, and — decisively — extensible after training by rescaling its frequency spectrum. That's what rope_scaling does: Position Interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN (preserves short-range behaviour while stretching long-range, roughly 10× fewer tokens and 2.5× fewer steps than PI) and LongRoPE (non-uniform, to ~2M). ALiBi is the alternative: a linear distance penalty on the logits with no positional embeddings, which extrapolates well.

Why LayerNorm and not BatchNorm? Pre-LN vs post-LN?

LayerNorm normalizes across the feature dimension within each token. BatchNorm normalizes across the batch, which breaks for text: sequences are variable-length so batch statistics are computed over inconsistent padding, batches are small, and BN's inference behaviour depends on running statistics collected from training batches.

Pre-LN vs post-LN: the original transformer put the norm after the residual addition (post-LN), which places a normalization on the residual path — gradients get rescaled at every block, so deep models are unstable and need learning-rate warmup. Modern models use pre-LN (norm inside the residual branch), leaving a clean identity path from input to output, usually plus one final norm. Many also swap to RMSNorm — no mean subtraction, no bias, cheaper, works just as well.

How does causal masking work, and why does it make training efficient?

Before the softmax, set the logits for all future positions to −∞ (a lower-triangular mask), so position t can only attend to positions ≤ t. That guarantees no label leakage from the token being predicted.

Why it's efficient: because the mask enforces causality structurally, you can compute the loss for every next-token prediction in the document in one parallel forward pass — an RNN needs one sequential step per token. That single property is what made pretraining on trillions of tokens economically viable. Note it applies only at training time: generation is inherently sequential.

⭐ Explain the KV cache, and why decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound.

During generation, the keys and values for previous tokens don't change, so you cache them instead of recomputing. Size: 2 · layers · heads_kv · d_head · seq_len · batch · bytes — linear in both sequence length and batch size.

The two regimes: prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel — large matmuls, compute-bound, and it sets TTFT. Decode produces one token per forward pass, so you re-read all model weights and the entire KV cache to compute a single token — the arithmetic intensity is terrible, making it memory-bandwidth-bound and setting inter-token latency. This framing explains why GQA/MLA, speculative decoding, continuous batching and paged attention all exist: they attack the decode-side memory wall, not FLOPs.

⭐ Why isn't “just increase the context length” the answer to long documents?

Four reasons, in order of how often they bite. (1) Attention compute is quadratic in length. (2) The KV cache grows linearly and competes with weights for VRAM, which caps your batch size and therefore your throughput and cost. (3) Positions beyond the trained range need RoPE rescaling (YaRN/NTK) or quality degrades — a longer window isn't free even architecturally. (4) Effective context is smaller than advertised context: lost-in-the-middle and long-context degradation are measurable, so material buried mid-context is under-used.

The framing: a context window is a budget you allocate, not a bucket you fill. Chunk and retrieve, or use a model actually trained long — and measure effective context with needle-in-a-haystack style probes rather than trusting the spec sheet.

Explain MLM including the 80/10/10 recipe, and what RoBERTa changed.

MLM: mask roughly 15% of tokens and predict the originals using context from both directions. Of the selected tokens, about 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% with a random token, and 10% left unchanged. The random and unchanged cases exist because [MASK] never appears at fine-tuning or inference time — the pretrain/finetune mismatch — so the model must not learn to rely on seeing it.

RoBERTa kept the architecture and fixed the recipe: much more data, much longer training, larger batches, byte-level BPE, dynamic masking (a new mask each epoch rather than one fixed mask), and it dropped NSP after finding it too easy and unhelpful. The lesson to state: training recipe mattered more than architecture — which is the same lesson ModernBERT reinforces.

⭐ ModernBERT or a prompted LLM for a 10M-document/day classifier?

Fine-tuned encoder, and I'd show the arithmetic. At 10M documents/day an LLM API call at even a fraction of a cent per document is a multi-million-dollar annual line item with a latency profile you can't control. A 150M-parameter encoder serves in single-digit milliseconds, runs on CPU if quantized, costs a fixed amount, keeps data in-house, and — with adequate labels — typically matches or beats a prompted general model on a narrow, well-specified task.

How I'd get there: LLM zero-shot to generate labels → human-verify a stratified sample → fine-tune ModernBERT-base (8k context, RoPE, FlashAttention, trained on text and code) → evaluate on a human-labelled test set → distil or quantize for serving. Keep the LLM for the long tail and for periodic re-labelling. And flag the honest caveat: benchmark DeBERTaV3 too — a published comparison found it still competitive, so the winner is task-dependent.

Why is raw BERT's [CLS] a poor sentence embedding, and what's the fix?

[CLS] was trained for Next Sentence Prediction, not similarity. Nothing in MLM pretraining makes cosine distance between two sentence vectors meaningful, and the contextual space is anisotropic — vectors occupy a narrow cone, so everything looks moderately similar to everything.

The fix is SBERT: siamese/triplet fine-tuning with mean pooling and a contrastive objective, so cosine becomes a trained quantity. The payoff is computational — it converts an O(n²) cross-encoder comparison problem into encode-once plus ANN search. Then know the deployment split: bi-encoder to retrieve, cross-encoder to rerank (a cross-encoder needs one forward pass per pair), with ColBERT-style late interaction as the middle ground. Practical details that cost real accuracy if missed: instruction prefixes (query:/passage:), normalising before cosine, and hard negative mining, which is the single biggest quality lever.
