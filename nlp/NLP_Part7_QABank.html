7.1 Foundations & preprocessing

Eight questions. Answer out loud before opening the answer — reading answers you already half-know is the least efficient possible use of this page.

Q1–8 · foundations
1. Tokenization vs stemming vs lemmatization?

Tokenization splits text into units. Stemming chops affixes by rule — fast, no lexicon, output may not be a word (happiness → happi). Lemmatization maps to a dictionary lemma using POS and morphology — slower, always a real word (better → good). Modern half: tokenization always; the other two only for lexical features and morphologically rich languages, never before a transformer.

2. When would you not remove stopwords or lemmatize?

Sentiment and negation (“not good” collapses to “good”), phrase/exact-match search, POS tagging and parsing (function words are the syntax), and any transformer input — it deviates from the pretraining distribution. Better than a downloaded stopword list: max_df, which computes a domain-adapted list from your own corpus.

3. Why is a split('.') sentence splitter wrong?

Abbreviations (Dr., e.g., Inc.), decimals, ellipses, quoted speech and URLs all contain periods that don't end sentences — and some languages don't use them at all. It's a learned pipeline component in spaCy for exactly this reason.

4. What is Zipf's law and what does it imply?

Frequency ∝ 1/rank. Two consequences: a tiny head of function words carries ~half your tokens (so stopword lists are short and removal is cheap), and a vast tail means a fixed word-level vocabulary will miss things — the pressure that produced subword tokenization and smoothing.

5. Preprocessing for TF-IDF vs for BERT — why different?

For sparse lexical features you want to collapse surface variation: lowercase, strip punctuation, remove stopwords, lemmatize, placeholder numbers. Before a transformer you want minimal cleaning — fix encoding, strip boilerplate, normalise Unicode — because aggressive normalization creates a train/serve mismatch with the pretraining distribution and destroys casing (a real NER feature) and negation.

6. What is POS tagging for in a world where BERT exists?

Input to lemmatization; ABSA aspect-term candidates; linguistic filters for keyphrase extraction; features for high-precision rule extraction; negation and scope detection in clinical text. And quote the baseline: ~97% accuracy is only ~7 points above assigning each word its most frequent tag.

7. Constituency vs dependency parsing + one production use.

Constituency gives a phrase-structure tree (CKY over a PCFG; labelled bracket F1). Dependency gives labelled head→dependent arcs (transition-based arc-eager or graph-based biaffine; UAS/LAS). Production: dependency arcs for relation extraction and for linking an opinion word to its aspect target; both for negation-scope rules in clinical NLP where determinism matters.

8. What are BIO tags and what can't they express?

Begin/Inside/Outside per token, turning span-finding into token classification; the B/I distinction separates two adjacent entities from one two-word entity. BIOES adds End/Single. Cannot express nested entities, overlapping types on one token, or discontinuous spans — which is why span-based and generative extraction exist.

7.2 Representation & embeddings

Twenty-one questions covering sparse representation, topic models and the embedding family.

Q9–29 · representation
9. Why can't you one-hot encode words and be done?

One-hot vectors are mutually orthogonal, so cosine similarity between any two distinct words is exactly 0 — no similarity is encoded at all. Plus |V| ≈ 10⁵–10⁶ dimensions, no word order, and no representation for unseen words.

10. Explain BoW and its three limitations.

A document becomes a vector of counts over the vocabulary — a multiset. (1) No word order (“dog bites man” = “man bites dog”). (2) No semantics (“excellent” and “superb” are unrelated). (3) High-dimensional sparsity, plus vocabulary drift at serve time. Fourth for credit: raw counts weight ubiquitous and discriminative terms equally.

11. ⭐ Write down TF-IDF. Why does IDF help? Where does it break?

tf(t,d) × idf(t), idf = log(N/df) — sklearn's smoothed log((1+N)/(1+df)) + 1 — then L2-normalize per document so cosine equals dot product. IDF down-weights terms appearing everywhere and up-weights concentrated ones. Breaks on: no semantics (vocabulary mismatch → paraphrases score ~0), sparsity, unstable IDF on small corpora, huge weight for rare typos, and unbounded linear tf.

12. TF-IDF vs BM25 — what does BM25 add?

Term-frequency saturation via k₁ (the 20th occurrence adds far less than the 2nd) and document-length normalization via b (so long documents don't win by size). Plus a probabilistic-relevance derivation rather than assembled heuristics. It remains the lexical half of every serious hybrid retrieval system.

13. What is the hashing trick and what do you give up?

Hash terms into a fixed number of buckets: no vocabulary to fit or persist, constant memory, streaming-friendly. You give up collision-freedom and the inverse transform — so no interpretability and no coefficient inspection.

14. LSA vs LDA vs NMF?

LSA: truncated SVD — fast, deterministic, handles synonymy, components can be negative and aren't interpretable as topics. NMF: non-negative parts-based factorization, often the most readable topics, no probabilistic story. LDA: generative — documents are Dirichlet mixtures over topics (α), topics Dirichlet over words (β), inferred by collapsed Gibbs or variational EM.

15. How do you choose the number of topics, and why coherence over perplexity?

Sweep k and evaluate with topic coherence (C_v or UMass), not held-out perplexity — the well-known finding is that perplexity can anti-correlate with human interpretability. Also inspect stability across seeds; topic labelling is manual either way.

16. ⭐ Your LDA topics are garbage on tweets. What do you do?

LDA needs enough tokens per document to estimate a mixture, and tweets don't have them. Options: aggregate (pool by author, hashtag or time window), switch to a short-text model, or — the 2026 default — embeddings + UMAP + HDBSCAN + c-TF-IDF (BERTopic-style), which needs far less preprocessing, scores better on coherence, and can label a document as noise instead of forcing it into a topic.

17. State the distributional hypothesis in one sentence.

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth) — words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Every embedding method is an implementation of that sentence.

18. What is a word embedding and why was it a step change?

Dense, low-dimensional learned vectors where geometry encodes distributional similarity. The step change wasn't accuracy — it was that similarity became computable, and that structure could generalise between related words instead of every word being an isolated column.

19. ⭐ CBOW vs skip-gram — which for rare words?

Skip-gram, because it makes one prediction per (centre, context) pair, so a rare word receives several focused updates. CBOW averages several contexts into one prediction, which blurs the rare-word signal — but it's faster and better for frequent words on large corpora.

20. Explain negative sampling. How many negatives?

Replace the |V|-way softmax with k+1 binary logistic decisions: one true pair labelled 1, k sampled noise pairs labelled 0. k = 5–20 for small corpora, 2–5 for large. Noise distribution is unigram^(3/4), which flattens Zipf so rare words appear as negatives more than raw frequency implies. Frequent words are subsampled with P(discard)=1−√(t/f(w)). Alternative: hierarchical softmax, O(log|V|).

21. Word2Vec vs GloVe vs FastText for a typo-heavy multilingual corpus?

FastText — a word is a bag of character n-grams (3–6) plus the whole word, so typos and unseen morphology still compose a sensible vector, and it handles morphologically rich languages. Word2Vec predicts from local windows; GloVe factorizes a global co-occurrence matrix with a weighted least-squares objective; neither can embed an unseen word.

22. ⭐ How do you handle OOV words?

Two layers. Static embeddings: FastText composes from character n-grams. Anything modern: the question dissolves — subword and especially byte-level tokenizers can encode any string, so there are no OOVs, and <UNK> is an artefact of fixed word-level vocabularies. Giving only the first half sounds five years out of date; only the second half misses the question.

23. Why are “good” and “bad” close together?

Embeddings encode distributional similarity, and antonyms occur in nearly identical contexts (“the food was ___”). Consequences: you can't do sentiment by cosine to “good”, and a purely dense retriever can return the semantic opposite of a query.

24. Static vs contextual — what does contextual buy you?

Static gives one vector per word type, so “bank” is a single blended vector and polysemy is unrepresentable. Contextual gives one vector per occurrence — which makes word-sense disambiguation nearly free and NER/coreference tractable. Caveat: contextual spaces are anisotropic, so raw cosine between untuned BERT vectors is unreliable.

25. How do you turn word vectors into a sentence vector?

Mean pooling, TF-IDF-weighted mean, or SIF (smooth inverse frequency + remove the first principal component). All are surprisingly strong baselines and all lose to a trained sentence encoder, for one reason: they optimise nothing at the sentence level.

26. Why is raw BERT [CLS] a bad sentence embedding?

It was trained for NSP, not similarity — nothing in MLM makes cosine between two sentence vectors meaningful — and the space is anisotropic, so everything looks moderately similar. Fix: SBERT-style siamese/triplet contrastive fine-tuning with mean pooling.

27. ⭐ Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder?

Bi-encoder: encode query and document separately, compare with cosine — documents can be encoded offline, so ANN search runs in milliseconds over millions. Cross-encoder: concatenate the pair through one model for full token-level interaction — best accuracy but one forward pass per pair, so it reranks the top 50 and never searches. ColBERT-style late interaction is the middle ground: per-token vectors scored with MaxSim, most of the accuracy, a much larger index.

28. How do you evaluate an embedding model for your use case, given MTEB exists?

Use MTEB/MMTEB to build a shortlist, then re-rank on your own labelled set — leaderboards are overfit, the task mix isn't your domain, and they ignore licence, size and latency. Score candidates on Recall@k and nDCG on your queries, and on cost per million tokens, dimension, max sequence length and multilingual coverage. Treat any specific ranking as stale on sight.

29. What are Matryoshka embeddings for?

Trained so that truncated prefixes remain useful — you can cut a 1024-dim vector to 256 dims and keep most of the quality, trading accuracy for index size and query cost at serve time without re-embedding. Also know instruction prefixes (query:/passage:), normalising before cosine, and hard-negative mining as the biggest quality lever.

7.3 Sequence models

Q30–38 · RNN → attention
30. How does an RNN work, and what are its three real limitations?

h_t = tanh(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b) with weights shared across timesteps; trained by BPTT. Limitations: (1) vanishing/exploding gradients from the repeated W_h factor; (2) no parallelism over time — the property transformers exploit; (3) degraded long-range memory. Fourth in seq2seq: the fixed-size context bottleneck.

31. Vanishing vs exploding gradients — different causes?

Same cause (repeated multiplication by W_h), opposite regimes. Exploding is numerical and loud (NaN) → fix with gradient clipping by global norm. Vanishing is architectural and silent — training appears to work while only short-range structure is learned → fix with gating, orthogonal init, layer norm, skip connections.

32. ⭐ Walk through the LSTM gates and the vanishing-gradient fix.

Forget f_t, input i_t, candidate C̃_t, then C_t = f_t⊙C_{t-1} + i_t⊙C̃_t; output o_t and h_t = o_t⊙tanh(C_t). σ for gates (0–1 valve), tanh for content. The mechanism: the cell state is updated additively and gated elementwise, so the backward path along C is multiplied only by f_t — with the forget gate near 1, gradient magnitude survives hundreds of steps. That's the constant error carousel.

33. LSTM vs GRU — how do you choose?

GRU merges cell and hidden state and uses reset + update gates — ~25% fewer parameters, faster, often better with limited data. LSTM can edge ahead on very long sequences. Accuracy is comparable on most tasks, so choose by validation. “GRU is just a worse LSTM” is a trap answer.

34. When can you use a BiLSTM and when can't you?

Fine when the whole sequence is available before you predict: POS, NER, classification, extractive QA. Impossible for autoregressive generation (reading future tokens is label leakage) and for true streaming/incremental decoding.

35. What is the seq2seq bottleneck and how did attention fix it?

The encoder compresses the entire input into one fixed-size vector, so quality decays with input length — visible as a falling BLEU-vs-length curve. Attention lets the decoder read all encoder states, weighted per output step: c_t = Σ α_{t,i} h_i. Dynamic, content-based, length-independent access — and it parallelizes.

36. Bahdanau vs Luong attention.

Bahdanau is additive: score = vᵀtanh(W₁h_i + W₂s_t) — a small MLP, more parameters, tolerates different dimensionalities. Luong is multiplicative: score = s_tᵀWh_i — a matmul, faster on GPUs, and the direct ancestor of QKᵀ.

37. Teacher forcing and exposure bias?

Teacher forcing feeds gold prefixes during training. At inference the decoder consumes its own predictions, so an early error compounds into a distribution it never trained on — exposure bias. Scheduled sampling is a partial fix.

38. Would you ship an RNN in 2026?

Rarely for text. Legitimate cases: tiny models on embedded hardware, and strict streaming needing O(1) state rather than a growing KV cache. Then show range: the concepts came back — a fixed-size recurrent state with constant per-token inference cost is exactly what Mamba and the SSM family revived, and production models now interleave recurrence with attention.

7.4 Tokenization

Nine questions. The highest yield in this bank relative to how little most candidates prepare.

Q39–47 · subwords
39. ⭐ Explain BPE training, then encoding.

Training: split the corpus into characters; count adjacent pairs; merge the most frequent pair; append it to an ordered merge list; repeat to the target vocabulary size. Encoding: split into characters and replay the merges in learned order, greedily — deterministic. The merge order is the model.

40. BPE vs WordPiece vs Unigram — direction, criterion, users.

BPE and WordPiece merge upward; Unigram prunes downward from a large candidate set via EM + loss-based pruning. BPE merges by raw frequency; WordPiece by likelihood, roughly freq(AB)/(freq(A)·freq(B)), preferring informative over merely common merges; Unigram maximises corpus likelihood under a unigram LM and keeps a probability per piece, so it can sample segmentations. Users: GPT/Llama/RoBERTa (BPE), BERT/ELECTRA (WordPiece, ## marker), T5/ALBERT/XLNet and most multilingual models (Unigram via SentencePiece).

41. ⭐ What does SentencePiece actually do differently?

It's a framework, not an algorithm — it implements BPE and Unigram. Its contribution: consume raw text as a byte/character stream with whitespace encoded as ▁, so it needs no pre-tokenizer, is exactly reversible, and works on Chinese, Japanese and Thai. “SentencePiece vs BPE” is a category error.

42. Why byte-level BPE? What does it cost?

Operating on the 256 raw bytes means any Unicode string is representable — zero UNK, ever. The cost: non-Latin characters become several tokens each, so those languages pay a token premium in cost, latency and effective context.

43. How do you pick a vocabulary size?

Measure, don't guess: fertility (tokens per word) and bytes-per-token on your corpus, plus per-language token premium. Small vocab → longer sequences → more quadratic attention cost and worse compression. Large vocab → huge embedding/output matrices that can dominate a small model's parameters, and undertrained rare tokens. Typical 30k–50k (BERT era) → 32k–130k+ (multilingual/code).

44. ⭐ Why can't a model count the r's in “strawberry”?

Because it never sees characters — “strawberry” is two or three opaque subword tokens. Same root cause for string reversal, rhyming and acrostics. Related: inconsistent digit grouping is a genuine cause of arithmetic errors, which is why some tokenizers split digits individually.

45. Why is your Hindi app 3× the token cost?

Token premium. Under a byte-level BPE trained mostly on English, non-Latin scripts consume several tokens per character. Same content, more money, more latency, and a smaller effective context — for those users only. Measure with characters-per-token and fertility. Frame it as a cost, latency and fairness issue.

46. Your NER labels are misaligned after tokenization. What went wrong?

Word-level labels were applied positionally to sub-tokens. Fix with word_ids()/offset_mapping: label the first sub-token of each word and mask continuations (and special tokens) with -100. Mirror-image bug at eval time: re-aggregate sub-token predictions into word-level spans before entity-level F1. Neither raises an error.

47. What happens if you pair a model with a different tokenizer?

Silent garbage — token IDs map to unrelated embeddings, so the model produces confident nonsense with no error raised. The tokenizer must match the checkpoint, and both should be pinned to a commit SHA.

7.5 Transformers

Q48–60 · attention & the block
48. Name every component of a transformer block.

Multi-head self-attention · positional information (RoPE in modern models) · position-wise FFN (d_ff = 4·d_model, GELU/SwiGLU) · residual connections · normalization (pre-LN, often RMSNorm) · in decoders, causal masking; in encoder-decoders, cross-attention. Rule of thumb: ~12·d² parameters per block, most of them in the FFN.

49. ⭐ Write scaled dot-product attention. Why √d_k?

softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V. The dot product of d_k unit-variance terms has variance d_k, so large logits push softmax into saturation — and the gradient of a saturated softmax is near zero, stalling learning. Dividing by √d_k restores unit variance. Mentioning the gradient completes the answer.

50. What do Q, K and V mean concretely?

Query: “what am I looking for?” Key: “what do I offer?” Value: “what I contribute if selected.” Attention is content-based routing — each token pulls in a weighted mixture of the others' values.

51. Why multi-head instead of one big head?

Not capacity — d_model is split, so the cost is roughly the same. The gain is representation subspaces: heads specialise (previous-token/positional, syntactic dependency, coreference, delimiter). Nuance: many heads are redundant and prunable, which is evidence the benefit is relational diversity rather than parameter count.

52. ⭐ Positional encoding: sinusoidal → learned → relative → RoPE → ALiBi.

Self-attention is permutation-equivariant, so without positions “dog bites man” = “man bites dog”. Sinusoidal: parameter-free, relative offsets are linear functions of it, extrapolates somewhat. Learned absolute: cannot extrapolate past the trained maximum. Relative: attend on offsets — generalises across lengths. RoPE: rotate Q/K by a position-dependent angle so qᵢᵀkⱼ depends only on i−j — relative by construction, zero parameters, KV-cache-friendly, and extensible by rescaling frequencies (Position Interpolation → NTK-aware → YaRN → LongRoPE, surfaced as rope_scaling). ALiBi: a linear distance penalty on logits, no positional embeddings, strong extrapolation.

53. How does causal masking work, and why parallel training?

Set future logits to −∞ (lower-triangular mask) before softmax, so position t sees only ≤ t. Because causality is structural, you can compute the loss for every next-token prediction in one parallel forward pass — the property that made trillion-token pretraining viable. It applies at training time only; generation is inherently sequential.

54. Encoder self-attention vs decoder self-attention vs cross-attention.

Encoder self-attention: bidirectional, every token reads every token — best representations, cannot generate. Decoder self-attention: causal, sees only the past. Cross-attention: queries from the decoder, keys/values from the encoder — the decoder's read-port into the source.

55. Why LayerNorm and not BatchNorm? Pre-LN vs post-LN?

LayerNorm normalizes per token over features; BatchNorm needs stable batch statistics, which variable-length padded text doesn't provide, and its inference behaviour depends on running statistics. Post-LN (original) puts the norm on the residual path, rescaling gradients at every block — unstable at depth, needs LR warmup. Pre-LN puts it inside the branch, leaving a clean identity path; modern models use it, often with RMSNorm (no mean subtraction, no bias).

56. What does the FFN do?

A per-position two-layer MLP with d_ff = 4·d_model, applied identically at every position — a per-token feature transform, not a mixing operation. It holds most of a block's parameters. Activation evolution: ReLU → GELU → SwiGLU/GeGLU (gated, with a ⅔ width adjustment to hold the parameter count).

57. ⭐ Complexity of attention, and where does inference memory go?

Attention: O(n²·d) compute, O(n²) scores; FFN: O(n·d²) — for short sequences the FFN dominates, for long ones attention does. At inference, memory goes to the KV cache: 2·layers·heads_kv·d_head·seq·batch·bytes, linear in both sequence and batch — which is what actually caps context and concurrency.

58. ⭐ Explain the KV cache. Why is decoding memory-bandwidth-bound?

Past keys/values don't change, so you cache rather than recompute them. Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel — big matmuls, compute-bound, sets TTFT. Decode produces one token per pass, re-reading all weights and the whole cache for a single token — terrible arithmetic intensity, hence memory-bandwidth-bound, setting inter-token latency. This framing explains GQA/MLA, speculative decoding, continuous batching and paged attention in one go.

59. ⭐ Why isn't “just increase the context length” the answer?

Quadratic attention compute; a linearly-growing KV cache competing with weights for VRAM (capping batch size, throughput and cost); positions beyond the trained range needing RoPE rescaling; and effective context < advertised context — lost-in-the-middle and long-context degradation are measurable. A context window is a budget you allocate, not a bucket you fill.

60. What did transformers fix about RNNs — and what did they give up?

Fixed: parallelism over positions during training and constant path length between any two tokens (RNN: O(n)) — the latter is why long-range modelling improved so sharply. Gave up: linear scaling. Attention is O(n²) in length, which is what every technique in Part 5 tries to soften without losing the parallelism.

7.6 Pretraining & model families

Q61–71 · objectives & the zoo
61. ⭐ GPT vs BERT.

GPT: decoder-only, autoregressive next-token objective, unidirectional, generative, expensive at volume. BERT: encoder-only, masked LM, bidirectional, discriminative, cheap and fast. The objective determines the family, and the family determines what you can do — and the deployment economics.

62. Explain MLM, the 80/10/10 recipe and the [MASK] mismatch.

Mask ~15% of tokens and predict them from both directions. Of those, ~80% become [MASK], 10% a random token, 10% unchanged — because [MASK] never appears at fine-tune or inference time, so the model must not rely on seeing it. That gap is the pretrain/finetune mismatch.

63. What did RoBERTa change, and why drop NSP?

Same architecture, fixed recipe: much more data, longer training, larger batches, byte-level BPE, dynamic masking. NSP was dropped after being found too easy and unhelpful. The lesson: recipe mattered more than architecture.

64. How is T5 different from BERT and GPT?

Encoder-decoder trained with span corruption using sentinel tokens, and every task cast as text-to-text. BART is the sibling with five noising functions (token masking, deletion, infilling, sentence permutation, document rotation). Enc-dec is the natural shape when input and output are distinct sequences.

65. What is ELECTRA's trick?

Replaced-token detection: a small generator proposes replacements and a discriminator classifies every token as real or fake. MLM only learns from the 15% it masked; ELECTRA gets signal at all positions, so it reaches comparable quality with substantially less compute.

66. DistilBERT — what's distilled and what do you lose?

6 layers distilled from BERT-base using soft logits, embedding-cosine and MLM losses: ~40% smaller, ~60% faster, ~97% of GLUE. You lose a little accuracy on the hardest tasks and some capacity for further fine-tuning — the canonical distillation figure to quote.

67. What makes DeBERTa's attention “disentangled”?

It represents each token with separate content and relative-position vectors and computes attention from both, rather than adding position into the content embedding — plus an enhanced mask decoder. DeBERTaV3 is still a top fine-tuning baseline in 2026.

68. ⭐ ModernBERT or a prompted LLM for a 10M-doc/day classifier?

Fine-tuned encoder, with the arithmetic. At that volume an API call per document is a multi-million-dollar line item with uncontrollable latency; a 150M encoder serves in single-digit ms, runs on CPU quantized, and typically matches or beats a prompted general model on a narrow task. Path: LLM zero-shot labels → human-verify a stratified sample → fine-tune ModernBERT-base (8k context, RoPE, FlashAttention, alternating local/global attention, GeGLU, unpadding; ~2T tokens of text and code) → human-labelled test set → distil/quantize. Keep the LLM for the long tail. Honest caveat: benchmark DeBERTaV3 too — a published comparison found it competitive.

69. Explain transfer learning in NLP and why pretrain-then-finetune won.

Pretrain on huge unlabelled corpora with a self-supervised objective, then adapt to a task with a small labelled set. It won because the pretrained representation supplies syntax and semantics for free, so downstream tasks need orders of magnitude less labelled data and reach better accuracy — and one base model amortises across many tasks.

70. Scaling laws, Chinchilla, and why over-train a smaller model?

Kaplan showed power-law loss scaling, read as “spend on parameters”. Chinchilla corrected it: for a fixed compute budget scale parameters and data together, ≈20 tokens per parameter — earlier models were undertrained, not too small. The third position: Chinchilla optimises training compute, but if you serve the model, inference dominates lifetime cost, so you deliberately over-train a smaller model (the Llama strategy). Arithmetic: FLOPs ≈ 6ND; AdamW mixed precision ≈ 16 bytes/param.

71. What is the curse of multilinguality?

As you add languages to a fixed-capacity model, per-language quality eventually degrades because capacity is diluted across languages and scripts. Mitigations: more capacity, better-balanced data, language-specific adapters — and always report per-language results.

7.7 Efficiency & architecture

Q72–80 · making it cheap
72. ⭐ MHA vs MQA vs GQA vs MLA — and the 2026 default?

All attack the KV cache. MHA: one K/V per query head, largest cache. MQA: all heads share one K/V — big cut, some quality loss and training instability. GQA: one K/V per group — MHA-like quality at near-MQA speed, the industry default. MLA: compress K/V into a low-rank latent and cache that. One-liner: MQA/GQA share heads; MLA compresses them. Nuance: DeepSeek-V2's ablations had GQA looking worse than MHA on quality while MLA held up and could even beat it.

73. What does FlashAttention change? Is it an approximation?

No — exact. It's IO-aware: tile Q/K/V into SRAM-sized blocks, compute partial softmaxes with a running max/sum, never materialise the n×n matrix in HBM, and recompute tiles in the backward pass. Same maths, O(n) memory, large wall-clock win — and it made most approximate-attention research unnecessary.

74. Longformer / BigBird / Performer — why did they lose?

They approximate attention (sparse window+global, window+global+random with guarantees, low-rank/kernelized). They lost because FlashAttention + GQA + RoPE scaling made exact attention fast and long enough that an approximation's accuracy cost stopped being worth it. Survivors: sliding-window attention and ModernBERT's alternating local/global layers.

75. ⭐ Mamba/SSMs vs transformers — and why hybrid?

Attention keeps all past tokens and can recall any one exactly, at O(n²) with an unbounded cache. An SSM uses a selective linear recurrence: O(n) in length, O(1) state per step — but the past is summarised, so precise recall of an arbitrary earlier token is weaker (the associative-recall gap). Hence hybrids: a few attention layers among many SSM layers — Jamba (Transformer–Mamba–MoE), Hymba (parallel attention + SSM heads, SOTA sub-2B). “Attention made context accessible; SSMs make context cheap.”

76. What is MoE, and what does it not save?

N expert FFNs plus a router activating top-k (usually 1–2) per token: large total parameters, small active parameters, cheap FLOPs. It does not save memory — every expert must be VRAM-resident. Plus load-balancing auxiliary loss, expert capacity and token dropping, and expert-parallel networking complexity.

77. How does YaRN extend context, and why is it cheaper than retraining?

RoPE encodes position as an angle with a frequency per dimension-pair. YaRN rescales that frequency spectrum non-uniformly — preserving short-range behaviour while stretching long-range positions — so an 8k-trained model runs at 128k. It's cheaper because you only need a short adaptation run: roughly 10× fewer tokens and 2.5× fewer steps than plain Position Interpolation, versus full long-context pretraining.

78. Quantization vs distillation vs pruning — one per constraint.

Memory-bound → quantization (GPTQ/AWQ/GGUF; INT8/INT4/FP8; ~4-bit floor). Latency-bound → distillation and structured pruning (heads/layers/FFN width — unstructured needs sparse kernels to pay off). Throughput-bound → batching and caching (continuous batching, paged attention, prefix cache, GQA for a bigger batch). Accuracy-bound → none of these; that's a data problem.

79. Explain speculative decoding. Does it change the output distribution?

A small draft model proposes k tokens; the target verifies all k in one parallel forward pass and accepts the longest valid prefix. Lossless with respect to the target's distribution when implemented correctly. Nearly free because decode was memory-bandwidth-bound, so the extra FLOPs were idle anyway. Relatives: Medusa heads, n-gram/prompt-lookup decoding.

80. Beam search vs top-p vs greedy — per task.

Greedy/T≈0 for extraction, classification and code. Beam search (B≈4–5, length-normalized) for closed-ended seq2seq like MT. Top-p 0.9 with T 0.7–1.0 for open-ended generation, because beam search there produces bland repetitive text. Mechanism: temperature scales logits (variance, not truthfulness); top-k is mis-sized when confidence varies; top-p adapts; min-p keeps a floor relative to the top token.

7.8 Fine-tuning & adaptation

Q81–89 · adaptation
81. ⭐ Walk the adaptation ladder. Where do you stop?

Zero-shot → few-shot/ICL → retrieval → feature extraction (freeze, train a head) → PEFT/LoRA → full FT → continued pretraining → from scratch. Stop at the first rung that hits your target metric, and say why. Hundreds of labels → feature extraction. 1k–100k on one GPU → LoRA/QLoRA. If the gap is knowledge rather than behaviour → retrieval, and climbing won't help.

82. Explain LoRA. What are r and alpha?

Freeze W, learn ΔW = BA of rank r, applied as W′ = W + (α/r)BA. r = 4–64 (capacity); alpha scales the update so effective LR ∝ α/r; target the q/k/v/o projections and often the MLP. Trainable parameters drop 100–10,000×. Two decisive properties: you can merge at inference (zero latency penalty) and hot-swap adapters over one base.

83. QLoRA — the four ingredients.

(1) frozen base quantized to 4-bit NF4; (2) LoRA adapters trained in bf16; (3) double quantization of the quantization constants; (4) paged optimizers to survive memory spikes. Result: a 65B-class fine-tune on a single large GPU.

84. When is LoRA not enough?

Teaching genuinely new knowledge, a new language or new domain vocabulary; large behavioural shifts; very large training sets where full FT's extra capacity pays. Then continued pretraining or full fine-tuning. Also know the family moved on: DoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA+, VeRA.

85. ⭐ Fine-tune or RAG for 40,000 support articles?

Retrieval for the knowledge, a light fine-tune for the voice and output contract. Fine-tuning teaches form, style and task behaviour; it does not reliably inject facts, and facts that change, need citations, or are access-controlled must live in a retrieval layer. “Fine-tune so it knows our docs” is expensive, immediately stale, uncitable and hallucination-prone.

86. What is catastrophic forgetting and how do you mitigate it?

Training on a new task overwrites previously learned capability. Mitigations: lower LR, fewer epochs, replay general data alongside task data, EWC-style regularisation, or adapter isolation — LoRA forgets less because the base weights never move. Evaluation consequence: your eval set must include the original capability, not just the new task.

87. Your BERT fine-tune swings 3 F1 points across seeds. Diagnose it.

Small-data fine-tuning instability — a known failure mode. Fixes: report mean ± std over ≥3 seeds, longer warmup, re-initialise the top layers, more epochs with early stopping on a dev set, lower LR, layer-wise LR decay. Also check for leakage and label noise. A single-seed number on 500 examples is not evidence.

88. SFT vs DPO vs GRPO vs PPO-RLHF.

SFT on (prompt, response) pairs → format and instruction-following. PPO-RLHF: reward model + KL-regularised policy optimisation, requiring four models resident (policy, frozen ref, reward, critic) — the cost is engineering. DPO: optimise directly on chosen/rejected pairs with an implicit reward — no reward model, no critic, two models; the offline reference point (relatives: KTO, SimPO, ORPO, IPO). GRPO: sample a group per prompt and compute advantages within the group, so the group mean replaces the critic — and with verifiable rewards (RLVR: unit tests, math checkers) this is where the reasoning gains came from.

89. You have 500 labels and need 10,000. Walk me through it.

LLM-as-teacher loop: prompt a strong model to label unlabelled data → human-verify a stratified sample → fine-tune a small encoder → evaluate on a human-labelled test set only. Supplement with back-translation and LLM paraphrase (EDA-style synonym swaps are weak), weak supervision via label functions + a label model, active learning on uncertain cases, and a Cleanlab-style noise audit — a 3% label error rate caps your achievable F1 regardless of model.

7.9 Tasks

Q90–97 · the task catalogue
90. Multi-label ticket classifier with heavy imbalance?

Sigmoid per label, not softmax, with per-label thresholds tuned on a dev set to a business precision/recall target. Class weights or focal loss for imbalance; report per-label F1 plus macro. Add abstention routing for low-confidence tickets, and calibrate before thresholding. Baseline first: TF-IDF + one-vs-rest logistic regression.

91. Classifying a 50-page document with a 512-token model?

Truncate (log the truncation rate), head+tail (256+256 — strong for reports where the conclusion is last), chunk-and-pool (whole document at k× compute), or a long-context encoder (ModernBERT, 8k). Also ask whether the label depends on one section — then retrieve-then-classify beats all four.

92. ⭐ NER for a new entity type with zero labels — this week?

GLiNER first: a compact bidirectional encoder matching entity-type descriptions against candidate spans — genuine zero-shot open-vocabulary NER on CPU, reported to beat ChatGPT and NER-tuned LLMs zero-shot (domain variants like GLiNER-BioMed exist). Then the teacher loop into a fine-tuned encoder, plus a rules/gazetteer precision layer. Metric: entity-level F1 via seqeval, per type, with errors split into boundary/type/spurious/missed.

93. Extractive vs abstractive summarization, and catching hallucinations?

Extractive selects sentences — faithful by construction, choppy. Abstractive generates — fluent and compressive, can invent. ROUGE cannot see a hallucination, so add an explicit faithfulness metric: NLI-based entailment (SummaC-style) or QA-based (QAGS/QuestEval), plus a rubric judge and human spot-checks. Long inputs: map-reduce, refine, hierarchical, retrieve-then-summarize.

94. How do you build ABSA? Where does it break?

Aspect-term extraction (span or token classification, POS/dependency features help) then aspect-sentiment pairing, often jointly. Breaks on negation and scope, sarcasm, comparatives (positive about the delta, not the product), and domain-specific polarity — “cheap” is good for hotels and bad for electronics, so no universal lexicon works.

95. Text-to-SQL: architecture and metric?

Schema linking is the hard part — map question phrases to tables/columns, with retrieval over the schema for large databases. Generate SQL with constrained decoding, then validate by parsing and dry-running. Metric: execution accuracy (does it return the right rows?), not string match. Guardrails: read-only credentials, a query allow-list, and human confirmation for anything destructive.

96. Zero-shot cross-lingual: train English, serve Hindi. What do you check?

Choose the strategy explicitly — zero-shot transfer (XLM-R), translate-train, or translate-test — they fail differently. Then check: per-language metrics (never only the macro average), tokenizer fertility and token premium for the target script, script/transliteration handling, code-switching, and a human-labelled target-language test set even if training data is English-only.

97. Semantic search vs keyword search — when is keyword still better?

For exact identifiers, product codes, part numbers, names, and rare terms the embedding never saw. Dense retrieval generalises but blurs exactness; BM25 nails literal matches. That's why hybrid lexical + dense is the production default rather than pure dense.

7.10 Evaluation

Q98–108 · measurement
98. ⭐ Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report?

Macro plus per-class, when the rare class is the point — micro is dominated by the majority class, so a model can show micro 0.94 and macro 0.60. The tell: in single-label multi-class, micro-F1 equals accuracy, so quoting both as independent evidence signals unfamiliarity. Also: PR-AUC over ROC-AUC for rare positives.

99. Why is token-level accuracy the wrong NER metric?

The O class dominates — on a corpus that's 90% O, predicting O everywhere scores 90%. Use entity-level P/R/F1 with exact boundary and type match (seqeval), per type, after re-aggregating sub-token predictions into spans.

100. Explain perplexity, and two reasons lower can be worse.

exp(−(1/N)Σ log P(w_i|context)) = 2^(cross-entropy in bits) = an effective branching factor. (1) Not comparable across tokenizers — a larger vocabulary yields more, individually easier tokens and lower perplexity for free. (2) It measures fit to a text distribution, not helpfulness, factuality, instruction-following or safety. Bonus: it doesn't apply in the usual sense to masked LMs.

101. ⭐ Why can't you compare perplexity across tokenizers?

Perplexity is per token, and tokenization defines what a token is. Split text into more, smaller, more-predictable units and per-token uncertainty falls without the model being better. Normalize to a tokenizer-independent unit — bits per byte (or per character) — to compare fairly.

102. BLEU vs ROUGE vs METEOR vs chrF — what does each measure?

BLEU: modified n-gram precision + brevity penalty, corpus-level, MT-oriented (use sacreBLEU with its signature). ROUGE: recall-oriented overlap — ROUGE-1/2 n-grams, ROUGE-L longest common subsequence; the summarization convention. METEOR: unigram matching with stems/synonyms plus a fragmentation penalty. chrF/chrF++: character n-gram F-score, robust to morphology.

103. Why is BLEU bad for summarization and terrible for dialogue?

It scores n-gram precision against a reference. Summarization legitimately paraphrases, so precision punishes good output; open-ended dialogue has no single correct reference at all, so the metric is measuring the wrong construct rather than measuring it imprecisely.

104. BERTScore vs COMET vs BLEURT.

BERTScore: greedy alignment of contextual token embeddings → P/R/F1; tolerant of paraphrase, needs a good backbone and rescaling to be interpretable. BLEURT: a regression model trained on human ratings. COMET: takes source + hypothesis (+ optional reference), trained on WMT human direct assessments over XLM-R — the reference-based state of the art for MT and far better human-correlated than any n-gram metric.

105. ⭐ Design an LLM-as-judge evaluation with bias controls.

Explicit rubric with named criteria, a fixed anchored scale, a required justification before the score, and pairwise comparison where possible. Biases and controls: position → randomise order and average both directions; verbosity → control for length or compare at matched lengths; self-preference → judge with a different model family. Also language-switching and formatting bias. Then calibrate against a human-labelled slice, report judge–human κ, and meta-evaluate the judge.

106. Your model is 0.4 F1 better. Is it better?

Unknown until tested. Run bootstrap resampling or a paired permutation test; on a 1,000-row test set a 0.4-point difference is comfortably inside the noise, and seed variance alone can exceed it. Also confirm it holds on the slices that matter, not just the aggregate.

107. Agreement is 0.55 κ and the model scores 0.72 F1. Meaning?

Something is wrong. Human agreement is a ceiling on measurable quality, so exceeding it usually means the model has learned one annotator's idiosyncrasy, or the test set is measuring noise, or there's leakage. Low κ means the task is underspecified — rewrite the guideline, pilot, adjudicate, re-measure. Where disagreement is legitimate (toxicity, humour), keep soft labels instead of forcing a majority vote.

108. How do you detect benchmark contamination?

N-gram overlap between the benchmark and your training corpus; canary strings planted in your data; and a performance gap between the public benchmark and a freshly-collected private set. Practical conclusion: a private held-out eval set is a competitive asset. Related: GLUE/SuperGLUE are largely saturated, and MTEB is heavily overfit.

7.11 Production

Q109–115 · shipping it
109. Design real-time intent classification at 5k QPS, p95 < 100 ms.

Cheap filter (rules/regex) → language ID and routing → a distilled, quantized encoder on CPU (5 ms) handling the confident majority → escalate calibrated-uncertain cases to a larger model → OOD/“other” detection so new intents surface rather than being mis-assigned. Length bucketing and dynamic batching; ONNX Runtime; exact-match then normalised-key cache. Metrics: macro-F1 plus coverage at target precision, p95/p99 latency, and the escalation rate as a drift signal. Every escalated case is training data.

110. ⭐ 0.89 F1 offline, users complaining. Debug it.

Assume the evaluation is wrong first. (1) Leakage — was dedup done before splitting, and was the split grouped by document/user/time? (2) Slices — per class, language, length bucket, segment; 0.89 aggregate can hide 0.3 on the class users care about. (3) Distribution shift between production inputs and the test set (length, language mix, OOV rate, truncation rate). (4) Is the metric the one the decision needs (macro vs micro, entity- vs token-level, default 0.5 threshold)? (5) Pipeline mismatch between training and serving preprocessing. (6) Label quality — audit 200 labels. (7) Collect the actual failing examples; “users are complaining” is often one salient error class.

111. How do you detect drift when labels arrive 30 days late?

Separate input drift (PSI/KL on token or embedding distributions, new-vocabulary rate) from concept drift (labels change meaning while the text distribution doesn't — invisible to input monitoring) from performance drift. Use proxies: confidence-distribution shift, abstention rate, OOV rate, prediction-mix shift, truncation rate, latency p95 — and above all user behaviour: edit rate, escalation rate, override rate, thumbs-down, time-to-resolution. Wrap with shadow deploys, canaries and a golden-set CI suite.

112. Cut inference cost 10× without losing 1 point of F1.

In order, measuring each step: (1) tiered cascade — rules, small encoder, large model, human, with escalation on calibrated uncertainty; if 90% of traffic never touches the expensive model, average cost falls ~10× with tail quality preserved. (2) Distil the large model into the small one. (3) Quantize to ~4-bit so it runs on CPU. (4) Batch — continuous batching, length bucketing, unpadding, ONNX/TensorRT. (5) Cache — exact, then normalised-key, then semantic with a similarity floor and TTL. Publish macro-F1 vs p95 vs $/1M docs at each step.

113. How do you keep PII out of prompts, training data and logs?

A detection pass combining NER (recall on names/addresses), rules and validation checksums (precision on structured IDs), applied at ingest before anything is persisted, and again on the logging path — trace redaction is a separate code path from training-data cleaning and both must exist. Pseudonymise with a reversible vault only where re-identification is required. Add retention limits, data residency, a training-data manifest (you cannot un-train on request), and canary tests for memorisation.

114. How do you make an NLP pipeline reproducible?

Pin revision= to a commit SHA (not main), pin the tokenizer and library versions (transformers v5 was a breaking major), seed everything, and record the exact preprocessing. Track experiments and dataset versions so you have lineage from prediction → model → data snapshot. Tests: tokenizer round-trip, label-alignment invariants, extracted-output schema validation, CheckList-style invariance, and a no-network path with a tiny model.

115. Build vs API for a text classifier — where's the crossover?

Write the arithmetic down. API cost = volume × price/doc; build cost = one-off labelling + training + a fixed serving footprint + maintenance. The crossover is usually low — often tens of thousands of documents per day — because a distilled encoder on CPU is nearly free per document. Non-cost factors that can decide it either way: latency SLA, data residency and privacy, the need to iterate on a schema, and whether the task is narrow (favours build) or open-ended (favours API). Then write the decision and its crossover volume down, so it can be revisited rather than relitigated.

7.12 The 15 trap questions ⭐⭐

Three things get people rejected in NLP interviews: quoting a legacy answer as if it were current, reporting a metric that cannot support the claim, and reaching for the biggest model when the constraint was latency. Every row below is one of those.

TrapNaive answerWhat a strong candidate says
“Always remove stopwords and lemmatize first.”AgreesOnly for lexical/classical features. Before a transformer it's harmful — you create a train/serve mismatch with the pretraining distribution and destroy negation (“not good”) and casing.
“How do you handle OOV words?”“Map them to <UNK>.”With subword (especially byte-level) tokenization there are no OOVs. <UNK> is a word-level-vocabulary artefact. For static embeddings, FastText composes from character n-grams.
“Attention weights show what the model used.”AgreesAttention is not explanation — weights can be redistributed with no output change, and information also flows through residuals and the FFN. Use attribution methods, validate by ablation.
“Lower the temperature to stop hallucinations.”AgreesTemperature controls variance, not truthfulness. Ground with retrieval, constrain the output, verify against a source, allow abstention — then measure faithfulness with NLI/QA-based metrics.
“BERT is a language model — what's its perplexity?”Reports a numberBERT is a masked LM; autoregressive perplexity doesn't apply (pseudo-perplexity is a different quantity). And perplexity isn't comparable across tokenizers anyway.
“Our NER is 98% accurate.”ProudThat's token accuracy, dominated by O. Report entity-level F1 with exact boundary + type via seqeval, per entity type.
“Just increase max_length / the context window.”AgreesQuadratic attention, growing KV cache, positions beyond the trained range, and lost-in-the-middle degradation. Chunk, retrieve, or use a model trained long — and measure effective context.
“Fine-tune the model so it knows our documentation.”AgreesFine-tuning teaches form and behaviour, not fresh facts. Volatile, citable or access-controlled knowledge belongs in retrieval; doing it by fine-tuning is expensive, stale, uncitable and hallucination-prone.
“Use the biggest LLM — it's state of the art.”AgreesAt volume a fine-tuned or distilled 150M encoder wins on latency, cost and often accuracy. Use the LLM as a teacher and serve the small model.
“ROUGE tells us if the summary is good.”AgreesIt measures n-gram overlap with a reference, so it cannot see a hallucination and punishes valid paraphrase. Add an NLI- or QA-based faithfulness metric and a rubric-bound judge.
“Use the slow tokenizer to debug offsets.”AgreesThe slow/fast distinction was removed in transformers v5. Use the Rust-backed tokenizer with return_offsets_mapping / word_ids(). (Also: TF/Flax support was removed — PyTorch only.)
“GRU is just a worse LSTM.”AgreesComparable on most tasks with ~25% fewer parameters, often better with limited data; LSTM can edge ahead on very long sequences. Choose empirically.
“temperature=0 makes it deterministic.”AgreesNear-deterministic. Batching changes floating-point reduction order, kernels are non-deterministic, and MoE routing depends on batch composition. Cache the response if you need byte-identical output.
“More data always helps.”AgreesNot with a 3% label error rate, near-duplicate leakage between train and test, or a drifted distribution. Clean, dedup and slice first — a smaller correct dataset usually wins.
“Our multilingual model is fine — macro average is 0.85.”AgreesReport per language. Non-Latin scripts pay a token premium, get less pretraining data, and usually carry most of the error.

9Why is BLEU precision-based and ROUGE recall-based?BLEU guards against under-generation with a brevity penalty (MT must say everything); ROUGE rewards covering the reference (summaries must not omit)
10Training FLOPs and AdamW memory≈6·N·D FLOPs; ≈16 bytes/param in mixed precision (bf16 weights + fp32 master + 2 moments) — the number that kills “let's pretrain”
Bonus: micro-F1 = accuracy

In single-label multi-class classification every misclassification is simultaneously a false positive for the predicted class and a false negative for the true one. So ΣFP = ΣFN, which makes micro-precision = micro-recall = micro-F1 = accuracy. Being able to prove this in one line is a small, memorable credibility win.

Gotchas cheat sheet

The tokenizer must match the checkpoint — mismatch is silent garbage. Sub-token label alignment is the #1 NER bug (word_ids() + -100). Raw [CLS] is not a sentence embedding. Embedding models often need instruction prefixes (query:/passage:). Normalize before cosine. max_length truncation is silent — log the rate. Padding must be masked in pooling, loss and metrics. Micro-F1 == accuracy in single-label multi-class. Perplexity isn't comparable across tokenizers. Near-duplicate leakage inflates everything — dedup before splitting. Random splits are wrong for grouped or temporal data. Don't lemmatize or strip stopwords before a transformer. Beam search is bland for open-ended generation. temperature=0 is near-deterministic, not reproducible. Attention weights are not explanations. Pin revision= to a commit SHA, not main. use_fast and the TF/Flax paths are gone in transformers v5. Report per-language and per-class, never only the macro average. Label noise caps your F1 — audit 200 random labels before blaming the model.
