Task taxonomy, the linguistic pipeline, BoW/TF-IDF/LSA/LDA

1.1 Why language is hard

Every technique in this subject exists to work around one of four properties of language. Name the property and the technique stops being arbitrary.

Interviewers rarely ask “why is NLP hard” directly. They ask it obliquely — “why can't you one-hot encode words and use a random forest?” — and the answer is a list of properties, not a list of tools.

PropertyWhat it meansWhat it forces into existence
AmbiguityLexical (“bank”), syntactic (“I saw the man with the telescope”), semantic scope, pragmatic reference (“it”)Contextual representations; parsing; coreference
DiscretenessWords are symbols with no natural metric. Pixels have distance; “cat” and “dog” do notEvery embedding method ever invented
Compositionality & distanceMeaning depends on structure, and on tokens arbitrarily far awayRecurrence, then attention
Zipf's lawFrequency ∝ 1/rank — a handful of words dominate, and the tail never endsSubword tokenization, smoothing, the <UNK> problem
MorphologyEnglish is morphologically poor; Turkish, Finnish and Tamil are notLemmatization, character n-grams, SentencePiece
VariationTypos, code-switching, transliteration, domain driftRobustness testing, byte-level tokenizers, drift monitoring
The answer they're looking for

“Why not one-hot + random forest?” One-hot vectors are mutually orthogonal, so the cosine similarity between any two distinct words is exactly 0 — the representation encodes no similarity at all. Add |V| ≈ 10⁵–10⁶ dimensions, no word order, and no way to represent a word you didn't see in training, and you have described every limitation the next 1.13–1.17 sections fix.

1.2 The task taxonomy ⭐

The single most useful habit in an NLP interview: before proposing anything, name the task type. It determines the architecture family, the metric, and the labelling cost — all three, automatically.

Candidates who skip this step end up recommending a decoder LLM for a job an encoder does better and 50× cheaper, or reporting accuracy on a task where accuracy is meaningless. Candidates who do it sound senior in one sentence.

Watch the framing

“Extract the customer's issue from this email” can be any of four task types — classification into a taxonomy, span extraction, seq2seq generation, or structured extraction into JSON — and each choice implies a different metric and a different annotation budget. Clarifying which one is the first thing a strong candidate does, and it is usually the difference between a good design answer and a rambling one.

1.3 The five eras — and why all five still get asked

Interview questions are archaeological. A question about HMMs is really a question about generative vs discriminative models; a question about TF-IDF is really about sparse vs dense representation.

The senior answer to “is classical NLP dead?”

No — it moved down the stack. You still tokenize, still normalise, still write regex and gazetteer passes for precision, still report entity-level F1, and you frequently distil an LLM into a small encoder because an LLM at 40 ms and 1,000 QPS is unaffordable. What died is hand-built feature engineering as the primary source of signal, not the classical layer itself.

1.4 Data before models

Most NLP failures are data failures wearing a modelling costume. Two of the items below cause more “great offline, terrible online” incidents than every architecture choice combined.

Your label guidelines are the real model specification. If two annotators disagree on 25% of cases, no architecture will exceed that ceiling — and your test set is measuring noise. Before touching a model, a strong candidate asks: who labelled this, against what guideline, and how much did they agree?

Data issueSymptomFix
Near-duplicate leakageExcellent test metrics, poor production metricsMinHash/LSH dedup before splitting — not after
Random split on grouped dataSame customer/document in train and testSplit by document, user, or time
Label noiseMetric plateaus below the achievable ceilingAudit 200 random labels; confident learning (Cleanlab)
Class imbalanceHigh accuracy, useless recall on the class you care aboutMacro-F1, class weights, threshold tuning
Low annotator agreementCeiling on any measurable improvementRewrite the guideline; consider soft labels
Licensing / PII in the corpusA legal problem discovered post-launchProvenance manifest, de-identification pass
The classic interview trap

“More data always helps.” Not with a 3% label error rate, not with near-duplicate leakage between train and test, and not with a drifted distribution. A smaller, clean, correctly-split dataset routinely beats a larger dirty one — and it makes every subsequent experiment interpretable.

1.5 Normalization

The step where well-intentioned cleaning quietly destroys signal — and where the correct amount depends entirely on what comes next.

The toolkit: Unicode normalization (NFC/NFKC), casefolding, accent stripping, whitespace and punctuation handling, HTML/boilerplate removal, encoding-error repair (mojibake), contraction expansion, spelling correction, emoji handling, and placeholder substitution for URLs, numbers and dates.

1.6 Classical tokenization

Splitting text into units, before subwords existed. This is where the OOV problem is born — which makes it the setup for the whole of Part 3.

Approaches: whitespace splitting, regex/Penn-Treebank rules, and learned models. Sentence segmentation is a model, not a split('.') — abbreviations (Mr., e.g.), decimals, ellipses and quoted speech all break the naive version. Languages without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai) turn word segmentation into its own supervised task.

Where this leads

Word-level tokenization implies a fixed vocabulary, which implies out-of-vocabulary words, which implies <UNK> and all its downstream damage. Subword tokenization (and §3.9–3.15 in Part 3) exists precisely to dissolve that chain.

1.7 Stemming vs lemmatization

A guaranteed interview question, usually asked together with tokenization — and the interesting half is when you would use either in 2026.

TokenizationStemmingLemmatization

Over-stemming vs under-stemming

Over-stemming collapses words that should stay apart: universal, university and universe all become univers. Under-stemming fails to collapse words that should merge (datum / data). Aggressive stemmers (Lancaster) do more of the first; conservative ones (Snowball) do more of the second.

1.8 Stopwords

Removing the most frequent words reduces noise and dimensionality — and in four common situations destroys the thing you were trying to measure.

Why remove them: Zipf's law means a few dozen types carry a huge share of tokens, contributing dimensions with almost no discriminative power. When not to:

SituationWhy removal breaks it
Sentiment / negation“not good” and “good” become the same document
Phrase or exact-match search“to be or not to be” is entirely stopwords
POS tagging / parsingFunction words are the syntactic skeleton
Any transformer inputRemoving them deviates from the pretraining distribution
The better answer than a stopword list

Use max_df in your vectorizer. It removes terms that appear in more than x% of documents — which is a data-driven, domain-adapted, language-agnostic stopword list computed from your corpus rather than imported from NLTK. In a corpus of insurance claims, “claim” is a stopword and no downloaded list will tell you that.

1.9 POS tagging

Labelling each token with its grammatical category. Asked partly for itself, and partly as a vehicle for the HMM/CRF question in Part 2.

Tagsets: Penn Treebank (fine-grained, English-specific — NN, VBZ, JJR) vs Universal Dependencies (17 coarse cross-lingual tags — NOUN, VERB, ADJ). Open classes (nouns, verbs) grow; closed classes (determiners, prepositions) don't.

What POS tagging is still for: input to lemmatization, candidate generation for aspect terms in ABSA, linguistic filtering for keyphrase extraction, features for rule-based high-precision extraction, and negation/scope detection in clinical text.

1.10 Syntactic parsing

Recovering sentence structure. Two formalisms, two metrics, and a handful of uses that genuinely survive into 2026.

Uses that survive: relation-extraction features, ABSA opinion-target linking, high-precision rule-based extraction, negation and scope detection in clinical text, and schema-linking heuristics for text-to-SQL. Non-projective arcs (crossing dependencies) matter for freer-word-order languages.

1.11 NER and BIO tagging

The classical framing of extraction — and the tagging scheme that turns “find the entities” into a per-token classification problem you can actually train.

Classical recipe: gazetteers and rules for precision, a learned model for recall. That split is still the right production architecture — a fine-tuned model with a curated allow-list on top beats either alone.

1.14 TF-IDF ⭐

Know the formula, not the vibe. “It weights important words higher” is not an answer — the interviewer wants the two components and the reason each exists.

Always run this first

This nine-line baseline takes minutes, trains on a laptop, serves in microseconds, and is fully interpretable. If your fine-tuned transformer can't beat it by a margin that justifies the GPU, that is a finding worth reporting — and saying so out loud in an interview reads as judgment, not laziness.

Also know: CountVectorizer (raw counts) vs TfidfVectorizer vs HashingVectorizer. The hashing trick maps terms into a fixed number of buckets via a hash function — no vocabulary to fit or persist, streaming-friendly, constant memory — at the cost of collisions and no inverse transform (so no interpretability).

1.15 Similarity and distance for text

Four measures, four different questions. Choosing the wrong one is a silent bug.

MeasureAnswersUse forWatch out
CosineSame direction?The default for text — scale-invariant, so document length doesn't dominateOn sparse vectors, zero overlap ⇒ 0 even for paraphrases
Dot productSame direction and magnitude?Embeddings trained with dot-product objectives; magnitude can encode “importance”Long documents win by accident if not normalised
JaccardHow much do the token sets overlap?Near-duplicate detection; with MinHash + LSH it scales to billionsIgnores frequency entirely

The vocabulary-mismatch problem

“How do I cancel my subscription?” and “I want to end my membership” share almost no terms, so their TF-IDF cosine similarity is near zero despite being the same request. No amount of tuning fixes this — it is a property of sparse lexical representation, and it is the single strongest motivation for dense embeddings.

1.16 LSA, NMF and LDA

Three ways to find latent structure in a document-term matrix. LDA is asked most often — not because you'll use it, but because it tests whether you understand generative models.

1.17 The distributional hypothesis

One sentence that every embedding method in the rest of this subject is an implementation of.

Firth, 1957

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” If two words appear in the same contexts, they mean similar things. Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText, BERT and every embedding model in production are engineering answers to that single claim.

1.18 Interview focus for Part 1

The questions this part is designed to make automatic. Answer out loud before opening the answer.

Foundations · 9 questions
Tokenization vs stemming vs lemmatization — and when would you use each in 2026?

Tokenization splits text into units (one text → many tokens). Stemming chops affixes by rule; fast, no lexicon, output may not be a word (happiness → happi). Lemmatization maps to a dictionary lemma using POS and morphology; slower, always a real word (better → good).

The part that earns marks: tokenization always; stemming/lemmatization only for lexical features (TF-IDF, BM25 fields, keyword rules) and morphologically rich languages. Never before a transformer — subword tokenizers already share morphology, and normalising away casing and function words moves your input off the pretraining distribution while destroying negation.

Explain Bag of Words and its three main limitations.

A document becomes a vector of counts over the vocabulary — a multiset, order discarded.

(1) No word order — “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are identical vectors. (2) No semantics — “excellent” and “superb” are as unrelated as “excellent” and “tractor”. (3) High-dimensional sparsity — one dimension per vocabulary term, almost all zero, and the vocabulary drifts at serve time. Add a fourth for credit: every term is weighted by raw count, so a word in every document counts as much as a discriminative one.

Write down TF-IDF. Why does IDF help, and where does TF-IDF break?

tfidf(t,d) = tf(t,d) × idf(t), with idf(t) = log(N/df(t)) — in scikit-learn the smoothed log((1+N)/(1+df)) + 1 — followed by L2 normalization of each document vector so that cosine equals dot product.

Why IDF helps: it down-weights terms that appear everywhere (no discriminative power) and up-weights terms concentrated in few documents. Where it breaks: no semantics (vocabulary mismatch means paraphrases score ~0), still sparse and high-dimensional, unstable IDF on tiny corpora, and rare typos or IDs can receive enormous weight. Also: tf grows without bound, which is what BM25's k₁ saturation fixes.

TF-IDF vs BM25 — what exactly does BM25 add?

Two things. Term-frequency saturation via k₁: the 20th occurrence of a term adds far less than the 2nd, whereas TF-IDF's tf term is linear and unbounded. Document-length normalization via b: without it, long documents accumulate matches and win by size alone.

BM25 is also derived from a probabilistic relevance model rather than assembled heuristically. It remains the lexical half of every serious hybrid retrieval system in 2026 — the dense half never fully replaced it, because exact term matching on names, codes and rare identifiers is something embeddings are bad at.

Why is a split('.') sentence splitter wrong, and why is POS tagging still useful?

Sentence splitting: abbreviations (Dr., e.g., Inc.), decimals (3.14), ellipses, quoted speech and URLs all contain periods that don't end sentences — and some languages don't use periods at all. It is a learned component in spaCy for a reason.

POS tagging in 2026: input to lemmatization; candidate generation for ABSA aspect terms; linguistic filters for keyphrase extraction; features for high-precision rule-based extraction; negation and scope detection in clinical text. Quote the baseline too — ~97% accuracy sounds impressive until you note that assigning each word its most frequent tag gets ~90%.

Constituency vs dependency parsing — and one production use for either.

Constituency parsing produces a phrase-structure tree (S → NP VP), typically via CKY over a PCFG; evaluated with labelled bracket F1. Dependency parsing produces labelled head→dependent arcs between words, via transition-based (arc-eager) or graph-based (biaffine) methods; evaluated with UAS (arcs correct) and LAS (arcs and labels correct).

Production use: dependency arcs for relation extraction and for linking an opinion word to its target in aspect-based sentiment; constituency phrases for noun-phrase candidate extraction. Both also power negation-scope rules in clinical NLP, where a false negative is a safety issue and you want deterministic behaviour.

What are BIO tags, why does NER use them, and what can't they express?

Begin / Inside / Outside per token turns span-finding into a token-classification problem you can train with a standard softmax. The B/I distinction is what distinguishes two adjacent single-word entities from one two-word entity. BIOES adds End and Single for sharper boundaries at the cost of more classes.

What it can't express: nested entities (Bank of [America]), overlapping types on the same token, and discontinuous spans. Those need span-based enumeration or generative extraction. And critically: evaluate with entity-level F1 (exact boundary + type, via seqeval) — token accuracy is inflated by the dominant O class.

LSA vs LDA vs NMF, how do you choose k, and why coherence over perplexity?

LSA is truncated SVD of the term-document matrix — fast, deterministic, handles synonymy, but components can be negative and aren't interpretable as topics. NMF factorizes into non-negative parts, often giving the most readable topics, with no probabilistic story. LDA is generative: documents are Dirichlet mixtures over topics (α), topics are Dirichlet distributions over words (β), inferred by collapsed Gibbs sampling or variational EM.

Choosing k: sweep and evaluate with topic coherence (C_v or UMass), not held-out perplexity — the well-known result is that perplexity can anti-correlate with human interpretability. And say the modern part: for short text, embeddings + HDBSCAN + c-TF-IDF (BERTopic-style) is the better default; LDA still earns its place on long, clean documents where you want interpretable priors.

State the distributional hypothesis, and connect the counting and prediction routes.

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth) — words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Every embedding method implements this.

The counting route: build a co-occurrence matrix, reweight with PPMI (raw counts over-reward frequent words), factorize with SVD to ~300 dimensions. The prediction route: Word2Vec's skip-gram trains vectors to predict context. The connection worth stating — Levy & Goldberg showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorizes a shifted PMI matrix, so these are two views of one computation rather than competing philosophies.

2.1 Naive Bayes

The baseline that refuses to die, and a small paradox interviewers enjoy: its central assumption is false, and it works anyway.

Naive Bayes applies Bayes' rule with a conditional independence assumption: given the class, every feature is independent of every other. For text that is plainly wrong — “New” and “York” are not independent — yet the model remains competitive on small datasets and takes milliseconds to train.

VariantModelsUse when
Multinomial NBWord countsThe default for text classification
Bernoulli NBWord presence/absenceShort texts; presence matters more than frequency
Complement NBCounts, from the complement classImbalanced text data — usually beats Multinomial there
Why it works despite violated assumptions

Classification only needs the argmax to be correct, not the probabilities. Dependence between features distorts the magnitude of the posterior badly, but often preserves its ranking — so the decision survives even though the confidence is meaningless. Two consequences you should volunteer: Naive Bayes probabilities are famously uncalibrated (never threshold them directly), and you need Laplace/Lidstone smoothing plus log-space computation to avoid zero probabilities and floating-point underflow.

2.2 Logistic regression and linear SVM

The baseline you should actually ship first — and the reason is not accuracy, it's that you can read it.

On TF-IDF features, logistic regression is the honest production baseline: convex, fast, calibratable, and interpretable via its coefficients. Linear SVM (hinge loss, maximum margin) is its close cousin and often marginally better on very high-dimensional sparse text. Non-linear kernels rarely help here — in ~50,000 dimensions the data is already close to linearly separable.

KnobEffectWhen
penalty='l2'Shrinks all weightsDefault

The interpretability argument

Print the top positive and negative coefficients per class. You will find label leakage, annotation artefacts and spurious correlations in minutes — a spam classifier keyed on the mail-server hostname, a sentiment model keyed on a product name. That debugging loop is far harder with a transformer, which is why this baseline is a diagnostic tool and not just a benchmark.

2.3 HMM and Viterbi ⭐

The pre-neural way to tag a sequence, and a permanent interview fixture — because if you can explain Viterbi you can explain dynamic programming, and if you can explain an HMM you can explain generative modelling.

An HMM models a sequence of hidden states (tags) emitting observations (words), with three parameter sets: π (initial state distribution), A (state-to-state transitions) and B (state-to-observation emissions). Three canonical problems: likelihood (forward algorithm), decoding (Viterbi), learning (Baum-Welch / EM).

2.4 CRF, label bias, and why a CRF head still helps ⭐

“HMM vs CRF” is one of the cleanest generative-vs-discriminative questions in NLP. There is a crisp answer; learn it verbatim.

Say this verbatim

“HMM models P(x,y) — generative, locally normalized, restrictive independence assumptions, so it can't use overlapping features. CRF models P(y|x) — discriminative, globally normalized over the whole sequence, arbitrary overlapping features, and immune to the label-bias problem that MEMMs have. CRF wins when you have labelled data; HMM needs less of it.”

2.5 Feature engineering — the lost art that still ships

Not nostalgia. This is what your precision layer and your fallback path are made of, and it is how you debug a model that “just knows”.

The one to remember

Character n-grams (3–6) are the cheapest robustness win in NLP: tolerant of typos, useful for morphologically rich languages, script-agnostic, and bounded in vocabulary. If a text pipeline is failing on noisy user input, adding character n-grams is the first thing to try — and it's the same idea FastText later formalised.

2.6 Classification metrics you must not fumble

The single most common way to lose an otherwise-good interview: reporting a number that cannot support the claim you just made.

The tell

In single-label multi-class classification, micro-F1 is exactly equal to accuracy (every error is simultaneously one FP and one FN). Reporting both as if they were independent evidence is a well-known signal that someone is quoting classification_report without reading it.

2.7 What a language model actually is

A distribution over sequences. Everything else — generation, scoring, pretraining — follows from that one definition.

The Markov approximation, and the sparsity wall it runs into immediately — which is the entire motivation for smoothing.

2.16 Bias in embeddings

Embeddings inherit and often amplify the associations in their corpus. In any regulated domain this is now a standard interview question, and there is a right way to frame the answer.

Measurement: WEAT / SEAT quantify differential association between attribute sets (e.g. gendered names) and target sets (e.g. occupations). Mitigation: hard-debiasing (project out a “gender direction”), INLP (iterative nullspace projection) — and the important finding that these often hide bias in the geometry rather than removing the information, which downstream classifiers can still recover.

The framing that lands

Measure bias at the decision level, not only in the embedding space. What matters is whether the deployed system produces different outcomes for comparable people — so audit per-subgroup metrics on the actual task, use counterfactual pairs (swap the name, swap the pronoun, keep everything else), and document it. Geometric debiasing is a mitigation, not evidence of fairness.

Models, LMs & embeddings · 9 questions
Why does Naive Bayes work when its independence assumption is clearly false?

Classification needs the argmax to be right, not the probabilities. Feature dependence distorts the magnitude of the posterior severely but often preserves its ranking, so the decision survives. Two things to volunteer: NB probabilities are badly uncalibrated (never threshold them directly — calibrate first), and you need Laplace/Lidstone smoothing plus log-space arithmetic to avoid zeros and underflow. For imbalanced text, Complement NB usually beats Multinomial.

⭐ HMM vs CRF — give me the crisp version.

HMM models P(x, y) — generative, locally normalized, with restrictive independence assumptions (emissions independent given the state), so it cannot use arbitrary overlapping features. CRF models P(y | x) — discriminative, globally normalized over the entire sequence, accepts arbitrary overlapping features, and does not suffer the label-bias problem that MEMMs do (where a state with few outgoing transitions hoards probability mass because normalisation happens per step).

Trade-off: CRF wins when you have labelled data; HMM needs less of it. And the modern relevance: a CRF layer on top of BiLSTM or BERT still helps, because it learns a transition matrix that rules out structurally invalid tag sequences (I-PER directly after O) which a per-token softmax cannot.

Write Viterbi's recursion and state its complexity.

δ_t(j) = max_i [ δ_{t-1}(i) + log a_ij ] + log b_j(o_t), storing argmax_i as a backpointer at each cell, then walking the backpointers from the best final state to recover the sequence.

Complexity: O(T·|S|²) time, O(T·|S|) space. The key insight is that you keep only the single best path into each state at each timestep — that max is what collapses an exponential search into a table. Always compute in log-space: products of probabilities underflow within a few dozen tokens. (Swap max for logsumexp and you have the forward algorithm instead.)

Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report, and what's the tell?

Micro pools all TP/FP/FN across classes, so it is dominated by the majority class. Macro averages per-class F1 equally, so a rare class counts as much as a common one. Report macro (plus per-class) when the rare class is the point — which in NLP it usually is. A model can show micro-F1 0.94 and macro-F1 0.60 simultaneously.

The tell: in single-label multi-class classification, micro-F1 is exactly equal to accuracy (each error is one FP and one FN), so quoting both as separate evidence signals someone reading classification_report without understanding it. Also: PR-AUC over ROC-AUC for rare positives, and per-label thresholds for multi-label.

Explain perplexity, and give me two reasons a lower-perplexity model can be worse.

PPL = exp(−(1/N) Σ log P(w_i | context)) = 2^(cross-entropy in bits) = the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities. Interpret it as an effective branching factor: perplexity 20 means the model is as uncertain as choosing uniformly among 20 words at each step.

Reason one: perplexity is not comparable across tokenizers — a larger vocabulary splits text into more, individually-easier-to-predict tokens, which lowers perplexity for free. Normalise to bits-per-byte to compare. Reason two: it only measures fit to a text distribution, not helpfulness, factuality, instruction-following or safety — a model can be an excellent next-token predictor and a bad assistant. Bonus third: it does not apply in the usual sense to masked LMs like BERT.

Beam search vs top-p vs greedy — pick one per task and justify it.

Greedy / temperature ≈ 0 for extraction, classification and code — there is one right answer and you want reproducibility. Beam search (B≈4–5, length-normalized) for closed-ended seq2seq like MT and reference-based summarization, where you're searching for the single best sequence. Top-p ≈ 0.9 with temperature 0.7–1.0 for open-ended generation, because beam search there produces bland, repetitive text.

Mechanism worth adding: temperature scales logits before softmax (variance, not truthfulness); top-k takes a fixed candidate count and so is mis-sized whenever the model is very confident or very unsure; top-p adapts by taking the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches p; min-p keeps a floor relative to the top token and is more stable at high temperature.

⭐ CBOW vs skip-gram, and explain negative sampling.

CBOW predicts the centre word from the averaged context — faster, better for frequent words, but averaging blurs the signal for rare ones. Skip-gram predicts each context word from the centre word — one update per (centre, context) pair, so it is better for rare words and small corpora. That asymmetry is the answer.

Negative sampling replaces the |V|-way softmax (400k dot products per example) with k+1 binary logistic decisions: one true pair labelled 1, and k sampled noise pairs labelled 0. Details that earn credit: k = 5–20 for small corpora, 2–5 for large; the noise distribution is unigram^(3/4), which flattens Zipf so rare words appear as negatives more often than raw frequency implies; frequent words are subsampled with P(discard) = 1 − √(t/f(w)). The alternative is hierarchical softmax, an O(log|V|) Huffman tree.

How do you handle OOV words, and why are “good” and “bad” close together?

OOV, in two layers. For static embeddings: FastText represents a word as a bag of character n-grams (3–6) plus the whole word, so an unseen word still composes a sensible vector — which also makes it strong for morphologically rich languages and typos. For anything modern: the question dissolves, because subword and especially byte-level tokenizers can encode any string, so there are no OOVs and <UNK> is an artefact of fixed word-level vocabularies.

Good/bad: embeddings encode distributional similarity, and antonyms occur in nearly identical contexts (“the food was ___”). So they land close together. Consequences: you cannot do sentiment by cosine distance to “good”, and a purely dense retriever can return the semantic opposite of a query.

Static vs contextual embeddings — what exactly does contextual buy you?

Static gives one vector per word type, so “bank” receives a single vector blending river-bank and investment-bank; polysemy is unrepresentable by construction. Contextual gives one vector per occurrence, computed from the surrounding sentence — ELMo did it with a BiLSTM and task-weighted layer combination, then BERT with self-attention.

What it buys: word-sense disambiguation largely for free, tractable NER and coreference, and representations that transfer to almost any task by fine-tuning. One caveat to mention: contextual spaces are anisotropic — vectors cluster in a narrow cone — so raw cosine similarity between untuned BERT vectors is unreliable. You need normalisation, whitening, or (properly) a sentence-level training objective like SBERT's.

3.1 RNN mechanics

One weight matrix, applied over and over. That repetition is where the power comes from — and where all three limitations come from too.

The recurrence: h_t = tanh(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b), output y_t = g(W_y h_t). The hidden state h_t is a compressed summary of everything seen so far, and the same weights are shared across every timestep — which is why an RNN can handle variable-length input at all.

3.2 Vanishing and exploding gradients ⭐

Same cause, opposite symptoms, different fixes. Candidates who lump them together lose the follow-up.

3.3 LSTM ⭐

Guaranteed to come up. The question is never “what is an LSTM” — it's “walk me through the gates” and “how exactly does the cell state fix vanishing gradients?”

Say it like this

“A vanilla RNN's gradient is multiplied by the same weight matrix at every step, so it shrinks or grows geometrically. The LSTM adds a separate cell state that is updated additively and gated elementwise — so there is a path along which the gradient is multiplied only by the forget gate. With the forget gate near 1, that path preserves gradient magnitude over hundreds of steps. It's called the constant error carousel.”

3.4 GRU

Two gates instead of three, one state instead of two. The honest answer to “which is better” is not a preference.

3.5 Bidirectional and deep RNNs

A BiRNN/BiLSTM runs one pass left-to-right and one right-to-left, then concatenates the states, so each position sees the full sentence. That is legal only when the whole sequence is available up front.

3.11 WordPiece

BPE with a better merge criterion. One formula separates them, and knowing it is a cheap way to sound precise.

Used by BERT, DistilBERT and ELECTRA. Instead of merging the most frequent pair, WordPiece merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the training data — which in practice scores candidates by

The one formula

score(A,B) = freq(AB) / (freq(A) × freq(B))

A pair only wins if it occurs together more than its parts' individual frequencies would predict. So WordPiece prefers merges that are informative, while BPE prefers merges that are merely common — a very frequent character next to a very frequent character gets penalised.

Other mechanics: continuation pieces are marked ## (token, ##ization), and encoding is greedy longest-match-first from the left rather than a merge replay. Text is pre-split on whitespace and punctuation, so it is not directly usable for languages without spaces.

How does an RNN work, and what are its real limitations?

h_t = tanh(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b) — a hidden state carrying a compressed summary of the prefix, with the same weights applied at every timestep. Trained with backpropagation through time.

Four limitations, precisely: (1) vanishing/exploding gradients, because the gradient through k steps carries a factor of W_h^k; (2) no parallelism over time — step t needs step t−1, so training can't be parallelised along the sequence, which is the property transformers exploit; (3) degraded long-range memory even with gating; (4) in seq2seq, the fixed-size context vector bottleneck.

Vanishing vs exploding gradients — same problem?

Same cause, opposite symptoms, different fixes. Both come from repeatedly multiplying by W_h: if the spectral radius is below 1 the gradient shrinks geometrically, above 1 it grows.

Exploding is loud — loss spikes, then NaN — and the fix is numerical: gradient clipping by global norm, one line. Vanishing is silent and far more dangerous: training appears to succeed while the model learns only short-range structure. The fix is architectural: gating (LSTM/GRU), plus orthogonal initialisation, layer norm and skip connections. Saying “numerical problem vs architectural problem” is what earns the point.

⭐ Walk through the LSTM gates. How exactly does the cell state fix vanishing gradients?

Forget f_t = σ(W_f·[h_{t-1},x_t]) — how much of the old cell state to keep. Input i_t — how much new content to write. Candidate C̃_t = tanh(...) — what that content is. Then C_t = f_t ⊙ C_{t-1} + i_t ⊙ C̃_t. Output o_t, and h_t = o_t ⊙ tanh(C_t). Sigmoid for gates (a 0–1 valve), tanh for content (a signed signal).

The mechanism: the cell state is updated additively and gated elementwise, so the gradient path backward along C is multiplied only by f_t — not by a weight matrix. With the forget gate near 1, gradient magnitude is preserved over hundreds of steps. That path is the constant error carousel. Parameter count: 4 × ((d_in + d_h)·d_h + d_h).

What is the seq2seq bottleneck, how did attention fix it, and what is exposure bias?

Bottleneck: the encoder compresses the entire input into one fixed-size context vector, so quality degrades as input length grows — visible as a falling BLEU-vs-length curve. Attention lets the decoder read all encoder states, weighted per output step: c_t = Σ α_{t,i} h_i with α = softmax(score). Bahdanau's score is additive (vᵀtanh(W₁h+W₂s)), Luong's multiplicative (sᵀWh) — the latter is a matmul, which is why it's the ancestor of QKᵀ.

Exposure bias: during training the decoder is fed gold prefixes (teacher forcing); at inference it is fed its own predictions, so one early error compounds into a distribution it never trained on. Scheduled sampling is a partial fix. And add the caveat: attention weights look like alignments and help debugging, but attention is not explanation.

⭐ Explain BPE — training and then encoding.

Training: start with the corpus split into characters. Count all adjacent symbol pairs, merge the most frequent pair into a single symbol, append it to an ordered merge list, and repeat until you hit the target vocabulary size. Encoding: split the input into characters and replay the learned merges in order, greedily — which makes it deterministic.

Details that show depth: the merge order is the model (a different order gives different tokens); pre-tokenization on whitespace/punctuation happens first; and byte-level BPE (GPT-2 onward) runs over the 256 raw bytes so any Unicode string is representable with zero UNK — at the cost of non-Latin characters consuming several tokens each.

BPE vs WordPiece vs Unigram vs SentencePiece.

Direction and criterion. BPE and WordPiece both merge upward; Unigram prunes downward from a large candidate vocabulary using EM plus loss-based pruning. BPE merges by raw pair frequency; WordPiece merges by likelihood, scoring roughly freq(AB)/(freq(A)·freq(B)), so it prefers informative merges over merely common ones. Unigram keeps a probability per piece, so it can return the most probable segmentation or sample one — which enables subword regularization (BPE-dropout is the BPE equivalent).

SentencePiece is not an algorithm — it is a framework implementing BPE and Unigram whose real contribution is treating input as a raw byte/character stream with whitespace encoded as ▁. That makes it pre-tokenizer-free, exactly reversible, and usable for Chinese, Japanese and Thai. Users: GPT/Llama/RoBERTa (BPE), BERT/ELECTRA (WordPiece), T5/ALBERT/XLNet and most multilingual models (Unigram via SentencePiece).

Why can't a model count the r's in “strawberry”, and why is your Hindi app 3× the token cost?

Both are tokenizer artefacts, not reasoning failures. “strawberry” is two or three opaque subword tokens — the model has no view of the individual letters, so character counting, string reversal and rhyming are structurally hard. (Related: inconsistent digit grouping is a genuine cause of arithmetic errors, which is why some tokenizers split digits individually.)

Token premium: under a byte-level BPE trained mostly on English, non-Latin scripts consume several tokens per character. The same content therefore costs more money, more latency, and gets a smaller effective context — for those users only. Measure it with characters-per-token and fertility. Framing it as simultaneously a cost, latency and fairness issue is what makes this a senior answer.

Your NER labels are misaligned after tokenization. What went wrong, and what breaks at eval time?

Training side: one word became several sub-tokens and the word-level labels were applied positionally without alignment. The fix is to use word_ids() (or offset_mapping): assign the label to the first sub-token of each word and mask continuations with -100, PyTorch's ignore_index, so they contribute no loss. Special tokens get -100 too.

Evaluation side — the mirror-image bug: you must re-aggregate sub-token predictions into word-level spans before computing entity-level F1 with seqeval. Scoring at the sub-token level measures a different task and usually flatters the model. Neither bug raises an error, which is exactly why they're common. Also note: in transformers v5 there is no slow/fast tokenizer choice — offset_mapping and word_ids() are always available.

Transformers & pretraining · 10 questions
⭐ Write scaled dot-product attention. Why √d_k?

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) V, with Q = XW_Q, K = XW_K, V = XW_V. Query = “what am I looking for”, key = “what do I offer”, value = “what I contribute if selected”.

Why √d_k: if q and k components have unit variance, their dot product sums d_k such terms, so its variance is d_k. With d_k=64 the logits swing wide enough that softmax saturates into a near one-hot distribution — and the gradient of a saturated softmax is close to zero, so learning stalls. Dividing by √d_k restores unit variance and keeps softmax in its responsive regime. Mentioning the gradient is what makes this a complete answer.

Why multi-head rather than one big head?

Not for capacity — d_model is split, so h heads of d_model/h dimensions cost roughly the same as one d_model head. The gain is representation subspaces: a single head must average every kind of relation into one attention distribution, whereas separate heads can specialise — previous-token/positional heads, syntactic dependency heads, coreference heads, delimiter heads.

Nuance worth volunteering: many heads turn out to be redundant and can be pruned with little quality loss, which is evidence that the benefit is diversity of relations rather than raw parameter count.

⭐ Why is positional encoding necessary, and why did the field move to RoPE?

Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: permute the input and the output permutes identically. Without position information, “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are the same input.

The lineage: sinusoidal absolute (parameter-free, relative offsets are linear functions of it, extrapolates somewhat) → learned absolute (BERT/GPT-2; cannot extrapolate at all past the trained maximum) → relative (Shaw, T5 buckets, Transformer-XL; generalises across lengths because distance is what matters) → RoPE, which rotates Q and K by a position-dependent angle so that qᵢᵀkⱼ depends only on i−j.

Why RoPE won: relative by construction, zero added parameters, compatible with KV caching, and — decisively — extensible after training by rescaling its frequency spectrum. That's what rope_scaling does: Position Interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN (preserves short-range behaviour while stretching long-range, roughly 10× fewer tokens and 2.5× fewer steps than PI) and LongRoPE (non-uniform, to ~2M). ALiBi is the alternative: a linear distance penalty on the logits with no positional embeddings, which extrapolates well.

Why LayerNorm and not BatchNorm? Pre-LN vs post-LN?

LayerNorm normalizes across the feature dimension within each token. BatchNorm normalizes across the batch, which breaks for text: sequences are variable-length so batch statistics are computed over inconsistent padding, batches are small, and BN's inference behaviour depends on running statistics collected from training batches.

Pre-LN vs post-LN: the original transformer put the norm after the residual addition (post-LN), which places a normalization on the residual path — gradients get rescaled at every block, so deep models are unstable and need learning-rate warmup. Modern models use pre-LN (norm inside the residual branch), leaving a clean identity path from input to output, usually plus one final norm. Many also swap to RMSNorm — no mean subtraction, no bias, cheaper, works just as well.

How does causal masking work, and why does it make training efficient?

Before the softmax, set the logits for all future positions to −∞ (a lower-triangular mask), so position t can only attend to positions ≤ t. That guarantees no label leakage from the token being predicted.

Why it's efficient: because the mask enforces causality structurally, you can compute the loss for every next-token prediction in the document in one parallel forward pass — an RNN needs one sequential step per token. That single property is what made pretraining on trillions of tokens economically viable. Note it applies only at training time: generation is inherently sequential.

⭐ Explain the KV cache, and why decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound.

During generation, the keys and values for previous tokens don't change, so you cache them instead of recomputing. Size: 2 · layers · heads_kv · d_head · seq_len · batch · bytes — linear in both sequence length and batch size.

The two regimes: prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel — large matmuls, compute-bound, and it sets TTFT. Decode produces one token per forward pass, so you re-read all model weights and the entire KV cache to compute a single token — the arithmetic intensity is terrible, making it memory-bandwidth-bound and setting inter-token latency. This framing explains why GQA/MLA, speculative decoding, continuous batching and paged attention all exist: they attack the decode-side memory wall, not FLOPs.

⭐ Why isn't “just increase the context length” the answer to long documents?

Four reasons, in order of how often they bite. (1) Attention compute is quadratic in length. (2) The KV cache grows linearly and competes with weights for VRAM, which caps your batch size and therefore your throughput and cost. (3) Positions beyond the trained range need RoPE rescaling (YaRN/NTK) or quality degrades — a longer window isn't free even architecturally. (4) Effective context is smaller than advertised context: lost-in-the-middle and long-context degradation are measurable, so material buried mid-context is under-used.

The framing: a context window is a budget you allocate, not a bucket you fill. Chunk and retrieve, or use a model actually trained long — and measure effective context with needle-in-a-haystack style probes rather than trusting the spec sheet.

Explain MLM including the 80/10/10 recipe, and what RoBERTa changed.

MLM: mask roughly 15% of tokens and predict the originals using context from both directions. Of the selected tokens, about 80% are replaced with [MASK], 10% with a random token, and 10% left unchanged. The random and unchanged cases exist because [MASK] never appears at fine-tuning or inference time — the pretrain/finetune mismatch — so the model must not learn to rely on seeing it.

RoBERTa kept the architecture and fixed the recipe: much more data, much longer training, larger batches, byte-level BPE, dynamic masking (a new mask each epoch rather than one fixed mask), and it dropped NSP after finding it too easy and unhelpful. The lesson to state: training recipe mattered more than architecture — which is the same lesson ModernBERT reinforces.

⭐ ModernBERT or a prompted LLM for a 10M-document/day classifier?

Fine-tuned encoder, and I'd show the arithmetic. At 10M documents/day an LLM API call at even a fraction of a cent per document is a multi-million-dollar annual line item with a latency profile you can't control. A 150M-parameter encoder serves in single-digit milliseconds, runs on CPU if quantized, costs a fixed amount, keeps data in-house, and — with adequate labels — typically matches or beats a prompted general model on a narrow, well-specified task.

How I'd get there: LLM zero-shot to generate labels → human-verify a stratified sample → fine-tune ModernBERT-base (8k context, RoPE, FlashAttention, trained on text and code) → evaluate on a human-labelled test set → distil or quantize for serving. Keep the LLM for the long tail and for periodic re-labelling. And flag the honest caveat: benchmark DeBERTaV3 too — a published comparison found it still competitive, so the winner is task-dependent.

Why is raw BERT's [CLS] a poor sentence embedding, and what's the fix?

[CLS] was trained for Next Sentence Prediction, not similarity. Nothing in MLM pretraining makes cosine distance between two sentence vectors meaningful, and the contextual space is anisotropic — vectors occupy a narrow cone, so everything looks moderately similar to everything.

The fix is SBERT: siamese/triplet fine-tuning with mean pooling and a contrastive objective, so cosine becomes a trained quantity. The payoff is computational — it converts an O(n²) cross-encoder comparison problem into encode-once plus ANN search. Then know the deployment split: bi-encoder to retrieve, cross-encoder to rerank (a cross-encoder needs one forward pass per pair), with ColBERT-style late interaction as the middle ground. Practical details that cost real accuracy if missed: instruction prefixes (query:/passage:), normalising before cosine, and hard negative mining, which is the single biggest quality lever.

5.12 Interview focus for Part 5

Nine questions covering the whole part. Several of these are the most current questions in NLP interviewing.

Efficiency & fine-tuning · 9 questions
⭐ MHA vs MQA vs GQA vs MLA — what does each reduce, and what's the 2026 default?

All four attack the same bottleneck: the KV cache, which is what makes decoding memory-bandwidth-bound. MHA gives every query head its own K/V — largest cache. MQA shares a single K/V head across all query heads — a drastic cache cut, faster decode, but measurable quality loss and training instability. GQA groups query heads with one K/V head per group — MHA-like quality at near-MQA speed, and the industry default (Llama-2 70B onward, Mistral). MLA compresses K and V into a low-rank latent and caches that, decompressing on the fly (DeepSeek).

The one-liner: “MQA and GQA share heads; MLA compresses them.” And the nuance that earns the follow-up: DeepSeek-V2's ablations showed GQA looking worse than MHA on modelling quality while MLA held up and could even beat MHA when tuned — so compression is not obviously the inferior strategy.

What does FlashAttention change? Is it an approximation?

No — it is exact. That's the trap in the question. FlashAttention is an IO-aware algorithm: it tiles Q, K and V into blocks that fit in the GPU's fast on-chip SRAM, computes partial softmaxes with a running max and sum, and never materialises the n×n score matrix in HBM. The backward pass recomputes tiles rather than storing them.

Result: identical mathematics, O(n) memory instead of O(n²), and a large wall-clock speedup — because attention was bound by memory traffic, not arithmetic. Versions 2 and 3 improved parallelism and hardware utilisation. Its importance beyond speed: it made most approximate-attention research unnecessary, because exact attention became fast enough.

Longformer, BigBird, Performer — why did most of these lose?

They approximate attention to escape O(n²): sparse patterns (Longformer's sliding window + global tokens; BigBird adds random attention and comes with theoretical guarantees), low-rank/kernelized (Linformer, Performer's FAVOR+ giving O(n)), and segment recurrence (Transformer-XL).

Why they lost: FlashAttention + GQA + RoPE scaling made exact attention fast and long enough that an approximation's quality cost stopped being worth paying. What survived are patterns that keep exactness locally — sliding-window attention (Mistral) and ModernBERT's alternating local/global layers. Knowing why they lost is a better answer than listing them.

⭐ Mamba/SSMs vs transformers — the trade-off, and why are production models hybrid?

Attention keeps every past token and can recall any one of them exactly, at O(n²) compute with an unbounded KV cache. An SSM (Mamba, Mamba-2) uses a learned, input-dependent linear recurrence: O(n) in sequence length and O(1) state per step at inference. The cost is that the past is summarised into a fixed state, so precise recall of an arbitrary earlier token is weaker — the associative-recall gap.

Hence hybrids, not replacement: interleave a few attention layers among many SSM layers. Attention supplies high-resolution recall; SSM layers summarise context cheaply. Jamba (Transformer–Mamba–MoE) and Hymba (parallel attention + SSM heads, state of the art for sub-2B models) are the reference points. The framing to use: “attention made context accessible; SSMs make context cheap — production models buy both.”

What is a Mixture of Experts, and what does it not save?

Replace the FFN with N expert FFNs plus a router that activates the top k (usually 1–2) per token. So total parameters are large while active parameters per token stay small — cheap FLOPs, high capacity.

What it does not save: memory. Every expert must be resident in VRAM whether or not it fires, so an 8×7B MoE needs roughly 8×7B of weights loaded. Add expert-parallel networking complexity, a load-balancing auxiliary loss to stop the router collapsing onto a few experts, and expert-capacity limits that cause token dropping. Naming the FLOPs-vs-memory asymmetry is the point of the question. Variants: shared and fine-grained experts (DeepSeek-style).

Quantization vs distillation vs pruning vs speculative decoding — pick one per constraint.

Memory-bound → quantization. Post-training (GPTQ, AWQ, GGUF k-quants) or QAT; INT8/INT4/FP8; weight-only vs weight+activation. ~4-bit is the practical floor for quality-sensitive work.

Latency-bound → distillation (and structured pruning). Response/logit-based, feature-based, or task-specific LLM→encoder distillation. DistilBERT is the canonical result: ~40% smaller, ~60% faster, ~97% of GLUE. On pruning, distinguish unstructured (needs sparse kernels to pay off) from structured (drop heads/layers/FFN width — actually faster on real hardware).

Throughput-bound → batching and caching: continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching, and GQA so a bigger batch fits. Speculative decoding also targets latency: a small draft model proposes k tokens and the target verifies them in one parallel pass — lossless w.r.t. the target distribution when done right, and nearly free because decode was bandwidth-bound anyway. Accuracy-bound → none of these help; that's a data and fine-tuning problem.

⭐ Walk the adaptation ladder. Where do you stop?

Zero-shot prompting → few-shot/ICL → retrieval augmentation → feature extraction (freeze the encoder, train a head) → PEFT/LoRA → full fine-tuning → continued/domain pretraining → pretrain from scratch. Cost, data appetite and iteration time rise at every rung.

Where to stop: the first rung that hits your target metric — and the reason you stopped is the answer. With a few hundred labels, feature extraction. With 1k–100k and a single GPU, LoRA or QLoRA. If the gap is knowledge rather than behaviour, retrieval — climbing further won't help. Pretraining from scratch is almost never right, and you should be able to show the FLOP arithmetic (≈6ND) that says so.

Explain LoRA and QLoRA. When is LoRA not enough?

LoRA: freeze W and learn a low-rank update ΔW = BA of rank r, applied as W′ = W + (α/r)·BA. Hyperparameters: r (4–64), alpha (scaling — effective LR ∝ α/r), target_modules (the q/k/v/o projections, often the MLP too), dropout. Trainable parameters drop 100–10,000×. Two properties made it universal: you can merge BA into W at inference so there's zero latency penalty, and you can hot-swap task adapters over one shared base.

QLoRA's four ingredients: a 4-bit NF4-quantized frozen base, LoRA adapters in bf16, double quantization, and paged optimizers — enabling a 65B-class fine-tune on a single large GPU.

When it isn't enough: teaching genuinely new knowledge, a new language or new domain vocabulary; large behavioural shifts; very large datasets where full fine-tuning's extra capacity pays. Then continued pretraining or full FT. Also know the family moved on — DoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA+, VeRA.

SFT vs DPO vs GRPO vs PPO-RLHF, and what's your data strategy with 500 labels?

The 2026 stack: SFT on (prompt, response) pairs for format and instruction-following → preference optimisation (DPO, and its relatives KTO/SimPO/ORPO/IPO) for alignment → RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO, DAPO) for reasoning.

PPO-RLHF needs four models resident — policy, frozen reference, reward model and critic — roughly 280B parameters' worth for a 70B policy; the cost is engineering, not maths. DPO optimises directly on chosen/rejected pairs with an implicit reward: no reward model, no critic, two models instead of four, which is why it's the offline reference point. GRPO samples a group of responses per prompt and computes advantages within the group, so the group mean replaces the value critic — matching or beating PPO with less memory, and with verifiable rewards (a unit test passes, a math answer checks) this is where the 2025–26 reasoning gains came from.

With 500 labels: the LLM-as-teacher loop — prompt a strong model to label unlabelled data, human-verify a stratified sample, fine-tune a small encoder, and evaluate on a human-labelled test set only (never on teacher labels, or you're measuring agreement rather than correctness). Supplement with back-translation and LLM paraphrase (EDA-style synonym swaps are weak), weak supervision via label functions, active learning on uncertain cases, and a Cleanlab-style noise audit — because a 3% label error rate caps your achievable F1 regardless of the model.

6.21 Long context and interpretability

Two frontier areas where the useful skill is knowing the honest limits.

6.22 Interview focus for Part 6

Ten questions across tasks, evaluation and production — the areas where interviews are most often lost.

Tasks, evaluation & production · 10 questions
How do you classify a 50-page document with a 512-token model?

Four options, and I'd measure rather than assume. Truncate — trivially cheap and often fine, because many documents front-load their signal; the essential discipline is to log the truncation rate. Head + tail (first 256 + last 256) — a strong cheap trick for reports and emails where the conclusion is at the end. Chunk and pool — encode chunks and mean/max/attention-pool, using the whole document at k× the compute. A long-context encoder — ModernBERT gives 8k tokens in one pass, which is the 2026 default.

I'd also ask whether the label depends on the whole document at all. Often it depends on one section, in which case retrieve-then-classify beats all four.

⭐ NER for a brand-new entity type with zero labels — what do you build this week?

Day 1–2: GLiNER. It's a compact bidirectional encoder that matches entity-type descriptions against candidate spans, giving genuine open-vocabulary zero-shot NER on CPU — reported to beat both ChatGPT and NER-fine-tuned LLMs zero-shot, with domain variants like GLiNER-BioMed. That's a working system with no labels.

Day 3–5: the teacher loop. Run GLiNER (or an LLM) over unlabelled data, human-verify a stratified sample, fine-tune an encoder on the result, and evaluate on a human-labelled test set only. Add a rules/gazetteer precision layer on top for tickers, IDs and controlled vocabularies. Metric: entity-level F1 via seqeval, per entity type, with the error taxonomy split into boundary / type / spurious / missed — boundary errors usually dominate and respond to a CRF head or a span formulation rather than a bigger model.

Extractive vs abstractive summarization — and how do you catch hallucinations?

Extractive selects sentences: faithful by construction, since every word came from the source, but choppy and redundant. Abstractive generates: fluent and genuinely compressive, but it can state things the source never said. That trade-off is the answer.

Catching hallucinations: ROUGE cannot — it measures n-gram overlap with a reference, so a fluent, invented summary can score well. Use an explicit faithfulness metric: NLI-based (does the source entail each summary sentence? SummaC-style) or QA-based (generate questions from the summary, answer them from the source, compare — QAGS/QuestEval), plus a rubric-bound LLM judge and a human spot-check. For long inputs: map-reduce, refine, hierarchical, or retrieve-then-summarize.

⭐ Micro vs macro F1, and why is token accuracy the wrong NER metric?

Micro pools TP/FP/FN across classes and is dominated by the majority class; macro averages per-class F1 equally. Report macro plus per-class when the rare class is the point — a model can simultaneously show micro-F1 0.94 and macro-F1 0.60. The tell: in single-label multi-class, micro-F1 equals accuracy.

NER: token accuracy is inflated by the dominant O class — on a corpus that is 90% O, predicting O everywhere scores 90%. Use entity-level F1 with exact boundary and type matching via seqeval, broken out per type. And re-aggregate sub-token predictions into word-level spans first, or you're scoring a different task.

BLEU vs ROUGE vs chrF vs BERTScore vs COMET — what do you report?

BLEU is modified n-gram precision with a brevity penalty, corpus-level, translation-oriented — use sacreBLEU with its signature. ROUGE is recall-oriented overlap (ROUGE-1/2 n-grams, ROUGE-L longest common subsequence) and is the summarization convention. METEOR adds stems and synonyms with a fragmentation penalty. chrF is a character n-gram F-score, robust to morphology. BERTScore greedily aligns token embeddings. BLEURT and COMET are learned — COMET is trained on WMT human direct assessments over XLM-R and correlates far better with humans than any n-gram metric.

Per task: translation → chrF + COMET (+ sacreBLEU for comparability); summarization → ROUGE-1/2/L + BERTScore + an explicit faithfulness metric; open-ended/dialogue → surface metrics are near-useless, so rubric-bound LLM judging plus a human-graded anchor set.

⭐ Design an LLM-as-judge evaluation. Name three biases and your control for each.

Design: an explicit rubric with named criteria, a fixed scale with anchor descriptions, a required justification before the score, and pairwise comparison wherever possible — pairwise is materially more reliable than absolute scoring.

Biases and controls: position/order bias → randomise order and average both directions. Verbosity bias → control for length explicitly, or compare at matched lengths. Self-preference (judges favour their own model family) → use a different family as judge. Add language-switching and formatting bias, and sensitivity to the rubric wording.

What makes it evidence: calibrate against a human-labelled slice, report judge–human agreement (κ), and meta-evaluate the judge. Then combine: deterministic metrics for fast regression, judge for open-ended dimensions, human-graded anchor set validating both.

Your model was 0.89 F1 offline and users are complaining. Walk me through the debug.

I'd assume the evaluation is wrong before assuming the model is. In order: (1) leakage — were near-duplicates deduplicated before the split, and was the split grouped by document/user/time? (2) Slices — recompute per class, per language, per length bucket, per customer segment; a 0.89 aggregate can hide 0.3 on the class users care about. (3) Distribution shift — compare production inputs to the test set (length, language mix, vocabulary, OOV rate, truncation rate).

(4) The metric itself — is it the metric the decision needs? Macro vs micro, entity-level vs token-level, threshold set at 0.5 by default. (5) The pipeline around the model — a preprocessing mismatch between training and serving, truncation, a broken language router. (6) Label quality — audit 200 random labels; a 3% error rate caps the ceiling. (7) The complaint itself — collect the actual failing examples; “users are complaining” often means one visible, high-salience error class rather than a broad regression.

How do you detect drift when labels arrive 30 days late?

Distinguish three drifts. Input drift — measurable now with PSI/KL on token or embedding distributions, new-vocabulary rate, new-intent detection. Concept drift — the labels change meaning while the text distribution is unchanged, so input monitoring cannot see it. Performance drift — the thing you care about, arriving 30 days late.

So you need proxies: confidence-distribution shift, abstention rate, unknown/OOV token rate, prediction-mix shift, truncation rate and latency p95. And the strongest signals are downstream user behaviour — edit rate on suggested text, escalation rate, override rate, thumbs-down, time-to-resolution — which arrive in hours and correlate with the real metric. Wrap it in shadow deploys, canaries, a golden-set CI regression suite, and a relabelling flywheel.

Cut the NLP inference bill 10× without losing accuracy.

In this order, measuring at each step. (1) A tiered cascade — rules for the trivially-classifiable, a small encoder for the confident majority, the large model only for the escalated tail, humans for the last 2%. If 90% of traffic never touches the expensive model, average cost drops by roughly an order of magnitude with tail quality preserved. Escalation must be driven by calibrated uncertainty, not a raw softmax score.

(2) Distil the large model's behaviour into the small encoder using it as a teacher. (3) Quantize (~4-bit floor) so the small model runs on CPU. (4) Batch properly — continuous batching, length bucketing, unpadding; and export to ONNX/TensorRT. (5) Cache — exact-match first, then normalised-key, then a semantic cache with a similarity floor and TTL because of the false-hit risk. Then publish a table of macro-F1 vs p95 latency vs $/1M documents at each step, so the trade is visible rather than asserted.

How do you keep PII out of a training corpus and out of logs, and how do you make it reproducible?

PII: a detection pass combining NER, rules and validation (checksums for IDs, Luhn for cards) — rules give you precision on structured identifiers, the model gives recall on names and addresses. Then pseudonymise, with a reversible token vault only where re-identification is genuinely required. Apply it at ingest, before anything is persisted, and again on the logging path — trace redaction is a separate code path from training-data cleaning and both must exist. Add retention limits, data residency, and a training-data manifest, because you cannot un-train a model on request. Test for memorisation with canary strings.

Reproducibility: pin revision= to a commit SHA (never main), pin the tokenizer and library versions, seed everything, and record the exact preprocessing. Track experiments and dataset versions so you have lineage from a prediction back to a model and a data snapshot. Write NLP-specific tests: tokenizer round-trip, label-alignment invariants, schema validation of extracted output, CheckList-style invariance tests, and a no-network unit-test path with a tiny model.

7.1 Foundations & preprocessing

Eight questions. Answer out loud before opening the answer — reading answers you already half-know is the least efficient possible use of this page.

Q1–8 · foundations
1. Tokenization vs stemming vs lemmatization?

Tokenization splits text into units. Stemming chops affixes by rule — fast, no lexicon, output may not be a word (happiness → happi). Lemmatization maps to a dictionary lemma using POS and morphology — slower, always a real word (better → good). Modern half: tokenization always; the other two only for lexical features and morphologically rich languages, never before a transformer.

2. When would you not remove stopwords or lemmatize?

Sentiment and negation (“not good” collapses to “good”), phrase/exact-match search, POS tagging and parsing (function words are the syntax), and any transformer input — it deviates from the pretraining distribution. Better than a downloaded stopword list: max_df, which computes a domain-adapted list from your own corpus.

3. Why is a split('.') sentence splitter wrong?

Abbreviations (Dr., e.g., Inc.), decimals, ellipses, quoted speech and URLs all contain periods that don't end sentences — and some languages don't use them at all. It's a learned pipeline component in spaCy for exactly this reason.

4. What is Zipf's law and what does it imply?

Frequency ∝ 1/rank. Two consequences: a tiny head of function words carries ~half your tokens (so stopword lists are short and removal is cheap), and a vast tail means a fixed word-level vocabulary will miss things — the pressure that produced subword tokenization and smoothing.

5. Preprocessing for TF-IDF vs for BERT — why different?

For sparse lexical features you want to collapse surface variation: lowercase, strip punctuation, remove stopwords, lemmatize, placeholder numbers. Before a transformer you want minimal cleaning — fix encoding, strip boilerplate, normalise Unicode — because aggressive normalization creates a train/serve mismatch with the pretraining distribution and destroys casing (a real NER feature) and negation.

6. What is POS tagging for in a world where BERT exists?

Input to lemmatization; ABSA aspect-term candidates; linguistic filters for keyphrase extraction; features for high-precision rule extraction; negation and scope detection in clinical text. And quote the baseline: ~97% accuracy is only ~7 points above assigning each word its most frequent tag.

7. Constituency vs dependency parsing + one production use.

Constituency gives a phrase-structure tree (CKY over a PCFG; labelled bracket F1). Dependency gives labelled head→dependent arcs (transition-based arc-eager or graph-based biaffine; UAS/LAS). Production: dependency arcs for relation extraction and for linking an opinion word to its aspect target; both for negation-scope rules in clinical NLP where determinism matters.

8. What are BIO tags and what can't they express?

Begin/Inside/Outside per token, turning span-finding into token classification; the B/I distinction separates two adjacent entities from one two-word entity. BIOES adds End/Single. Cannot express nested entities, overlapping types on one token, or discontinuous spans — which is why span-based and generative extraction exist.

7.2 Representation & embeddings

Twenty-one questions covering sparse representation, topic models and the embedding family.

Q9–29 · representation
9. Why can't you one-hot encode words and be done?

One-hot vectors are mutually orthogonal, so cosine similarity between any two distinct words is exactly 0 — no similarity is encoded at all. Plus |V| ≈ 10⁵–10⁶ dimensions, no word order, and no representation for unseen words.

10. Explain BoW and its three limitations.

A document becomes a vector of counts over the vocabulary — a multiset. (1) No word order (“dog bites man” = “man bites dog”). (2) No semantics (“excellent” and “superb” are unrelated). (3) High-dimensional sparsity, plus vocabulary drift at serve time. Fourth for credit: raw counts weight ubiquitous and discriminative terms equally.

11. ⭐ Write down TF-IDF. Why does IDF help? Where does it break?

tf(t,d) × idf(t), idf = log(N/df) — sklearn's smoothed log((1+N)/(1+df)) + 1 — then L2-normalize per document so cosine equals dot product. IDF down-weights terms appearing everywhere and up-weights concentrated ones. Breaks on: no semantics (vocabulary mismatch → paraphrases score ~0), sparsity, unstable IDF on small corpora, huge weight for rare typos, and unbounded linear tf.

12. TF-IDF vs BM25 — what does BM25 add?

Term-frequency saturation via k₁ (the 20th occurrence adds far less than the 2nd) and document-length normalization via b (so long documents don't win by size). Plus a probabilistic-relevance derivation rather than assembled heuristics. It remains the lexical half of every serious hybrid retrieval system.

13. What is the hashing trick and what do you give up?

Hash terms into a fixed number of buckets: no vocabulary to fit or persist, constant memory, streaming-friendly. You give up collision-freedom and the inverse transform — so no interpretability and no coefficient inspection.

14. LSA vs LDA vs NMF?

LSA: truncated SVD — fast, deterministic, handles synonymy, components can be negative and aren't interpretable as topics. NMF: non-negative parts-based factorization, often the most readable topics, no probabilistic story. LDA: generative — documents are Dirichlet mixtures over topics (α), topics Dirichlet over words (β), inferred by collapsed Gibbs or variational EM.

15. How do you choose the number of topics, and why coherence over perplexity?

Sweep k and evaluate with topic coherence (C_v or UMass), not held-out perplexity — the well-known finding is that perplexity can anti-correlate with human interpretability. Also inspect stability across seeds; topic labelling is manual either way.

16. ⭐ Your LDA topics are garbage on tweets. What do you do?

LDA needs enough tokens per document to estimate a mixture, and tweets don't have them. Options: aggregate (pool by author, hashtag or time window), switch to a short-text model, or — the 2026 default — embeddings + UMAP + HDBSCAN + c-TF-IDF (BERTopic-style), which needs far less preprocessing, scores better on coherence, and can label a document as noise instead of forcing it into a topic.

17. State the distributional hypothesis in one sentence.

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth) — words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Every embedding method is an implementation of that sentence.

18. What is a word embedding and why was it a step change?

Dense, low-dimensional learned vectors where geometry encodes distributional similarity. The step change wasn't accuracy — it was that similarity became computable, and that structure could generalise between related words instead of every word being an isolated column.

19. ⭐ CBOW vs skip-gram — which for rare words?

Skip-gram, because it makes one prediction per (centre, context) pair, so a rare word receives several focused updates. CBOW averages several contexts into one prediction, which blurs the rare-word signal — but it's faster and better for frequent words on large corpora.

20. Explain negative sampling. How many negatives?

Replace the |V|-way softmax with k+1 binary logistic decisions: one true pair labelled 1, k sampled noise pairs labelled 0. k = 5–20 for small corpora, 2–5 for large. Noise distribution is unigram^(3/4), which flattens Zipf so rare words appear as negatives more than raw frequency implies. Frequent words are subsampled with P(discard)=1−√(t/f(w)). Alternative: hierarchical softmax, O(log|V|).

21. Word2Vec vs GloVe vs FastText for a typo-heavy multilingual corpus?

FastText — a word is a bag of character n-grams (3–6) plus the whole word, so typos and unseen morphology still compose a sensible vector, and it handles morphologically rich languages. Word2Vec predicts from local windows; GloVe factorizes a global co-occurrence matrix with a weighted least-squares objective; neither can embed an unseen word.

22. ⭐ How do you handle OOV words?

Two layers. Static embeddings: FastText composes from character n-grams. Anything modern: the question dissolves — subword and especially byte-level tokenizers can encode any string, so there are no OOVs, and <UNK> is an artefact of fixed word-level vocabularies. Giving only the first half sounds five years out of date; only the second half misses the question.

23. Why are “good” and “bad” close together?

Embeddings encode distributional similarity, and antonyms occur in nearly identical contexts (“the food was ___”). Consequences: you can't do sentiment by cosine to “good”, and a purely dense retriever can return the semantic opposite of a query.

24. Static vs contextual — what does contextual buy you?

Static gives one vector per word type, so “bank” is a single blended vector and polysemy is unrepresentable. Contextual gives one vector per occurrence — which makes word-sense disambiguation nearly free and NER/coreference tractable. Caveat: contextual spaces are anisotropic, so raw cosine between untuned BERT vectors is unreliable.

25. How do you turn word vectors into a sentence vector?

Mean pooling, TF-IDF-weighted mean, or SIF (smooth inverse frequency + remove the first principal component). All are surprisingly strong baselines and all lose to a trained sentence encoder, for one reason: they optimise nothing at the sentence level.

26. Why is raw BERT [CLS] a bad sentence embedding?

It was trained for NSP, not similarity — nothing in MLM makes cosine between two sentence vectors meaningful — and the space is anisotropic, so everything looks moderately similar. Fix: SBERT-style siamese/triplet contrastive fine-tuning with mean pooling.

27. ⭐ Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder?

Bi-encoder: encode query and document separately, compare with cosine — documents can be encoded offline, so ANN search runs in milliseconds over millions. Cross-encoder: concatenate the pair through one model for full token-level interaction — best accuracy but one forward pass per pair, so it reranks the top 50 and never searches. ColBERT-style late interaction is the middle ground: per-token vectors scored with MaxSim, most of the accuracy, a much larger index.

28. How do you evaluate an embedding model for your use case, given MTEB exists?

Use MTEB/MMTEB to build a shortlist, then re-rank on your own labelled set — leaderboards are overfit, the task mix isn't your domain, and they ignore licence, size and latency. Score candidates on Recall@k and nDCG on your queries, and on cost per million tokens, dimension, max sequence length and multilingual coverage. Treat any specific ranking as stale on sight.

29. What are Matryoshka embeddings for?

Trained so that truncated prefixes remain useful — you can cut a 1024-dim vector to 256 dims and keep most of the quality, trading accuracy for index size and query cost at serve time without re-embedding. Also know instruction prefixes (query:/passage:), normalising before cosine, and hard-negative mining as the biggest quality lever.

7.3 Sequence models

Q30–38 · RNN → attention
30. How does an RNN work, and what are its three real limitations?

h_t = tanh(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b) with weights shared across timesteps; trained by BPTT. Limitations: (1) vanishing/exploding gradients from the repeated W_h factor; (2) no parallelism over time — the property transformers exploit; (3) degraded long-range memory. Fourth in seq2seq: the fixed-size context bottleneck.

31. Vanishing vs exploding gradients — different causes?

Same cause (repeated multiplication by W_h), opposite regimes. Exploding is numerical and loud (NaN) → fix with gradient clipping by global norm. Vanishing is architectural and silent — training appears to work while only short-range structure is learned → fix with gating, orthogonal init, layer norm, skip connections.

32. ⭐ Walk through the LSTM gates and the vanishing-gradient fix.

Forget f_t, input i_t, candidate C̃_t, then C_t = f_t⊙C_{t-1} + i_t⊙C̃_t; output o_t and h_t = o_t⊙tanh(C_t). σ for gates (0–1 valve), tanh for content. The mechanism: the cell state is updated additively and gated elementwise, so the backward path along C is multiplied only by f_t — with the forget gate near 1, gradient magnitude survives hundreds of steps. That's the constant error carousel.

33. LSTM vs GRU — how do you choose?

GRU merges cell and hidden state and uses reset + update gates — ~25% fewer parameters, faster, often better with limited data. LSTM can edge ahead on very long sequences. Accuracy is comparable on most tasks, so choose by validation. “GRU is just a worse LSTM” is a trap answer.

34. When can you use a BiLSTM and when can't you?

Fine when the whole sequence is available before you predict: POS, NER, classification, extractive QA. Impossible for autoregressive generation (reading future tokens is label leakage) and for true streaming/incremental decoding.

35. What is the seq2seq bottleneck and how did attention fix it?

The encoder compresses the entire input into one fixed-size vector, so quality decays with input length — visible as a falling BLEU-vs-length curve. Attention lets the decoder read all encoder states, weighted per output step: c_t = Σ α_{t,i} h_i. Dynamic, content-based, length-independent access — and it parallelizes.

36. Bahdanau vs Luong attention.

Bahdanau is additive: score = vᵀtanh(W₁h_i + W₂s_t) — a small MLP, more parameters, tolerates different dimensionalities. Luong is multiplicative: score = s_tᵀWh_i — a matmul, faster on GPUs, and the direct ancestor of QKᵀ.

37. Teacher forcing and exposure bias?

Teacher forcing feeds gold prefixes during training. At inference the decoder consumes its own predictions, so an early error compounds into a distribution it never trained on — exposure bias. Scheduled sampling is a partial fix.

38. Would you ship an RNN in 2026?

Rarely for text. Legitimate cases: tiny models on embedded hardware, and strict streaming needing O(1) state rather than a growing KV cache. Then show range: the concepts came back — a fixed-size recurrent state with constant per-token inference cost is exactly what Mamba and the SSM family revived, and production models now interleave recurrence with attention.

7.4 Tokenization

Nine questions. The highest yield in this bank relative to how little most candidates prepare.

Q39–47 · subwords
39. ⭐ Explain BPE training, then encoding.

Training: split the corpus into characters; count adjacent pairs; merge the most frequent pair; append it to an ordered merge list; repeat to the target vocabulary size. Encoding: split into characters and replay the merges in learned order, greedily — deterministic. The merge order is the model.

40. BPE vs WordPiece vs Unigram — direction, criterion, users.

BPE and WordPiece merge upward; Unigram prunes downward from a large candidate set via EM + loss-based pruning. BPE merges by raw frequency; WordPiece by likelihood, roughly freq(AB)/(freq(A)·freq(B)), preferring informative over merely common merges; Unigram maximises corpus likelihood under a unigram LM and keeps a probability per piece, so it can sample segmentations. Users: GPT/Llama/RoBERTa (BPE), BERT/ELECTRA (WordPiece, ## marker), T5/ALBERT/XLNet and most multilingual models (Unigram via SentencePiece).

41. ⭐ What does SentencePiece actually do differently?

It's a framework, not an algorithm — it implements BPE and Unigram. Its contribution: consume raw text as a byte/character stream with whitespace encoded as ▁, so it needs no pre-tokenizer, is exactly reversible, and works on Chinese, Japanese and Thai. “SentencePiece vs BPE” is a category error.

42. Why byte-level BPE? What does it cost?

Operating on the 256 raw bytes means any Unicode string is representable — zero UNK, ever. The cost: non-Latin characters become several tokens each, so those languages pay a token premium in cost, latency and effective context.

43. How do you pick a vocabulary size?

Measure, don't guess: fertility (tokens per word) and bytes-per-token on your corpus, plus per-language token premium. Small vocab → longer sequences → more quadratic attention cost and worse compression. Large vocab → huge embedding/output matrices that can dominate a small model's parameters, and undertrained rare tokens. Typical 30k–50k (BERT era) → 32k–130k+ (multilingual/code).

44. ⭐ Why can't a model count the r's in “strawberry”?

Because it never sees characters — “strawberry” is two or three opaque subword tokens. Same root cause for string reversal, rhyming and acrostics. Related: inconsistent digit grouping is a genuine cause of arithmetic errors, which is why some tokenizers split digits individually.

45. Why is your Hindi app 3× the token cost?

Token premium. Under a byte-level BPE trained mostly on English, non-Latin scripts consume several tokens per character. Same content, more money, more latency, and a smaller effective context — for those users only. Measure with characters-per-token and fertility. Frame it as a cost, latency and fairness issue.

46. Your NER labels are misaligned after tokenization. What went wrong?

Word-level labels were applied positionally to sub-tokens. Fix with word_ids()/offset_mapping: label the first sub-token of each word and mask continuations (and special tokens) with -100. Mirror-image bug at eval time: re-aggregate sub-token predictions into word-level spans before entity-level F1. Neither raises an error.

47. What happens if you pair a model with a different tokenizer?

Silent garbage — token IDs map to unrelated embeddings, so the model produces confident nonsense with no error raised. The tokenizer must match the checkpoint, and both should be pinned to a commit SHA.

7.5 Transformers

Q48–60 · attention & the block
48. Name every component of a transformer block.

Multi-head self-attention · positional information (RoPE in modern models) · position-wise FFN (d_ff = 4·d_model, GELU/SwiGLU) · residual connections · normalization (pre-LN, often RMSNorm) · in decoders, causal masking; in encoder-decoders, cross-attention. Rule of thumb: ~12·d² parameters per block, most of them in the FFN.

49. ⭐ Write scaled dot-product attention. Why √d_k?

softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V. The dot product of d_k unit-variance terms has variance d_k, so large logits push softmax into saturation — and the gradient of a saturated softmax is near zero, stalling learning. Dividing by √d_k restores unit variance. Mentioning the gradient completes the answer.

50. What do Q, K and V mean concretely?

Query: “what am I looking for?” Key: “what do I offer?” Value: “what I contribute if selected.” Attention is content-based routing — each token pulls in a weighted mixture of the others' values.

51. Why multi-head instead of one big head?

Not capacity — d_model is split, so the cost is roughly the same. The gain is representation subspaces: heads specialise (previous-token/positional, syntactic dependency, coreference, delimiter). Nuance: many heads are redundant and prunable, which is evidence the benefit is relational diversity rather than parameter count.

52. ⭐ Positional encoding: sinusoidal → learned → relative → RoPE → ALiBi.

Self-attention is permutation-equivariant, so without positions “dog bites man” = “man bites dog”. Sinusoidal: parameter-free, relative offsets are linear functions of it, extrapolates somewhat. Learned absolute: cannot extrapolate past the trained maximum. Relative: attend on offsets — generalises across lengths. RoPE: rotate Q/K by a position-dependent angle so qᵢᵀkⱼ depends only on i−j — relative by construction, zero parameters, KV-cache-friendly, and extensible by rescaling frequencies (Position Interpolation → NTK-aware → YaRN → LongRoPE, surfaced as rope_scaling). ALiBi: a linear distance penalty on logits, no positional embeddings, strong extrapolation.

53. How does causal masking work, and why parallel training?

Set future logits to −∞ (lower-triangular mask) before softmax, so position t sees only ≤ t. Because causality is structural, you can compute the loss for every next-token prediction in one parallel forward pass — the property that made trillion-token pretraining viable. It applies at training time only; generation is inherently sequential.

54. Encoder self-attention vs decoder self-attention vs cross-attention.

Encoder self-attention: bidirectional, every token reads every token — best representations, cannot generate. Decoder self-attention: causal, sees only the past. Cross-attention: queries from the decoder, keys/values from the encoder — the decoder's read-port into the source.

55. Why LayerNorm and not BatchNorm? Pre-LN vs post-LN?

LayerNorm normalizes per token over features; BatchNorm needs stable batch statistics, which variable-length padded text doesn't provide, and its inference behaviour depends on running statistics. Post-LN (original) puts the norm on the residual path, rescaling gradients at every block — unstable at depth, needs LR warmup. Pre-LN puts it inside the branch, leaving a clean identity path; modern models use it, often with RMSNorm (no mean subtraction, no bias).

56. What does the FFN do?

A per-position two-layer MLP with d_ff = 4·d_model, applied identically at every position — a per-token feature transform, not a mixing operation. It holds most of a block's parameters. Activation evolution: ReLU → GELU → SwiGLU/GeGLU (gated, with a ⅔ width adjustment to hold the parameter count).

57. ⭐ Complexity of attention, and where does inference memory go?

Attention: O(n²·d) compute, O(n²) scores; FFN: O(n·d²) — for short sequences the FFN dominates, for long ones attention does. At inference, memory goes to the KV cache: 2·layers·heads_kv·d_head·seq·batch·bytes, linear in both sequence and batch — which is what actually caps context and concurrency.

58. ⭐ Explain the KV cache. Why is decoding memory-bandwidth-bound?

Past keys/values don't change, so you cache rather than recompute them. Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel — big matmuls, compute-bound, sets TTFT. Decode produces one token per pass, re-reading all weights and the whole cache for a single token — terrible arithmetic intensity, hence memory-bandwidth-bound, setting inter-token latency. This framing explains GQA/MLA, speculative decoding, continuous batching and paged attention in one go.

59. ⭐ Why isn't “just increase the context length” the answer?

Quadratic attention compute; a linearly-growing KV cache competing with weights for VRAM (capping batch size, throughput and cost); positions beyond the trained range needing RoPE rescaling; and effective context < advertised context — lost-in-the-middle and long-context degradation are measurable. A context window is a budget you allocate, not a bucket you fill.

60. What did transformers fix about RNNs — and what did they give up?

Fixed: parallelism over positions during training and constant path length between any two tokens (RNN: O(n)) — the latter is why long-range modelling improved so sharply. Gave up: linear scaling. Attention is O(n²) in length, which is what every technique in Part 5 tries to soften without losing the parallelism.

7.6 Pretraining & model families

Q61–71 · objectives & the zoo
61. ⭐ GPT vs BERT.

GPT: decoder-only, autoregressive next-token objective, unidirectional, generative, expensive at volume. BERT: encoder-only, masked LM, bidirectional, discriminative, cheap and fast. The objective determines the family, and the family determines what you can do — and the deployment economics.

62. Explain MLM, the 80/10/10 recipe and the [MASK] mismatch.

Mask ~15% of tokens and predict them from both directions. Of those, ~80% become [MASK], 10% a random token, 10% unchanged — because [MASK] never appears at fine-tune or inference time, so the model must not rely on seeing it. That gap is the pretrain/finetune mismatch.

63. What did RoBERTa change, and why drop NSP?

Same architecture, fixed recipe: much more data, longer training, larger batches, byte-level BPE, dynamic masking. NSP was dropped after being found too easy and unhelpful. The lesson: recipe mattered more than architecture.

64. How is T5 different from BERT and GPT?

Encoder-decoder trained with span corruption using sentinel tokens, and every task cast as text-to-text. BART is the sibling with five noising functions (token masking, deletion, infilling, sentence permutation, document rotation). Enc-dec is the natural shape when input and output are distinct sequences.

65. What is ELECTRA's trick?

Replaced-token detection: a small generator proposes replacements and a discriminator classifies every token as real or fake. MLM only learns from the 15% it masked; ELECTRA gets signal at all positions, so it reaches comparable quality with substantially less compute.

66. DistilBERT — what's distilled and what do you lose?

6 layers distilled from BERT-base using soft logits, embedding-cosine and MLM losses: ~40% smaller, ~60% faster, ~97% of GLUE. You lose a little accuracy on the hardest tasks and some capacity for further fine-tuning — the canonical distillation figure to quote.

67. What makes DeBERTa's attention “disentangled”?

It represents each token with separate content and relative-position vectors and computes attention from both, rather than adding position into the content embedding — plus an enhanced mask decoder. DeBERTaV3 is still a top fine-tuning baseline in 2026.

68. ⭐ ModernBERT or a prompted LLM for a 10M-doc/day classifier?

Fine-tuned encoder, with the arithmetic. At that volume an API call per document is a multi-million-dollar line item with uncontrollable latency; a 150M encoder serves in single-digit ms, runs on CPU quantized, and typically matches or beats a prompted general model on a narrow task. Path: LLM zero-shot labels → human-verify a stratified sample → fine-tune ModernBERT-base (8k context, RoPE, FlashAttention, alternating local/global attention, GeGLU, unpadding; ~2T tokens of text and code) → human-labelled test set → distil/quantize. Keep the LLM for the long tail. Honest caveat: benchmark DeBERTaV3 too — a published comparison found it competitive.

69. Explain transfer learning in NLP and why pretrain-then-finetune won.

Pretrain on huge unlabelled corpora with a self-supervised objective, then adapt to a task with a small labelled set. It won because the pretrained representation supplies syntax and semantics for free, so downstream tasks need orders of magnitude less labelled data and reach better accuracy — and one base model amortises across many tasks.

70. Scaling laws, Chinchilla, and why over-train a smaller model?

Kaplan showed power-law loss scaling, read as “spend on parameters”. Chinchilla corrected it: for a fixed compute budget scale parameters and data together, ≈20 tokens per parameter — earlier models were undertrained, not too small. The third position: Chinchilla optimises training compute, but if you serve the model, inference dominates lifetime cost, so you deliberately over-train a smaller model (the Llama strategy). Arithmetic: FLOPs ≈ 6ND; AdamW mixed precision ≈ 16 bytes/param.

71. What is the curse of multilinguality?

As you add languages to a fixed-capacity model, per-language quality eventually degrades because capacity is diluted across languages and scripts. Mitigations: more capacity, better-balanced data, language-specific adapters — and always report per-language results.

7.7 Efficiency & architecture

Q72–80 · making it cheap
72. ⭐ MHA vs MQA vs GQA vs MLA — and the 2026 default?

All attack the KV cache. MHA: one K/V per query head, largest cache. MQA: all heads share one K/V — big cut, some quality loss and training instability. GQA: one K/V per group — MHA-like quality at near-MQA speed, the industry default. MLA: compress K/V into a low-rank latent and cache that. One-liner: MQA/GQA share heads; MLA compresses them. Nuance: DeepSeek-V2's ablations had GQA looking worse than MHA on quality while MLA held up and could even beat it.

73. What does FlashAttention change? Is it an approximation?

No — exact. It's IO-aware: tile Q/K/V into SRAM-sized blocks, compute partial softmaxes with a running max/sum, never materialise the n×n matrix in HBM, and recompute tiles in the backward pass. Same maths, O(n) memory, large wall-clock win — and it made most approximate-attention research unnecessary.

74. Longformer / BigBird / Performer — why did they lose?

They approximate attention (sparse window+global, window+global+random with guarantees, low-rank/kernelized). They lost because FlashAttention + GQA + RoPE scaling made exact attention fast and long enough that an approximation's accuracy cost stopped being worth it. Survivors: sliding-window attention and ModernBERT's alternating local/global layers.

75. ⭐ Mamba/SSMs vs transformers — and why hybrid?

Attention keeps all past tokens and can recall any one exactly, at O(n²) with an unbounded cache. An SSM uses a selective linear recurrence: O(n) in length, O(1) state per step — but the past is summarised, so precise recall of an arbitrary earlier token is weaker (the associative-recall gap). Hence hybrids: a few attention layers among many SSM layers — Jamba (Transformer–Mamba–MoE), Hymba (parallel attention + SSM heads, SOTA sub-2B). “Attention made context accessible; SSMs make context cheap.”

76. What is MoE, and what does it not save?

N expert FFNs plus a router activating top-k (usually 1–2) per token: large total parameters, small active parameters, cheap FLOPs. It does not save memory — every expert must be VRAM-resident. Plus load-balancing auxiliary loss, expert capacity and token dropping, and expert-parallel networking complexity.

77. How does YaRN extend context, and why is it cheaper than retraining?

RoPE encodes position as an angle with a frequency per dimension-pair. YaRN rescales that frequency spectrum non-uniformly — preserving short-range behaviour while stretching long-range positions — so an 8k-trained model runs at 128k. It's cheaper because you only need a short adaptation run: roughly 10× fewer tokens and 2.5× fewer steps than plain Position Interpolation, versus full long-context pretraining.

78. Quantization vs distillation vs pruning — one per constraint.

Memory-bound → quantization (GPTQ/AWQ/GGUF; INT8/INT4/FP8; ~4-bit floor). Latency-bound → distillation and structured pruning (heads/layers/FFN width — unstructured needs sparse kernels to pay off). Throughput-bound → batching and caching (continuous batching, paged attention, prefix cache, GQA for a bigger batch). Accuracy-bound → none of these; that's a data problem.

79. Explain speculative decoding. Does it change the output distribution?

A small draft model proposes k tokens; the target verifies all k in one parallel forward pass and accepts the longest valid prefix. Lossless with respect to the target's distribution when implemented correctly. Nearly free because decode was memory-bandwidth-bound, so the extra FLOPs were idle anyway. Relatives: Medusa heads, n-gram/prompt-lookup decoding.

80. Beam search vs top-p vs greedy — per task.

Greedy/T≈0 for extraction, classification and code. Beam search (B≈4–5, length-normalized) for closed-ended seq2seq like MT. Top-p 0.9 with T 0.7–1.0 for open-ended generation, because beam search there produces bland repetitive text. Mechanism: temperature scales logits (variance, not truthfulness); top-k is mis-sized when confidence varies; top-p adapts; min-p keeps a floor relative to the top token.

7.8 Fine-tuning & adaptation

Q81–89 · adaptation
81. ⭐ Walk the adaptation ladder. Where do you stop?

Zero-shot → few-shot/ICL → retrieval → feature extraction (freeze, train a head) → PEFT/LoRA → full FT → continued pretraining → from scratch. Stop at the first rung that hits your target metric, and say why. Hundreds of labels → feature extraction. 1k–100k on one GPU → LoRA/QLoRA. If the gap is knowledge rather than behaviour → retrieval, and climbing won't help.

82. Explain LoRA. What are r and alpha?

Freeze W, learn ΔW = BA of rank r, applied as W′ = W + (α/r)BA. r = 4–64 (capacity); alpha scales the update so effective LR ∝ α/r; target the q/k/v/o projections and often the MLP. Trainable parameters drop 100–10,000×. Two decisive properties: you can merge at inference (zero latency penalty) and hot-swap adapters over one base.

83. QLoRA — the four ingredients.

(1) frozen base quantized to 4-bit NF4; (2) LoRA adapters trained in bf16; (3) double quantization of the quantization constants; (4) paged optimizers to survive memory spikes. Result: a 65B-class fine-tune on a single large GPU.

84. When is LoRA not enough?

Teaching genuinely new knowledge, a new language or new domain vocabulary; large behavioural shifts; very large training sets where full FT's extra capacity pays. Then continued pretraining or full fine-tuning. Also know the family moved on: DoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA+, VeRA.

85. ⭐ Fine-tune or RAG for 40,000 support articles?

Retrieval for the knowledge, a light fine-tune for the voice and output contract. Fine-tuning teaches form, style and task behaviour; it does not reliably inject facts, and facts that change, need citations, or are access-controlled must live in a retrieval layer. “Fine-tune so it knows our docs” is expensive, immediately stale, uncitable and hallucination-prone.

86. What is catastrophic forgetting and how do you mitigate it?

Training on a new task overwrites previously learned capability. Mitigations: lower LR, fewer epochs, replay general data alongside task data, EWC-style regularisation, or adapter isolation — LoRA forgets less because the base weights never move. Evaluation consequence: your eval set must include the original capability, not just the new task.

87. Your BERT fine-tune swings 3 F1 points across seeds. Diagnose it.

Small-data fine-tuning instability — a known failure mode. Fixes: report mean ± std over ≥3 seeds, longer warmup, re-initialise the top layers, more epochs with early stopping on a dev set, lower LR, layer-wise LR decay. Also check for leakage and label noise. A single-seed number on 500 examples is not evidence.

88. SFT vs DPO vs GRPO vs PPO-RLHF.

SFT on (prompt, response) pairs → format and instruction-following. PPO-RLHF: reward model + KL-regularised policy optimisation, requiring four models resident (policy, frozen ref, reward, critic) — the cost is engineering. DPO: optimise directly on chosen/rejected pairs with an implicit reward — no reward model, no critic, two models; the offline reference point (relatives: KTO, SimPO, ORPO, IPO). GRPO: sample a group per prompt and compute advantages within the group, so the group mean replaces the critic — and with verifiable rewards (RLVR: unit tests, math checkers) this is where the reasoning gains came from.

89. You have 500 labels and need 10,000. Walk me through it.

LLM-as-teacher loop: prompt a strong model to label unlabelled data → human-verify a stratified sample → fine-tune a small encoder → evaluate on a human-labelled test set only. Supplement with back-translation and LLM paraphrase (EDA-style synonym swaps are weak), weak supervision via label functions + a label model, active learning on uncertain cases, and a Cleanlab-style noise audit — a 3% label error rate caps your achievable F1 regardless of model.

7.9 Tasks

Q90–97 · the task catalogue
90. Multi-label ticket classifier with heavy imbalance?

Sigmoid per label, not softmax, with per-label thresholds tuned on a dev set to a business precision/recall target. Class weights or focal loss for imbalance; report per-label F1 plus macro. Add abstention routing for low-confidence tickets, and calibrate before thresholding. Baseline first: TF-IDF + one-vs-rest logistic regression.

91. Classifying a 50-page document with a 512-token model?

Truncate (log the truncation rate), head+tail (256+256 — strong for reports where the conclusion is last), chunk-and-pool (whole document at k× compute), or a long-context encoder (ModernBERT, 8k). Also ask whether the label depends on one section — then retrieve-then-classify beats all four.

92. ⭐ NER for a new entity type with zero labels — this week?

GLiNER first: a compact bidirectional encoder matching entity-type descriptions against candidate spans — genuine zero-shot open-vocabulary NER on CPU, reported to beat ChatGPT and NER-tuned LLMs zero-shot (domain variants like GLiNER-BioMed exist). Then the teacher loop into a fine-tuned encoder, plus a rules/gazetteer precision layer. Metric: entity-level F1 via seqeval, per type, with errors split into boundary/type/spurious/missed.

93. Extractive vs abstractive summarization, and catching hallucinations?

Extractive selects sentences — faithful by construction, choppy. Abstractive generates — fluent and compressive, can invent. ROUGE cannot see a hallucination, so add an explicit faithfulness metric: NLI-based entailment (SummaC-style) or QA-based (QAGS/QuestEval), plus a rubric judge and human spot-checks. Long inputs: map-reduce, refine, hierarchical, retrieve-then-summarize.

94. How do you build ABSA? Where does it break?

Aspect-term extraction (span or token classification, POS/dependency features help) then aspect-sentiment pairing, often jointly. Breaks on negation and scope, sarcasm, comparatives (positive about the delta, not the product), and domain-specific polarity — “cheap” is good for hotels and bad for electronics, so no universal lexicon works.

95. Text-to-SQL: architecture and metric?

Schema linking is the hard part — map question phrases to tables/columns, with retrieval over the schema for large databases. Generate SQL with constrained decoding, then validate by parsing and dry-running. Metric: execution accuracy (does it return the right rows?), not string match. Guardrails: read-only credentials, a query allow-list, and human confirmation for anything destructive.

96. Zero-shot cross-lingual: train English, serve Hindi. What do you check?

Choose the strategy explicitly — zero-shot transfer (XLM-R), translate-train, or translate-test — they fail differently. Then check: per-language metrics (never only the macro average), tokenizer fertility and token premium for the target script, script/transliteration handling, code-switching, and a human-labelled target-language test set even if training data is English-only.

97. Semantic search vs keyword search — when is keyword still better?

For exact identifiers, product codes, part numbers, names, and rare terms the embedding never saw. Dense retrieval generalises but blurs exactness; BM25 nails literal matches. That's why hybrid lexical + dense is the production default rather than pure dense.

7.10 Evaluation

Q98–108 · measurement
98. ⭐ Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report?

Macro plus per-class, when the rare class is the point — micro is dominated by the majority class, so a model can show micro 0.94 and macro 0.60. The tell: in single-label multi-class, micro-F1 equals accuracy, so quoting both as independent evidence signals unfamiliarity. Also: PR-AUC over ROC-AUC for rare positives.

99. Why is token-level accuracy the wrong NER metric?

The O class dominates — on a corpus that's 90% O, predicting O everywhere scores 90%. Use entity-level P/R/F1 with exact boundary and type match (seqeval), per type, after re-aggregating sub-token predictions into spans.

100. Explain perplexity, and two reasons lower can be worse.

exp(−(1/N)Σ log P(w_i|context)) = 2^(cross-entropy in bits) = an effective branching factor. (1) Not comparable across tokenizers — a larger vocabulary yields more, individually easier tokens and lower perplexity for free. (2) It measures fit to a text distribution, not helpfulness, factuality, instruction-following or safety. Bonus: it doesn't apply in the usual sense to masked LMs.

101. ⭐ Why can't you compare perplexity across tokenizers?

Perplexity is per token, and tokenization defines what a token is. Split text into more, smaller, more-predictable units and per-token uncertainty falls without the model being better. Normalize to a tokenizer-independent unit — bits per byte (or per character) — to compare fairly.

102. BLEU vs ROUGE vs METEOR vs chrF — what does each measure?

BLEU: modified n-gram precision + brevity penalty, corpus-level, MT-oriented (use sacreBLEU with its signature). ROUGE: recall-oriented overlap — ROUGE-1/2 n-grams, ROUGE-L longest common subsequence; the summarization convention. METEOR: unigram matching with stems/synonyms plus a fragmentation penalty. chrF/chrF++: character n-gram F-score, robust to morphology.

103. Why is BLEU bad for summarization and terrible for dialogue?

It scores n-gram precision against a reference. Summarization legitimately paraphrases, so precision punishes good output; open-ended dialogue has no single correct reference at all, so the metric is measuring the wrong construct rather than measuring it imprecisely.

104. BERTScore vs COMET vs BLEURT.

BERTScore: greedy alignment of contextual token embeddings → P/R/F1; tolerant of paraphrase, needs a good backbone and rescaling to be interpretable. BLEURT: a regression model trained on human ratings. COMET: takes source + hypothesis (+ optional reference), trained on WMT human direct assessments over XLM-R — the reference-based state of the art for MT and far better human-correlated than any n-gram metric.

105. ⭐ Design an LLM-as-judge evaluation with bias controls.

Explicit rubric with named criteria, a fixed anchored scale, a required justification before the score, and pairwise comparison where possible. Biases and controls: position → randomise order and average both directions; verbosity → control for length or compare at matched lengths; self-preference → judge with a different model family. Also language-switching and formatting bias. Then calibrate against a human-labelled slice, report judge–human κ, and meta-evaluate the judge.

106. Your model is 0.4 F1 better. Is it better?

Unknown until tested. Run bootstrap resampling or a paired permutation test; on a 1,000-row test set a 0.4-point difference is comfortably inside the noise, and seed variance alone can exceed it. Also confirm it holds on the slices that matter, not just the aggregate.

107. Agreement is 0.55 κ and the model scores 0.72 F1. Meaning?

Something is wrong. Human agreement is a ceiling on measurable quality, so exceeding it usually means the model has learned one annotator's idiosyncrasy, or the test set is measuring noise, or there's leakage. Low κ means the task is underspecified — rewrite the guideline, pilot, adjudicate, re-measure. Where disagreement is legitimate (toxicity, humour), keep soft labels instead of forcing a majority vote.

108. How do you detect benchmark contamination?

N-gram overlap between the benchmark and your training corpus; canary strings planted in your data; and a performance gap between the public benchmark and a freshly-collected private set. Practical conclusion: a private held-out eval set is a competitive asset. Related: GLUE/SuperGLUE are largely saturated, and MTEB is heavily overfit.

7.11 Production

Q109–115 · shipping it
109. Design real-time intent classification at 5k QPS, p95 < 100 ms.

Cheap filter (rules/regex) → language ID and routing → a distilled, quantized encoder on CPU (5 ms) handling the confident majority → escalate calibrated-uncertain cases to a larger model → OOD/“other” detection so new intents surface rather than being mis-assigned. Length bucketing and dynamic batching; ONNX Runtime; exact-match then normalised-key cache. Metrics: macro-F1 plus coverage at target precision, p95/p99 latency, and the escalation rate as a drift signal. Every escalated case is training data.

110. ⭐ 0.89 F1 offline, users complaining. Debug it.

Assume the evaluation is wrong first. (1) Leakage — was dedup done before splitting, and was the split grouped by document/user/time? (2) Slices — per class, language, length bucket, segment; 0.89 aggregate can hide 0.3 on the class users care about. (3) Distribution shift between production inputs and the test set (length, language mix, OOV rate, truncation rate). (4) Is the metric the one the decision needs (macro vs micro, entity- vs token-level, default 0.5 threshold)? (5) Pipeline mismatch between training and serving preprocessing. (6) Label quality — audit 200 labels. (7) Collect the actual failing examples; “users are complaining” is often one salient error class.

111. How do you detect drift when labels arrive 30 days late?

Separate input drift (PSI/KL on token or embedding distributions, new-vocabulary rate) from concept drift (labels change meaning while the text distribution doesn't — invisible to input monitoring) from performance drift. Use proxies: confidence-distribution shift, abstention rate, OOV rate, prediction-mix shift, truncation rate, latency p95 — and above all user behaviour: edit rate, escalation rate, override rate, thumbs-down, time-to-resolution. Wrap with shadow deploys, canaries and a golden-set CI suite.

112. Cut inference cost 10× without losing 1 point of F1.

In order, measuring each step: (1) tiered cascade — rules, small encoder, large model, human, with escalation on calibrated uncertainty; if 90% of traffic never touches the expensive model, average cost falls ~10× with tail quality preserved. (2) Distil the large model into the small one. (3) Quantize to ~4-bit so it runs on CPU. (4) Batch — continuous batching, length bucketing, unpadding, ONNX/TensorRT. (5) Cache — exact, then normalised-key, then semantic with a similarity floor and TTL. Publish macro-F1 vs p95 vs $/1M docs at each step.

113. How do you keep PII out of prompts, training data and logs?

A detection pass combining NER (recall on names/addresses), rules and validation checksums (precision on structured IDs), applied at ingest before anything is persisted, and again on the logging path — trace redaction is a separate code path from training-data cleaning and both must exist. Pseudonymise with a reversible vault only where re-identification is required. Add retention limits, data residency, a training-data manifest (you cannot un-train on request), and canary tests for memorisation.

114. How do you make an NLP pipeline reproducible?

Pin revision= to a commit SHA (not main), pin the tokenizer and library versions (transformers v5 was a breaking major), seed everything, and record the exact preprocessing. Track experiments and dataset versions so you have lineage from prediction → model → data snapshot. Tests: tokenizer round-trip, label-alignment invariants, extracted-output schema validation, CheckList-style invariance, and a no-network path with a tiny model.

115. Build vs API for a text classifier — where's the crossover?

Write the arithmetic down. API cost = volume × price/doc; build cost = one-off labelling + training + a fixed serving footprint + maintenance. The crossover is usually low — often tens of thousands of documents per day — because a distilled encoder on CPU is nearly free per document. Non-cost factors that can decide it either way: latency SLA, data residency and privacy, the need to iterate on a schema, and whether the task is narrow (favours build) or open-ended (favours API). Then write the decision and its crossover volume down, so it can be revisited rather than relitigated.

“More data always helps.”AgreesNot with a 3% label error rate, near-duplicate leakage between train and test, or a drifted distribution. Clean, dedup and slice first — a smaller correct dataset usually wins.
“Our multilingual model is fine — macro average is 0.85.”AgreesReport per language. Non-Latin scripts pay a token premium, get less pretraining data, and usually carry most of the error.

Gotchas cheat sheet

The tokenizer must match the checkpoint — mismatch is silent garbage. Sub-token label alignment is the #1 NER bug (word_ids() + -100). Raw [CLS] is not a sentence embedding. Embedding models often need instruction prefixes (query:/passage:). Normalize before cosine. max_length truncation is silent — log the rate. Padding must be masked in pooling, loss and metrics. Micro-F1 == accuracy in single-label multi-class. Perplexity isn't comparable across tokenizers. Near-duplicate leakage inflates everything — dedup before splitting. Random splits are wrong for grouped or temporal data. Don't lemmatize or strip stopwords before a transformer. Beam search is bland for open-ended generation. temperature=0 is near-deterministic, not reproducible. Attention weights are not explanations. Pin revision= to a commit SHA, not main. use_fast and the TF/Flax paths are gone in transformers v5. Report per-language and per-class, never only the macro average. Label noise caps your F1 — audit 200 random labels before blaming the model.
