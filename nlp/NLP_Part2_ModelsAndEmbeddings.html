Part 2 of 7 · Models, LMs & Embeddings

Counting, predicting, and the first real vectors Naive Bayes to CRFs, n-gram language models with smoothing and perplexity, decoding strategies, and the embedding revolution — Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText — up to the static/contextual pivot.

Three things live here that interviewers return to constantly. Sequence models before neural nets — HMM, Viterbi and CRF — because “HMM vs CRF” is a clean test of generative vs discriminative thinking, and because a CRF head on top of BERT still helps. Perplexity, which almost everyone can define and almost nobody can criticise (it is not comparable across tokenizers). And Word2Vec, where the real question is never “what is an embedding” but CBOW vs skip-gram, negative sampling, and why “good” and “bad” end up close together. We finish on the pivot that sets up the rest of the subject: one vector per word versus one vector per word in context.

2.1 Naive Bayes

The baseline that refuses to die, and a small paradox interviewers enjoy: its central assumption is false, and it works anyway.

Naive Bayes applies Bayes' rule with a conditional independence assumption: given the class, every feature is independent of every other. For text that is plainly wrong — “New” and “York” are not independent — yet the model remains competitive on small datasets and takes milliseconds to train.

VariantModelsUse when
Multinomial NBWord countsThe default for text classification
Bernoulli NBWord presence/absenceShort texts; presence matters more than frequency
Complement NBCounts, from the complement classImbalanced text data — usually beats Multinomial there
Why it works despite violated assumptions

Classification only needs the argmax to be correct, not the probabilities. Dependence between features distorts the magnitude of the posterior badly, but often preserves its ranking — so the decision survives even though the confidence is meaningless. Two consequences you should volunteer: Naive Bayes probabilities are famously uncalibrated (never threshold them directly), and you need Laplace/Lidstone smoothing plus log-space computation to avoid zero probabilities and floating-point underflow.

2.2 Logistic regression and linear SVM

The baseline you should actually ship first — and the reason is not accuracy, it's that you can read it.

On TF-IDF features, logistic regression is the honest production baseline: convex, fast, calibratable, and interpretable via its coefficients. Linear SVM (hinge loss, maximum margin) is its close cousin and often marginally better on very high-dimensional sparse text. Non-linear kernels rarely help here — in ~50,000 dimensions the data is already close to linearly separable.

KnobEffectWhen
penalty='l2'Shrinks all weightsDefault
penalty='l1'Drives weights to exactly zeroYou want feature selection / a compact model
class_weight='balanced'Re-weights the loss by inverse class frequencyImbalanced data — almost always for text
Platt / isotonic calibrationTurns scores into usable probabilitiesYou threshold the output for a business rule
The interpretability argument

Print the top positive and negative coefficients per class. You will find label leakage, annotation artefacts and spurious correlations in minutes — a spam classifier keyed on the mail-server hostname, a sentiment model keyed on a product name. That debugging loop is far harder with a transformer, which is why this baseline is a diagnostic tool and not just a benchmark.

2.3 HMM and Viterbi ⭐

The pre-neural way to tag a sequence, and a permanent interview fixture — because if you can explain Viterbi you can explain dynamic programming, and if you can explain an HMM you can explain generative modelling.

An HMM models a sequence of hidden states (tags) emitting observations (words), with three parameter sets: π (initial state distribution), A (state-to-state transitions) and B (state-to-observation emissions). Three canonical problems: likelihood (forward algorithm), decoding (Viterbi), learning (Baum-Welch / EM).

2.4 CRF, label bias, and why a CRF head still helps ⭐

“HMM vs CRF” is one of the cleanest generative-vs-discriminative questions in NLP. There is a crisp answer; learn it verbatim.

Say this verbatim

“HMM models P(x,y) — generative, locally normalized, restrictive independence assumptions, so it can't use overlapping features. CRF models P(y|x) — discriminative, globally normalized over the whole sequence, arbitrary overlapping features, and immune to the label-bias problem that MEMMs have. CRF wins when you have labelled data; HMM needs less of it.”

2.5 Feature engineering — the lost art that still ships

Not nostalgia. This is what your precision layer and your fallback path are made of, and it is how you debug a model that “just knows”.

Feature familyExamplesStill used for
LexicalWord and character n-gramsEvery sparse baseline
ShapeXxxx, dd/dd/dddd, ALLCAPS, has-digitNER, document AI, ID detection
AffixesFirst/last 3 charactersMorphology proxy for unseen words
GazetteerMembership in a curated listHigh-precision entity layer
SyntacticPOS tag, dependency label, head wordRelation extraction, ABSA
Positional / windowPosition in document, ±2 token contextSequence labelling
The one to remember

Character n-grams (3–6) are the cheapest robustness win in NLP: tolerant of typos, useful for morphologically rich languages, script-agnostic, and bounded in vocabulary. If a text pipeline is failing on noisy user input, adding character n-grams is the first thing to try — and it's the same idea FastText later formalised.

2.6 Classification metrics you must not fumble

The single most common way to lose an otherwise-good interview: reporting a number that cannot support the claim you just made.

The tell

In single-label multi-class classification, micro-F1 is exactly equal to accuracy (every error is simultaneously one FP and one FN). Reporting both as if they were independent evidence is a well-known signal that someone is quoting classification_report without reading it.

2.7 What a language model actually is

A distribution over sequences. Everything else — generation, scoring, pretraining — follows from that one definition.

P(w₁…w_n) = Π P(w_i | w₁…w_{i-1}). Three flavours, and only one of them is a true samplable generator:

Uses beyond generation, worth naming: scoring and reranking (ASR and MT hypothesis rescoring), spelling correction, and — the one that changed everything — as a pretraining objective whose learned representations transfer to every downstream task.

2.8 N-gram language models

The Markov approximation, and the sparsity wall it runs into immediately — which is the entire motivation for smoothing.

Approximate the full history with the previous n−1 tokens: P(w_i|w_{i-1}) = C(w_{i-1},w_i) / C(w_{i-1}) for a bigram. Add <s> and </s> boundary tokens, decide an <UNK> policy, and you have a working LM in twenty lines.

2.10 Perplexity and its three traps ⭐

Almost every candidate can define perplexity. Almost none can criticise it — which is exactly what the follow-up question asks.

2.11 Decoding strategies

How you turn a distribution into text. There is a right answer per task, and giving the task-dependent version is the whole point.

temperature=0 is not reproducible

It is near-deterministic. Batching changes floating-point reduction order, GPU kernels are non-deterministic, and MoE routing depends on which other requests share the batch. If you need byte-identical outputs for a test, cache the response — don't rely on temperature=0.

2.12 Word embeddings — the idea

Dense, low-dimensional, learned vectors where geometry encodes distributional similarity. The step change was not accuracy; it was that similarity became computable.

One-hot / BoWEmbedding
Dimensions|V| ≈ 10⁵–10⁶50–300 (later 384–4096)
DensitySparse, mostly zeroDense, all dimensions used
Similarity0 between any two distinct wordsMeaningful cosine
GeneralisationNone across wordsShares structure between similar words
InterpretabilityEach dimension is a wordDimensions are opaque
Why “good” and “bad” are close

“Similar” here means distributionally similar — appearing in the same contexts. Antonyms appear in almost identical contexts (“the food was ___”), so they land close together. This is the classic gotcha, and it explains why you cannot do sentiment analysis by cosine-similarity to the word “good”, and why an embedding-only retrieval system can return the semantic opposite of the query.

2.13 Word2Vec ⭐

Two architectures, two training tricks, and a set of details interviewers use to tell “I read the blog post” from “I understand the objective”.

Two more details worth knowing: Word2Vec learns two matrices (input/context), and the “embedding” is usually the input matrix (sometimes their sum). And the famous analogy king − man + woman ≈ queen is real but oversold — analogy benchmarks are partly an artefact of the evaluation protocol, and saying so is a mark of having read past the headline.

The count-based alternative, and the one that solved OOV for static embeddings.

The OOV answer, in two layers

Asked “how do you handle OOV words?”, give both halves. For static embeddings: FastText composes a vector from character n-grams, so an unseen word still gets a sensible representation. For anything modern: the question dissolves — subword and byte-level tokenizers mean there are no OOVs, and <UNK> is an artefact of fixed word-level vocabularies. Giving only the first half sounds five years out of date; giving only the second half misses the question.

2.15 Using and evaluating embeddings

Intrinsic evaluation is fast and only weakly predictive. Extrinsic evaluation is slow and the only one that decides anything.

KindHowWorth
IntrinsicCorrelation with human similarity judgments (WordSim-353, SimLex-999), analogy accuracy, cluster coherenceQuick sanity check; weak predictor of downstream gain
ExtrinsicSwap the embedding, measure your actual task metricThe only evidence that should change a decision

Composing sentence vectors from word vectors — mean pooling, TF-IDF-weighted mean, and SIF (smooth inverse frequency weighting followed by removing the first principal component). These are surprisingly strong baselines and all of them are beaten by a trained sentence encoder, for a reason worth stating: they optimise nothing at the sentence level.

Visualisation: PCA (linear, preserves global structure), t-SNE (local structure only — distances between clusters are not meaningful, and cluster sizes are not either), UMAP (better global structure, faster, still stochastic). Quoting the t-SNE caveat unprompted is a small but reliable credibility signal.

Train your own when the domain vocabulary is genuinely alien to public corpora: clinical notes, legal citations, e-commerce SKUs, log lines, ticker symbols. Otherwise start pretrained.

2.16 Bias in embeddings

Embeddings inherit and often amplify the associations in their corpus. In any regulated domain this is now a standard interview question, and there is a right way to frame the answer.

Measurement: WEAT / SEAT quantify differential association between attribute sets (e.g. gendered names) and target sets (e.g. occupations). Mitigation: hard-debiasing (project out a “gender direction”), INLP (iterative nullspace projection) — and the important finding that these often hide bias in the geometry rather than removing the information, which downstream classifiers can still recover.

The framing that lands

Measure bias at the decision level, not only in the embedding space. What matters is whether the deployed system produces different outcomes for comparable people — so audit per-subgroup metrics on the actual task, use counterfactual pairs (swap the name, swap the pronoun, keep everything else), and document it. Geometric debiasing is a mitigation, not evidence of fairness.

2.18 Interview focus for Part 2

Nine questions that between them cover this entire part. Answer out loud first.

Models, LMs & embeddings · 9 questions
Why does Naive Bayes work when its independence assumption is clearly false?

Classification needs the argmax to be right, not the probabilities. Feature dependence distorts the magnitude of the posterior severely but often preserves its ranking, so the decision survives. Two things to volunteer: NB probabilities are badly uncalibrated (never threshold them directly — calibrate first), and you need Laplace/Lidstone smoothing plus log-space arithmetic to avoid zeros and underflow. For imbalanced text, Complement NB usually beats Multinomial.

⭐ HMM vs CRF — give me the crisp version.

HMM models P(x, y) — generative, locally normalized, with restrictive independence assumptions (emissions independent given the state), so it cannot use arbitrary overlapping features. CRF models P(y | x) — discriminative, globally normalized over the entire sequence, accepts arbitrary overlapping features, and does not suffer the label-bias problem that MEMMs do (where a state with few outgoing transitions hoards probability mass because normalisation happens per step).

Trade-off: CRF wins when you have labelled data; HMM needs less of it. And the modern relevance: a CRF layer on top of BiLSTM or BERT still helps, because it learns a transition matrix that rules out structurally invalid tag sequences (I-PER directly after O) which a per-token softmax cannot.

Write Viterbi's recursion and state its complexity.

δ_t(j) = max_i [ δ_{t-1}(i) + log a_ij ] + log b_j(o_t), storing argmax_i as a backpointer at each cell, then walking the backpointers from the best final state to recover the sequence.

Complexity: O(T·|S|²) time, O(T·|S|) space. The key insight is that you keep only the single best path into each state at each timestep — that max is what collapses an exponential search into a table. Always compute in log-space: products of probabilities underflow within a few dozen tokens. (Swap max for logsumexp and you have the forward algorithm instead.)

Micro vs macro F1 — which do you report, and what's the tell?

Micro pools all TP/FP/FN across classes, so it is dominated by the majority class. Macro averages per-class F1 equally, so a rare class counts as much as a common one. Report macro (plus per-class) when the rare class is the point — which in NLP it usually is. A model can show micro-F1 0.94 and macro-F1 0.60 simultaneously.

The tell: in single-label multi-class classification, micro-F1 is exactly equal to accuracy (each error is one FP and one FN), so quoting both as separate evidence signals someone reading classification_report without understanding it. Also: PR-AUC over ROC-AUC for rare positives, and per-label thresholds for multi-label.

Explain perplexity, and give me two reasons a lower-perplexity model can be worse.

PPL = exp(−(1/N) Σ log P(w_i | context)) = 2^(cross-entropy in bits) = the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities. Interpret it as an effective branching factor: perplexity 20 means the model is as uncertain as choosing uniformly among 20 words at each step.

Reason one: perplexity is not comparable across tokenizers — a larger vocabulary splits text into more, individually-easier-to-predict tokens, which lowers perplexity for free. Normalise to bits-per-byte to compare. Reason two: it only measures fit to a text distribution, not helpfulness, factuality, instruction-following or safety — a model can be an excellent next-token predictor and a bad assistant. Bonus third: it does not apply in the usual sense to masked LMs like BERT.

Beam search vs top-p vs greedy — pick one per task and justify it.

Greedy / temperature ≈ 0 for extraction, classification and code — there is one right answer and you want reproducibility. Beam search (B≈4–5, length-normalized) for closed-ended seq2seq like MT and reference-based summarization, where you're searching for the single best sequence. Top-p ≈ 0.9 with temperature 0.7–1.0 for open-ended generation, because beam search there produces bland, repetitive text.

Mechanism worth adding: temperature scales logits before softmax (variance, not truthfulness); top-k takes a fixed candidate count and so is mis-sized whenever the model is very confident or very unsure; top-p adapts by taking the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches p; min-p keeps a floor relative to the top token and is more stable at high temperature.

⭐ CBOW vs skip-gram, and explain negative sampling.

CBOW predicts the centre word from the averaged context — faster, better for frequent words, but averaging blurs the signal for rare ones. Skip-gram predicts each context word from the centre word — one update per (centre, context) pair, so it is better for rare words and small corpora. That asymmetry is the answer.

Negative sampling replaces the |V|-way softmax (400k dot products per example) with k+1 binary logistic decisions: one true pair labelled 1, and k sampled noise pairs labelled 0. Details that earn credit: k = 5–20 for small corpora, 2–5 for large; the noise distribution is unigram^(3/4), which flattens Zipf so rare words appear as negatives more often than raw frequency implies; frequent words are subsampled with P(discard) = 1 − √(t/f(w)). The alternative is hierarchical softmax, an O(log|V|) Huffman tree.

How do you handle OOV words, and why are “good” and “bad” close together?

OOV, in two layers. For static embeddings: FastText represents a word as a bag of character n-grams (3–6) plus the whole word, so an unseen word still composes a sensible vector — which also makes it strong for morphologically rich languages and typos. For anything modern: the question dissolves, because subword and especially byte-level tokenizers can encode any string, so there are no OOVs and <UNK> is an artefact of fixed word-level vocabularies.

Good/bad: embeddings encode distributional similarity, and antonyms occur in nearly identical contexts (“the food was ___”). So they land close together. Consequences: you cannot do sentiment by cosine distance to “good”, and a purely dense retriever can return the semantic opposite of a query.

Static vs contextual embeddings — what exactly does contextual buy you?

Static gives one vector per word type, so “bank” receives a single vector blending river-bank and investment-bank; polysemy is unrepresentable by construction. Contextual gives one vector per occurrence, computed from the surrounding sentence — ELMo did it with a BiLSTM and task-weighted layer combination, then BERT with self-attention.

What it buys: word-sense disambiguation largely for free, tractable NER and coreference, and representations that transfer to almost any task by fine-tuning. One caveat to mention: contextual spaces are anisotropic — vectors cluster in a narrow cone — so raw cosine similarity between untuned BERT vectors is unreliable. You need normalisation, whitening, or (properly) a sentence-level training objective like SBERT's.
