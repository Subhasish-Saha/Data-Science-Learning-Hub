Part 5 of 7 · Efficiency & Fine-Tuning

Making it cheap, and making it yours MHA → MQA → GQA → MLA, FlashAttention, sparse attention, Mamba hybrids and MoE — then the adaptation ladder, LoRA and QLoRA, and the modern post-training stack.

Every question in this part is really the same question: which axis are you short on? Latency, memory, throughput or accuracy each have a different tool, and candidates who reach for the wrong one give themselves away. The architecture half traces how the field made attention affordable — MQA and GQA share K/V heads, MLA compresses them, FlashAttention changes nothing mathematically and everything practically, and SSMs like Mamba make context cheap rather than merely accessible (which is why 2026 production models are hybrids, not replacements). The fine-tuning half is the adaptation ladder: stop at the first rung that hits your target metric, know exactly what LoRA freezes and what QLoRA quantizes, and be able to say why fine-tuning does not inject facts.

Expect to explain the rank decomposition, name the hyperparameters, and say what QLoRA's four ingredients are.

Fine-tune vs RAG — the trap

Fine-tuning teaches form, style, task behaviour and domain idiom. Retrieval supplies facts. Facts that change, need citations, or are access-controlled belong in a retrieval layer — injecting them by fine-tuning is expensive, immediately stale, uncitable, and encourages confident hallucination. “Fine-tune the model so it knows our 40,000 support articles” is the wrong instinct; the right architecture is retrieval for the knowledge, a light fine-tune for the voice and the output contract.

Efficiency & fine-tuning · 9 questions
⭐ MHA vs MQA vs GQA vs MLA — what does each reduce, and what's the 2026 default?

All four attack the same bottleneck: the KV cache, which is what makes decoding memory-bandwidth-bound. MHA gives every query head its own K/V — largest cache. MQA shares a single K/V head across all query heads — a drastic cache cut, faster decode, but measurable quality loss and training instability. GQA groups query heads with one K/V head per group — MHA-like quality at near-MQA speed, and the industry default (Llama-2 70B onward, Mistral). MLA compresses K and V into a low-rank latent and caches that, decompressing on the fly (DeepSeek).

The one-liner: “MQA and GQA share heads; MLA compresses them.” And the nuance that earns the follow-up: DeepSeek-V2's ablations showed GQA looking worse than MHA on modelling quality while MLA held up and could even beat MHA when tuned — so compression is not obviously the inferior strategy.

What does FlashAttention change? Is it an approximation?

No — it is exact. That's the trap in the question. FlashAttention is an IO-aware algorithm: it tiles Q, K and V into blocks that fit in the GPU's fast on-chip SRAM, computes partial softmaxes with a running max and sum, and never materialises the n×n score matrix in HBM. The backward pass recomputes tiles rather than storing them.

Result: identical mathematics, O(n) memory instead of O(n²), and a large wall-clock speedup — because attention was bound by memory traffic, not arithmetic. Versions 2 and 3 improved parallelism and hardware utilisation. Its importance beyond speed: it made most approximate-attention research unnecessary, because exact attention became fast enough.

Longformer, BigBird, Performer — why did most of these lose?

They approximate attention to escape O(n²): sparse patterns (Longformer's sliding window + global tokens; BigBird adds random attention and comes with theoretical guarantees), low-rank/kernelized (Linformer, Performer's FAVOR+ giving O(n)), and segment recurrence (Transformer-XL).

Why they lost: FlashAttention + GQA + RoPE scaling made exact attention fast and long enough that an approximation's quality cost stopped being worth paying. What survived are patterns that keep exactness locally — sliding-window attention (Mistral) and ModernBERT's alternating local/global layers. Knowing why they lost is a better answer than listing them.

⭐ Mamba/SSMs vs transformers — the trade-off, and why are production models hybrid?

Attention keeps every past token and can recall any one of them exactly, at O(n²) compute with an unbounded KV cache. An SSM (Mamba, Mamba-2) uses a learned, input-dependent linear recurrence: O(n) in sequence length and O(1) state per step at inference. The cost is that the past is summarised into a fixed state, so precise recall of an arbitrary earlier token is weaker — the associative-recall gap.

Hence hybrids, not replacement: interleave a few attention layers among many SSM layers. Attention supplies high-resolution recall; SSM layers summarise context cheaply. Jamba (Transformer–Mamba–MoE) and Hymba (parallel attention + SSM heads, state of the art for sub-2B models) are the reference points. The framing to use: “attention made context accessible; SSMs make context cheap — production models buy both.”

What is a Mixture of Experts, and what does it not save?

Replace the FFN with N expert FFNs plus a router that activates the top k (usually 1–2) per token. So total parameters are large while active parameters per token stay small — cheap FLOPs, high capacity.

What it does not save: memory. Every expert must be resident in VRAM whether or not it fires, so an 8×7B MoE needs roughly 8×7B of weights loaded. Add expert-parallel networking complexity, a load-balancing auxiliary loss to stop the router collapsing onto a few experts, and expert-capacity limits that cause token dropping. Naming the FLOPs-vs-memory asymmetry is the point of the question. Variants: shared and fine-grained experts (DeepSeek-style).

Quantization vs distillation vs pruning vs speculative decoding — pick one per constraint.

Memory-bound → quantization. Post-training (GPTQ, AWQ, GGUF k-quants) or QAT; INT8/INT4/FP8; weight-only vs weight+activation. ~4-bit is the practical floor for quality-sensitive work.

Latency-bound → distillation (and structured pruning). Response/logit-based, feature-based, or task-specific LLM→encoder distillation. DistilBERT is the canonical result: ~40% smaller, ~60% faster, ~97% of GLUE. On pruning, distinguish unstructured (needs sparse kernels to pay off) from structured (drop heads/layers/FFN width — actually faster on real hardware).

Throughput-bound → batching and caching: continuous batching, paged attention, prefix caching, and GQA so a bigger batch fits. Speculative decoding also targets latency: a small draft model proposes k tokens and the target verifies them in one parallel pass — lossless w.r.t. the target distribution when done right, and nearly free because decode was bandwidth-bound anyway. Accuracy-bound → none of these help; that's a data and fine-tuning problem.

⭐ Walk the adaptation ladder. Where do you stop?

Zero-shot prompting → few-shot/ICL → retrieval augmentation → feature extraction (freeze the encoder, train a head) → PEFT/LoRA → full fine-tuning → continued/domain pretraining → pretrain from scratch. Cost, data appetite and iteration time rise at every rung.

Where to stop: the first rung that hits your target metric — and the reason you stopped is the answer. With a few hundred labels, feature extraction. With 1k–100k and a single GPU, LoRA or QLoRA. If the gap is knowledge rather than behaviour, retrieval — climbing further won't help. Pretraining from scratch is almost never right, and you should be able to show the FLOP arithmetic (≈6ND) that says so.

Explain LoRA and QLoRA. When is LoRA not enough?

LoRA: freeze W and learn a low-rank update ΔW = BA of rank r, applied as W′ = W + (α/r)·BA. Hyperparameters: r (4–64), alpha (scaling — effective LR ∝ α/r), target_modules (the q/k/v/o projections, often the MLP too), dropout. Trainable parameters drop 100–10,000×. Two properties made it universal: you can merge BA into W at inference so there's zero latency penalty, and you can hot-swap task adapters over one shared base.

QLoRA's four ingredients: a 4-bit NF4-quantized frozen base, LoRA adapters in bf16, double quantization, and paged optimizers — enabling a 65B-class fine-tune on a single large GPU.

When it isn't enough: teaching genuinely new knowledge, a new language or new domain vocabulary; large behavioural shifts; very large datasets where full fine-tuning's extra capacity pays. Then continued pretraining or full FT. Also know the family moved on — DoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA+, VeRA.

SFT vs DPO vs GRPO vs PPO-RLHF, and what's your data strategy with 500 labels?

The 2026 stack: SFT on (prompt, response) pairs for format and instruction-following → preference optimisation (DPO, and its relatives KTO/SimPO/ORPO/IPO) for alignment → RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO, DAPO) for reasoning.

PPO-RLHF needs four models resident — policy, frozen reference, reward model and critic — roughly 280B parameters' worth for a 70B policy; the cost is engineering, not maths. DPO optimises directly on chosen/rejected pairs with an implicit reward: no reward model, no critic, two models instead of four, which is why it's the offline reference point. GRPO samples a group of responses per prompt and computes advantages within the group, so the group mean replaces the value critic — matching or beating PPO with less memory, and with verifiable rewards (a unit test passes, a math answer checks) this is where the 2025–26 reasoning gains came from.

With 500 labels: the LLM-as-teacher loop — prompt a strong model to label unlabelled data, human-verify a stratified sample, fine-tune a small encoder, and evaluate on a human-labelled test set only (never on teacher labels, or you're measuring agreement rather than correctness). Supplement with back-translation and LLM paraphrase (EDA-style synonym swaps are weak), weak supervision via label functions, active learning on uncertain cases, and a Cleanlab-style noise audit — because a 3% label error rate caps your achievable F1 regardless of the model.
